# Concept 24 — Banking Liquidity, Market Liquidity and System-Wide Liquidity

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; current liquidity conditions require dated, source-backed measurement  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 23 — Economic Systems, Institutions and Structural Change](economic-systems-institutions-and-structural-change.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain what liquidity means in different settings, how liquidity is created or withdrawn, why apparently wealthy or solvent actors can still fail to pay on time, and how liquidity pressures may transmit through financial and economic networks.

## 1. The starting question: liquid in what sense?

People describe a business, bank, market or economy as “liquid,” but the word can refer to different conditions. A household may own land and equipment yet lack money for food today. A bank may hold valuable loans but struggle to meet withdrawals. A financial market may have many listed securities but become difficult to trade in size without moving prices sharply.

Before using the term, ask:

1. **Who or what is the subject?** A household, business, bank, investor, market, payment system or whole economy?
2. **Which obligation or transaction must be met?** Payroll, supplier invoices, withdrawals, margin calls, debt maturities, settlement or another payment?
3. **What resource is available, in which currency and at what time?** Cash, deposits, central-bank reserves, credit lines, saleable assets or collateral?
4. **How quickly can that resource be mobilized?** Is it immediately usable, subject to approval, dependent on a sale, or available only after settlement?
5. **At what cost and under what conditions?** Consider fees, interest, haircuts, price impact, collateral requirements and access restrictions.
6. **Is the issue temporary timing or deeper inability to meet obligations?** Liquidity and solvency are related but different.
7. **What evidence establishes the condition?** A balance-sheet snapshot, payment record, funding rate, transaction data, collateral schedule or reported stress?

Liquidity is not a single universal quantity. Its meaning depends on the entity, obligation, resource, currency, time horizon and institutional setting.

## 2. Core meanings of liquidity

### Cash and payment liquidity

The ability to make payments when they fall due using cash, transaction deposits or other immediately usable payment resources.

### Funding liquidity

The ability to obtain cash or financing to meet current and expected obligations. Funding may come from operating receipts, deposits, credit facilities, asset sales, borrowing or other sources. It may become more expensive or unavailable during stress.

### Market liquidity

The ability to buy or sell an asset promptly, in meaningful quantity, at low transaction cost and without causing a large price movement. Market liquidity depends on the asset, trade size, market conditions and time.

### Central-bank and banking-system liquidity

In a banking context, **reserves** are balances that eligible institutions hold at the central bank, together with other qualifying reserve assets under the relevant framework. Reserves support settlement and central-bank operations. They are not the same thing as households' bank deposits or the broad money stock.

### Collateral liquidity

The ability to use an asset to obtain funding or meet a collateral requirement. An asset may be valuable but unsuitable as collateral, ineligible for a facility, difficult to value, or subject to a large haircut.

### System-wide liquidity

A broad description of how readily financial institutions and other participants can make payments, obtain funding, trade assets and mobilize acceptable collateral across the system. It is not necessarily a single stock that can be observed directly. A system can have abundant liquidity in one segment or currency and severe constraints in another.

These meanings overlap, but none should automatically be substituted for another. A market can trade actively while a particular firm faces a cash shortfall. A bank may have substantial reserves while a borrower cannot obtain credit. A valuable asset may not be saleable quickly at its expected price.

## 3. Liquidity, wealth, profitability and solvency

These concepts answer different questions:

- **Liquidity:** Can the relevant payment or obligation be met when due?
- **Wealth or net worth:** What is the value of assets minus liabilities under a stated valuation basis and date?
- **Profitability:** Does the activity generate earnings under the accounting definition and period used?
- **Solvency:** Can the entity meet its obligations over the relevant horizon, considering the value and timing of assets and liabilities under the applicable assessment?
- **Cash flow:** What cash enters and leaves during a specified period?
- **Funding access:** Can the entity obtain additional cash or credit on usable terms?
- **Market liquidity:** Can an asset be traded with limited delay, cost and price impact?

A business can be profitable but illiquid if customers pay late while wages and suppliers must be paid now. It can be liquid temporarily because it has borrowed, while its liabilities still exceed its assets. A business can also have positive net worth but be unable to turn its assets into cash before a critical payment date.

A useful diagnostic is to examine both **amounts** and **timing**. A balance sheet may show assets greater than liabilities, but if the assets mature much later than the obligations, the entity may still experience a payment crisis.

## 4. A simple timing example

Suppose a retailer has ₦1,000,000 in assets, including ₦700,000 of inventory, ₦200,000 owed by customers and ₦100,000 in cash. It must pay a supplier ₦250,000 tomorrow. These hypothetical figures are illustrative.

The retailer may have assets with a total stated value of ₦1,000,000, but only ₦100,000 is immediately available as cash. Inventory may take days or weeks to sell, and customer receivables may not be collected before the payment deadline. The retailer therefore faces a near-term cash gap even though its total assets appear larger than the bill.

The question is not simply “Does it own enough?” It is “Which resources can be converted into usable payment funds by the due date, at what cost, and with what certainty?”

This example does not establish that inventory or receivables are worthless. It shows that timing, convertibility and uncertainty matter.

## 5. The cash-conversion cycle and operating liquidity

A business often pays for inputs before it receives cash from customers. The interval between cash outflows and the eventual collection of sales receipts can create a financing need.

A simplified operating sequence is:

**Pay for inputs → hold or process inventory → sell to customer → wait for collection → receive cash.**

In ordinary language, the business may have to fund purchasing, transport, wages and storage before a sale becomes cash in its account. The longer the time between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, the more working capital it may need, all else equal. Supplier credit, customer deposits, faster inventory turnover or quicker collections can reduce the funding gap, but each option has costs and constraints.

The **cash-conversion cycle** is a working-capital measure commonly constructed from inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days. Exact definitions and calculations vary with accounting data and conventions. It should not be interpreted in isolation: a longer cycle may be normal in one industry, and averages can hide seasonal peaks or individual payment bottlenecks.

A firm can improve reported liquidity in the short term by delaying suppliers, but this may transfer the cash pressure to other firms and damage future supply relationships. One participant's apparent buffer can be another participant's overdue receivable.

## 6. Bank liquidity: assets, liabilities and timing

Banks hold assets that produce cash at different times and owe liabilities that may be payable on different schedules. Loans often repay over time; deposits may be withdrawable sooner; wholesale funding may mature on a fixed date. Banks therefore manage liquidity as well as credit risk and capital adequacy.

A bank's liquidity position depends on matters such as:

- expected deposit inflows and withdrawals;
- scheduled loan repayments and new lending;
- access to secured or unsecured funding;
- marketability and eligibility of liquid assets;
- collateral available for central-bank or market facilities;
- payment and settlement obligations;
- currency and maturity mismatches;
- confidence, information and depositor behavior;
- legal, regulatory and operational restrictions.

A bank holding assets that are sound over the long term may still face a near-term funding problem if it cannot mobilize cash quickly enough. Conversely, a short-term liquidity problem does not automatically establish that its assets are worth less than its liabilities. Both liquidity and solvency need separate assessment.

A stylized mechanism is:

**Unexpected withdrawals → cash needs rise → liquid assets or funding are used → remaining buffers shrink → additional funding may be needed.**

In ordinary language, withdrawals require the bank to deliver payment resources. The bank may use cash and reserves, obtain funding or sell assets. If the pressure continues, its remaining buffers can become less comfortable. The sequence describes a possible mechanism, not a claim that every withdrawal wave becomes a crisis.

## 7. Central-bank reserves, deposits and money

These are connected but distinct:

- **Currency:** physical notes and coins issued under the relevant monetary framework.
- **Commercial-bank deposit:** a liability of the bank to its customer, recorded in the customer's account.
- **Central-bank reserve balance:** a claim of an eligible institution on the central bank, used within the applicable settlement and policy framework.
- **Broad money:** a statistical aggregate defined by the relevant authority, often including currency held by the public and specified deposits or other instruments.
- **Cash held by a business or household:** physical currency in its possession; it is not the same as a bank reserve balance.
- **Liquid asset:** an asset that can be used or converted into payment resources within the relevant time and cost limits.

A transfer between two customers at the same bank may change the ownership of deposits without requiring a transfer between banks' reserve accounts for that individual payment. Payments between banks generally require interbank settlement arrangements. Exact operations depend on the payment system and settlement framework.

Commercial banks, central banks, payment systems and non-bank participants have different roles. Do not infer that an increase in central-bank reserves automatically causes an equal increase in lending, broad money, spending or productive investment. Transmission depends on banks' balance sheets, borrower demand, risk assessments, regulation, monetary policy, market conditions and other constraints.

## 8. Market liquidity: trading without excessive price impact

Market liquidity is not just the existence of buyers and sellers. It concerns how easily trades can be executed at the desired size, speed and price.

Useful dimensions include:

- **Tightness:** the cost of trading, often reflected in bid–ask spreads.
- **Depth:** the amount available to buy or sell near current prices.
- **Immediacy:** how quickly a trade can be completed.
- **Resilience:** how quickly prices and available depth recover after a trade or shock.
- **Price impact:** how much the market price moves as a result of executing a trade.
- **Market breadth and participation:** the range and diversity of participants and available trading interest.

An asset with a quoted price may not be sellable in large quantity at that price. A last-traded price, midpoint or indicative quote is not proof that a participant can execute at that price for the required size.

A market may become less liquid if dealers reduce risk-taking, funding becomes expensive, uncertainty rises, participants withdraw, or trading infrastructure fails. These mechanisms can reinforce one another, but the causal path must be established with market-specific evidence.

## 9. Funding liquidity and market liquidity can reinforce one another

An institution may need cash and decide to sell assets. If many participants try to sell similar assets simultaneously, prices may fall and trading depth may deteriorate. Lower prices can reduce the value of collateral, leading lenders to demand more collateral or reduce funding. That can prompt further sales.

A possible feedback loop is:

**Funding pressure → asset sales → lower prices or greater price impact → weaker collateral values → tighter funding → further pressure.**

In plain language, an institution short of cash may sell assets to raise it. If many institutions sell at once, they may receive less than expected. Lower asset values can then make lenders more cautious or trigger collateral calls, worsening the original cash problem. This is a possible amplification mechanism, not an automatic result: central-bank facilities, private buyers, capital buffers, market structure and other factors may interrupt the loop.

The concept of a **fire sale** generally refers to a pressured sale at a substantial discount relative to an appropriate benchmark or fundamental value, but the benchmark and evidence must be stated. A price decline alone does not prove a fire sale.

## 10. Collateral, haircuts, margin and secured funding

Collateral supports a funding or performance obligation. The lender or counterparty may apply a **haircut**, valuing collateral below its current market value to account for price changes, liquidity, legal enforceability and liquidation costs.

For example, if eligible collateral has a market value of ₦10 million and a hypothetical 20% haircut applies, the amount recognized for lending purposes would be ₦8 million, assuming the facility uses that simple convention. The haircut is not necessarily a fee; it reduces the value credited against the exposure.

A **margin call** can require a participant to add collateral or cash when exposure, prices or contractual requirements change. The call may create immediate funding demand even when the participant expects the underlying position to be profitable over a longer horizon.

Collateral value and collateral liquidity are not the same. A valuable asset can be difficult to sell, legally encumbered, ineligible for a facility, concentrated in a stressed market or subject to uncertain valuation. A collateral schedule must identify ownership, existing encumbrances, eligibility, valuation date, haircut, currency and the entity entitled to enforce it.

## 11. Liquidity shortages, funding stress and insolvency

These terms should not be collapsed:

- **Liquidity shortage:** available payment resources are insufficient for a specified need, at a specified time and boundary.
- **Funding stress:** obtaining or rolling over funds becomes difficult or expensive.
- **Insolvency:** the entity cannot meet the applicable solvency test; legal definitions differ by jurisdiction and context.
- **Default:** a specified contractual or legal obligation is not performed as required.
- **Arrears:** amounts remain unpaid after their due date under the relevant definition.
- **Delinquency:** payment or performance is overdue according to the applicable contract or classification.
- **Bank run:** rapid withdrawals or funding withdrawal create acute pressure on a bank; the term should be applied using evidence of the behavior and context.
- **Deleveraging:** reducing leverage or balance-sheet exposure, potentially through repayment, asset sales, reduced lending or raising capital.

An illiquid but solvent entity may recover if it can obtain bridging finance or wait for assets to mature. An insolvent entity may temporarily continue making payments using new borrowing or asset sales. A single missed payment does not automatically establish insolvency, and a solvent balance-sheet estimate does not guarantee that cash will be available at the right moment.

Assessment requires a time horizon, legal/accounting boundary, valuation assumptions, expected cash flows, obligations and access to funding.

## 12. How liquidity moves through a network

Liquidity problems can cross counterparties through payment and funding relationships. For example, if one large buyer pays suppliers late, those suppliers may struggle to meet payroll or pay their own vendors. A supplier may then delay another payment. Such a chain can propagate cash pressure even if the original issue began with one participant.

A stylized chain is:

**Buyer delays payment → supplier's receivable remains uncollected → supplier's usable cash falls → supplier delays its own obligations → other counterparties face pressure.**

In ordinary language, money that one business expected to receive is not available when needed. The supplier may respond by using reserves, borrowing, selling assets or delaying payments to others. The original delay can therefore affect other firms. Whether the pressure spreads widely depends on buffers, alternatives, timing, concentration, credit access and whether the delayed payment is eventually made.

Do not treat every late payment as systemic risk. Establish the size of the exposure, the network position of the parties, substitutes, timing, buffers and actual downstream effects.

## 13. Liquidity indicators and their limitations

Potential measures include:

| Measure | What it can help describe | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Cash balance | Immediately held cash at a stated time | Excludes other usable resources and future obligations |
| Current ratio | Current assets relative to current liabilities under accounting classifications | Asset quality and timing can differ greatly |
| Quick ratio | A narrower comparison of liquid/current assets to current liabilities, depending on definition | Receivables may not be collected on time; definitions vary |
| Operating cash flow | Net cash from operating activities over a period | Historical flows do not guarantee future payment capacity |
| Cash-conversion cycle | Timing relationship among inventory, receivables and payables | Averages can conceal seasonal or counterparty-specific stress |
| Bid–ask spread | One dimension of transaction cost | Does not fully capture depth, size or market resilience |
| Market depth | Available orders near quoted prices | Displayed orders may change or be cancelled |
| Turnover | Trading activity relative to a stated base | High turnover does not alone prove low price impact or resilience |
| Funding spread | Relative cost of funding under a stated benchmark | Can reflect credit risk, term, currency, collateral and market conditions |
| Liquid-asset buffer | Assets judged available for specified liquidity needs | Eligibility, encumbrance and stress haircuts matter |
| Liquidity coverage measure | Capacity to cover defined stressed outflows under a specific regulatory or analytical framework | The rule, horizon, eligible assets and assumptions must be identified |
| Maturity gap | Differences between cash inflows and outflows across time buckets | Depends on assumptions about behavioral maturity and contingent flows |

No ratio should be interpreted without the entity boundary, date, currency, maturity horizon, definition and source. Cross-entity comparisons require consistent accounting and regulatory definitions. A measure can be useful as an indicator without being a complete description of liquidity.

## 14. Worked example: an otherwise viable business faces a cash squeeze

Imagine a distributor that sells goods to shops on 30-day credit but must pay its suppliers within seven days. Sales are growing, and its accounting statements show a positive margin. However, some customers pay late, and the distributor cannot obtain an affordable credit line.

A weak conclusion would be “the business is profitable, so it has no financial problem.” A better analysis separates profitability, working capital, timing and financing.

Questions to investigate:

- What are the actual contractual payment terms?
- What share of invoices is collected on time?
- How much cash is available today?
- Which inventory can be sold quickly, and at what likely discount?
- What supplier credit is available, and what happens if payments are late?
- What are the costs and conditions of short-term finance?
- Are late collections temporary, seasonal or persistent?
- Would changing customer terms reduce sales or improve liquidity?
- Are there concentration risks if one large customer pays late?

The distributor may have a viable business model but an unsustainable cash-conversion pattern. Alternatively, weak margins, bad debts or poor demand may be the deeper problem. The evidence must distinguish those possibilities. The example is hypothetical and makes no claim about a particular company or sector.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Liquidity vs wealth | Liquidity concerns timely access to payment resources; wealth concerns net asset value under a stated basis. |
| Liquidity vs profitability | Ability to meet payments differs from generating earnings. |
| Liquidity vs solvency | A near-term funding gap differs from an inability to meet obligations under the relevant solvency test. |
| Funding liquidity vs market liquidity | Ability to obtain cash differs from ability to trade assets without excessive cost or price impact. |
| Cash vs bank deposit | Physical currency differs from a claim on a bank. |
| Bank deposit vs central-bank reserve | Customer deposits are bank liabilities; reserve balances are claims held by eligible institutions at the central bank. |
| Broad money vs reserves | Statistical money aggregates differ from central-bank settlement balances. |
| Asset value vs collateral value | Market valuation differs from the amount a lender recognizes after eligibility rules and haircuts. |
| Market price vs executable price | A quote or last trade does not guarantee execution for a given size. |
| Turnover vs liquidity | Trading volume alone does not establish depth, tightness or resilience. |
| Liquidity support vs solvency support | A facility may bridge a timing gap; absorbing losses or restoring net worth is a different intervention. |
| Temporary illiquidity vs insolvency | One is a timing/funding condition; the other concerns ability to meet obligations under the applicable test. |
| Payment delay vs default | Consequences depend on contractual terms, grace periods and legal definitions. |
| Liquid assets vs unencumbered liquid assets | Assets pledged or legally restricted may not be available for other obligations. |
| Liquidity buffer vs guaranteed protection | A buffer can reduce vulnerability but may be inadequate under severe or correlated stress. |
| Liquidity creation vs productive investment | Financial liquidity can facilitate transactions but does not automatically create productive capacity. |
| Liquidity indicator vs diagnosis | A ratio or spread signals a dimension of conditions; it does not alone establish the cause or full severity. |

## 17. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions

- Liquidity is context-specific and depends on resource availability, timing, cost and access.
- Funding liquidity and market liquidity are different, though they can reinforce one another.
- Liquidity, profitability, wealth and solvency answer different questions.
- Bank deposits and central-bank reserves are distinct balance-sheet items.
- Collateral valuation, eligibility, encumbrance and haircuts affect usable funding capacity.
- Liquidity pressure can propagate through payments and financial networks, but propagation depends on buffers, exposures, alternatives and timing.
- An indicator can reveal one dimension of liquidity without proving its cause or providing a complete diagnosis.

### Scope limits

- This note does not assess the current liquidity of any bank, market, business, country or currency.
- No current Nigerian liquidity figures, stress classifications or crisis predictions are asserted.
- Regulatory ratios and central-bank facilities differ across jurisdictions and dates; consult the applicable primary rules before applying them.
- Legal insolvency tests, payment-system arrangements and collateral enforceability require jurisdiction-specific verification.
- Examples and mechanisms are conceptual, not evidence that a specific chain of events has occurred.

### Questions to revisit

- Which indicators best capture payment liquidity for Nigerian SMEs with irregular collections and supplier-credit constraints?
- How can reported liquid assets be reconciled with encumbrances, currency mismatch and stress haircuts?
- Which public datasets provide sufficiently frequent, comparable information on banking-system liquidity and funding conditions?
- How should market liquidity be measured where trading is fragmented or reported transaction data are incomplete?
- What evidence distinguishes a local liquidity problem from a systemically important network transmission?
- How should liquidity facilities be evaluated separately from recapitalization or loss absorption?
- Which liquidity measures are comparable across countries, institutions and market segments?

## 18. Short summary

Liquidity concerns whether payment resources or assets can be mobilized when needed, at acceptable cost and under the relevant constraints. Cash liquidity, funding liquidity, market liquidity, collateral liquidity and system-wide liquidity describe related but distinct conditions. An entity may be profitable or have positive net worth yet fail to meet a payment on time because its assets and receipts are not available when obligations fall due. Liquidity pressure can spread through payment and funding networks, and market sales can sometimes amplify it, but these are mechanisms to test rather than outcomes to assume. Lifeconomist should define the subject, obligation, resource, time horizon and evidence before declaring an entity or system liquid or illiquid.

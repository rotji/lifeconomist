## 1. Why measurement and inference need their own concept

Economic systems cannot be investigated reliably by collecting numbers alone. Before a number can support a conclusion, the analyst needs to know what the number represents, how it was produced, which people or activities it covers, which time period and unit it refers to, how it may be wrong, and what claim the evidence can actually support.

The central discipline is to separate five things:

1. **Reality:** The condition, event, relationship or process that exists in the world.
2. **Observation:** A trace or record obtained from reality, such as a survey response, price quote, administrative record, transaction or physical measurement.
3. **Measurement:** A rule-governed process that maps observations to a defined concept, variable, quantity or indicator.
4. **Inference:** Reasoning from observed evidence to a conclusion about a condition, relationship, population, mechanism or cause.
5. **Decision:** An action taken using the conclusion, alongside objectives, constraints, costs, values and risk tolerance.

A useful working chain is:

**Economic reality → observation → measurement and validation → inference → decision → observed consequences and feedback.**

In plain language, the world does not arrive as a ready-made spreadsheet. We observe selected traces, interpret them using definitions and methods, and then make claims from them. Each step can introduce error. A decision can also change the world being measured, so the investigation should return to new observations rather than treating the first conclusion as final.

This chain is a discipline, not a claim that all analysis is linear. Measurement may reveal that a concept is poorly defined; a failed inference may show that a source is unsuitable; and a decision may change incentives and behaviour, requiring the analyst to revise the model.

## 2. Concepts, variables, measures, indicators, proxies and indices

These terms often get used as if they were synonyms. They are related but do different jobs.

- **Concept:** An idea or construct we want to understand, such as household welfare, market liquidity, productive capacity, institutional effectiveness or economic resilience.
- **Construct:** A concept that cannot be observed directly in full and must be represented through theory, indicators or observable consequences.
- **Variable:** A characteristic that can take different values across people, firms, places, events or time periods.
- **Observation:** A recorded value for a defined unit and time or event.
- **Measure:** A value produced under a specified measurement rule. In some fields “measure” is used broadly; the method should clarify its meaning.
- **Indicator:** A measure selected to signal a condition, trend, process or outcome relevant to a question.
- **Proxy:** An observable variable used as an imperfect stand-in for something harder to observe directly.
- **Index:** A composite or normalized measure that combines several values according to defined rules.
- **Metric:** A quantified measure used for description, comparison, monitoring or evaluation; it is not automatically a valid indicator.
- **Estimate:** A value inferred from data and a method, often for a population quantity or model parameter that is not directly observed.
- **Forecast:** A statement about a future outcome under a specified method and information set.
- **Target:** A desired or chosen value, often for planning or performance management. A target is not a measurement of what currently exists.

### Why the distinctions matter

Suppose an analyst wants to understand whether a local business ecosystem is becoming more resilient. The concept is resilience. A variable might be the number of days a business can operate during a supply interruption. A measure specifies how those days are calculated. An indicator could be the median number of days of disruption experienced by a defined sample. A proxy might be the number of alternative suppliers, if direct disruption performance is unavailable. An index might combine supplier concentration, inventory coverage, power reliability and access to finance. A target could be to reduce average downtime.

Those objects are not interchangeable. More alternative suppliers may be associated with resilience, but it is not resilience itself. A composite index may be convenient, but its result depends on its components, scaling and weights. A target expresses an intention, not an observed outcome.

**Concept → operational definition → variable and unit → data → measure/indicator → interpretation.**

In plain language, the analyst must translate a broad idea into something observable without pretending that the observable quantity fully captures the idea. The operational definition should explain what is included, excluded and counted.

## 3. Operational definitions: decide exactly what is being measured

An operational definition specifies how a concept will be identified or calculated for a particular investigation. It should be detailed enough that another analyst could apply it and understand why the resulting values are comparable.

A measurement specification normally records:

- **Purpose:** Which question or decision the measure is intended to support.
- **Concept:** The underlying idea or condition of interest.
- **Definition:** What counts and what does not count.
- **Unit of analysis:** Person, household, firm, product, transaction, institution, location, event, sector or country.
- **Unit of observation:** The entity or record from which the value is collected; this can differ from the unit of analysis.
- **Numerator and denominator:** For rates and ratios, what is counted in each part and why.
- **Unit and scale:** Naira, people, tonnes, days, percentage, index points or another defined unit.
- **Population and coverage:** Which units are included, excluded or inaccessible.
- **Geography and period:** The spatial boundary and time reference.
- **Source and collection method:** Survey, administrative records, sensor, market quote, model, field observation or another source.
- **Frequency and timing:** How often it is observed, and whether it refers to a point in time, interval or event.
- **Transformations:** Cleaning, adjustments, seasonal treatment, deflation, aggregation, weighting or imputation.
- **Quality and uncertainty:** Known limitations, error risks, missingness and confidence or uncertainty representation.
- **Revision policy:** Whether historical values can change and how versions are preserved.
- **Owner and responsibility:** Who produces, checks, maintains and approves the measure.
- **Interpretation limits:** What conclusions the measure does not justify.

The level of specification depends on the intended use. A quick exploratory comparison can use a provisional definition, but the provisional status should be explicit. A core indicator used to declare an economic state, compare countries, allocate funds or trigger an intervention requires stronger controls.

## 4. Units, denominators, stocks, flows, rates and ratios

Numbers can look comparable while measuring different things. The unit and denominator often determine what a statistic means.

- **Count:** Number of defined items or events within a specified scope.
- **Amount or level:** A quantity such as naira, tonnes, people or units of inventory.
- **Stock:** A quantity measured at a point in time, such as outstanding debt on a stated date.
- **Flow:** A quantity accumulated over an interval, such as new borrowing during a month.
- **Rate:** A quantity per unit of exposure or time, or a change relative to a reference, depending on the definition.
- **Ratio:** One quantity divided by another. The two parts must be defined and meaningfully related.
- **Share:** A component divided by a specified total.
- **Growth rate:** Change relative to a defined starting value over a stated period, with treatment of zero or negative bases clarified.
- **Index:** A measure expressed relative to a reference or constructed from multiple components.
- **Per-capita measure:** A quantity divided by a population measure, with population definition and timing specified.
- **Average:** A summary statistic such as mean or median; it does not describe every unit.
- **Cumulative measure:** An accumulation over a stated interval or since a defined starting point.

A stock should not be compared directly with a flow without explaining the time relationship. A debt stock at year-end and new borrowing over the year answer different questions. A national total may rise while the per-capita value falls if population grows faster. A sector's share can decline even while its real output rises if the rest of the economy grows faster.

**Defined quantity + consistent unit + correct denominator + aligned time basis → interpretable comparison.**

In plain language, a number needs its measurement frame. “Five billion” is incomplete without saying five billion of what, in which currency, for which units, at what date or over what period. “A 10% increase” also needs a baseline, interval and definition of the measured quantity.

## 5. Nominal and real values; gross and net; levels and changes

Several measurement distinctions recur across economic analysis.

### Nominal versus real

- **Nominal value:** Expressed in prices prevailing in the relevant period.
- **Real value:** Adjusted using a specified price index or deflator to estimate changes in quantity or purchasing power under a defined method.

Nominal revenue can rise because more goods were sold, prices rose, product quality changed, or the product mix shifted. A real adjustment helps separate price change from other changes, but the result depends on the deflator and the question. A general consumer price index may not be the correct deflator for every output or asset.

### Gross versus net

Gross values are measured before a specified deduction; net values are measured after it. The deduction must be named. Gross output, value added, gross capital formation and gross income are not interchangeable. “Net” can mean net of depreciation, taxes, transfers, intermediate inputs or another component depending on context.

### Level versus change

A level describes a quantity at a time or over a period. A change compares two defined values. A growth rate is a particular relative change, not the same as the absolute difference. High growth from a small base may still leave a quantity low; a decline from an unusually high level may leave it above a longer-run norm.

### Per-capita versus aggregate

Per-capita values divide an aggregate by a population measure. They can help compare differently sized populations but conceal distribution and differences in cost of living, household composition, regional access or within-country inequality.

### Seasonally adjusted versus unadjusted

Seasonal adjustment attempts to separate recurring calendar patterns from other movement. It depends on a statistical method and may be revised when new observations arrive. Unadjusted and adjusted values answer different questions and should not be mixed without disclosure.

The analyst should name the adjustment, source, base period, reference population and limitations rather than calling a value simply “real,” “net” or “adjusted.”

## 6. Measurement error, missingness and revisions

Observed data can differ from the intended quantity for several reasons.

- **Sampling error:** A sample differs from the population by chance under the sampling design.
- **Coverage error:** Parts of the target population are excluded or overrepresented.
- **Nonresponse error:** Selected units do not respond, and response patterns may be systematically related to the topic.
- **Measurement error:** The recorded value differs from the intended value because of question wording, instrument limits, recall, incentives, classification or recording.
- **Processing error:** Mistakes arise during entry, coding, cleaning, linking, aggregation or transformation.
- **Model error:** A model represents the process poorly or uses unsuitable assumptions.
- **Selection bias:** Observed cases differ systematically from the cases needed for the intended conclusion.
- **Survivorship bias:** Analysis includes units that remain observable or successful while missing those that exited, failed or disappeared.
- **Publication or reporting bias:** Some results or events are more likely to be reported than others.
- **Revision:** Previously published values change after new information, improved methods, corrections or reclassification.

Missing values are not necessarily zero. A blank record can mean not collected, unknown, not applicable, suppressed, delayed, failed to link or truly absent. These states should be distinguished where possible.

Missingness can be especially damaging when it is systematic. If informal businesses are less likely to appear in administrative records, an administrative dataset may systematically understate their activity rather than merely contain random gaps. If failed firms disappear from a dataset, an analysis of surviving firms can overstate typical performance.

### A revision is not automatically evidence of manipulation

A revised value may reflect routine improvement, late reporting, seasonal-factor updates, benchmark changes or a methodological break. It can also reflect a correction or error. Investigate the stated reason, size, direction, historical pattern and transparency of revisions before drawing a conclusion.

Preserve both the latest value and, when important to the decision, the value known at the time of the original decision. This distinction matters for historical evaluation and for testing whether a monitoring system would have worked in real time.

## 7. Comparability: same label does not guarantee same meaning

Two values can be compared only when the relevant definitions, scope, unit, time basis and methods are sufficiently aligned.

Comparability risks include:

- Different population coverage or sampling frames.
- Different definitions of employment, unemployment, output or business activity.
- Different accounting boundaries or classifications.
- Different currency conversion methods or price bases.
- Different reference periods or reporting calendars.
- Different treatment of informal, unregistered or unpaid activity.
- Changes in data collection, survey design, instruments or administrative systems.
- Reclassification of sectors, products, regions or institutional units.
- Different methods for seasonal adjustment, imputation or deflation.
- Breaks in time series after rebasing or methodology changes.

A country may report a statistical series under the same name before and after a rebasing exercise, but historical values can change because coverage, source data, weights or methods changed. A break in the series should be documented and not mistaken for an economic shock.

When direct comparability is not available, options include rebuilding values using common definitions, using an official bridge series, presenting the periods separately, adding a sensitivity range, or stating that the comparison is not currently supportable.

## 8. Descriptive evidence, association, prediction and causation

These are different levels of claim.

- **Description:** What was observed in a defined dataset or population?
- **Comparison:** How do defined groups, places or periods differ?
- **Association:** Do variables tend to vary together?
- **Prediction:** How well does information available at a stated time predict an outcome?
- **Mechanism:** What process could connect one condition to another?
- **Causal inference:** What would have happened to the outcome under a different exposure, intervention or condition, all else specified by the causal question?
- **Explanation:** A supported account combining evidence, mechanism and relevant alternatives. The strength of explanation depends on the evidence and method.
- **Recommendation:** What action is preferable given objectives, costs, constraints, uncertainty and consequences.

A relationship can be real without being causal. Two variables may move together because one affects the other, because both are influenced by a third factor, because the relationship runs in the opposite direction, because the sample was selected in a particular way, or because of coincidence.

**Observed association → candidate explanations → alternative causes and confounders → appropriate design and tests → calibrated conclusion.**

In plain language, seeing two things move together is a reason to investigate, not a licence to say one caused the other. The analyst must consider other explanations, determine what evidence would distinguish them, and use a method suited to the causal question. The final conclusion should be no stronger than the design allows.

### Mechanism is not proof

A plausible mechanism helps explain how an effect could occur. For example, higher borrowing costs may reduce some investment by increasing financing costs. But the actual response depends on credit availability, expected demand, cash reserves, subsidies, market structure, exchange rates and other conditions. A plausible mechanism identifies what to examine; it does not establish the magnitude or even the direction of the net effect in every case.

### Confounding and reverse causality

A confounder influences both the explanatory variable and the outcome, making their observed relationship difficult to interpret. Reverse causality occurs when the outcome also influences the supposed cause. For example, weak economic activity may lead to policy easing, so an observed association between easing and weak activity cannot alone show that easing caused the weakness.

Causal claims may require randomized experiments, natural experiments, credible comparison groups, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, process tracing or other designs. Each method relies on assumptions that need to be stated and assessed. No single method is automatically credible merely because it has a technical name.

## 9. Evidence hierarchy, triangulation and source independence

Evidence should be evaluated in relation to the claim. There is no universal ranking in which one source type is always best.

Possible evidence includes:

- Direct observations of an event or physical condition.
- Administrative records created for operational or regulatory purposes.
- Surveys with documented sampling, wording and response processes.
- Official statistics with methods and revision policies.
- Audited financial statements or regulated disclosures.
- Transaction records, invoices, contracts and payment traces.
- Satellite imagery, sensors, geospatial data or remote observations.
- Research studies with transparent methods and appropriate designs.
- Expert testimony, interviews and informed local observations.
- Media reports, public statements and secondary summaries.
- Models, forecasts and simulations.
- Anecdotes, screenshots and unverified claims.

Each source has a different failure profile. Administrative records may be detailed but omit unregistered activity. Audited statements provide assurance within a defined scope but do not prove every business claim. Satellite imagery can show a physical change but may not establish ownership, purpose or transaction value. Interviews can reveal mechanisms and context but may not estimate prevalence. Official statistics can be the best available measure while still carrying sampling error or revisions.

### Triangulation is more than counting sources

Triangulation means comparing evidence that can test the same claim from different angles. It is strongest when the sources have meaningfully different error mechanisms.

**Claim → source A + source B + source C → assess agreement, dependence and contradiction → calibrated assessment.**

In plain language, three websites repeating one press release do not amount to three independent confirmations. A shipment record, an independently captured port observation and a buyer's receipt may provide more distinct evidence about a delivery than several articles repeating the same announcement. Even independent sources may agree for the wrong reason, so the analyst must examine what each source actually establishes.

Record source lineage, dates, directness, independence, coverage, incentives, contradictions and any dependence on a common original source.

## 10. Uncertainty: what is unknown, how much is unknown, and why

Uncertainty is not one single quantity. At minimum, distinguish:

- **Natural or process variability:** Real variation in the system, such as changing demand or weather.
- **Sampling uncertainty:** Uncertainty arising because only part of a population was observed.
- **Measurement uncertainty:** Uncertainty about the measured value due to instruments, recording or definitions.
- **Model uncertainty:** Uncertainty about whether the chosen model and assumptions represent the process adequately.
- **Parameter uncertainty:** Uncertainty about numerical values within a model.
- **Scenario uncertainty:** Uncertainty about which future conditions or pathways will occur.
- **Data uncertainty:** Uncertainty due to gaps, source limitations, stale records or uncertain provenance.
- **Interpretive uncertainty:** More than one plausible explanation fits the available evidence.
- **Deep uncertainty:** Relevant parties do not agree on, or cannot reliably specify, the model, probabilities or future pathways.

A probability, confidence interval, score or qualitative label should be tied to a clear interpretation. A model output of 70% is meaningful as a probability only if the model and calibration justify that reading. A “high confidence” label should have a documented rubric or explanation, not merely express the analyst's feeling.

### Confidence in a claim versus quality of the underlying data

Good data do not guarantee a strong conclusion if the causal design is weak. Conversely, imperfect data can still support a useful conclusion if the limitation is understood and the claim is narrow enough.

Keep separate assessments of:
1. Source reliability and provenance.
2. Measurement validity and coverage.
3. Method suitability.
4. Independence and consistency of evidence.
5. Sensitivity to assumptions.
6. Strength of the final inference.

Do not collapse these into a single confidence number unless the scoring model is documented, tested and appropriate for the intended use.

## 11. Models, indicators and composite scores

A model simplifies reality to answer a question. That simplification can be useful, but its outputs depend on the chosen variables, functional form, training data, assumptions and validation process.

Common risks include:

- **Overfitting:** A model captures quirks of historical data that do not generalize.
- **Underfitting:** A model is too simple to represent important structure.
- **Data leakage:** Information unavailable at the intended decision time enters training or evaluation.
- **Concept drift:** Relationships or data patterns change over time.
- **Distribution shift:** The environment in deployment differs from the environment used for training or calibration.
- **Specification error:** Important variables, relationships or constraints are omitted or misrepresented.
- **Proxy failure:** A convenient variable stops representing the intended concept.
- **Threshold instability:** Small changes in data or assumptions alter classification.
- **Aggregation bias:** A group-level pattern is wrongly applied to individuals, or subgroup differences are hidden.
- **Feedback effects:** Decisions based on a model change the data later used to assess it.

Composite indicators introduce additional choices: component selection, normalization, weighting, aggregation, missing-value treatment and directionality. Two reasonable methods can produce different rankings. A composite score should be accompanied by its components and sensitivity to choices, especially if used for high-consequence decisions.

A score can be operationally useful without being a direct measurement of a single underlying reality. It should be named and interpreted accordingly.

## 12. Leading, coincident and lagging indicators

Indicators can be classified by their relationship to a condition or event of interest:

- **Leading indicator:** May move before the target outcome.
- **Coincident indicator:** Tends to move around the same time as the target condition.
- **Lagging indicator:** Reflects outcomes or adjustments that occur after the underlying change.

These labels are empirical and context-dependent. A variable that leads one business cycle or market may not lead another. Publication delays can make a coincident economic variable arrive too late to function as a useful early-warning signal. A leading indicator can also produce false alarms.

Evaluate indicators on at least four dimensions:

1. **Lead time:** How far before the target event does the signal become informative?
2. **Reliability:** How often does the signal correctly identify the relevant condition?
3. **False positives and false negatives:** How often does it signal a problem that does not occur, or miss one that does?
4. **Decision usefulness:** Does the warning arrive early enough and with enough specificity for a feasible response?

An early warning system should preserve the data that were available at the time, not merely test signals against revised historical series. Otherwise it may look better retrospectively than it would have performed in real time.

## 13. Aggregation, distributions and ecological fallacies

Aggregates summarize but can hide important variation. A national average can improve while some regions deteriorate. A sector's output can rise while the number of firms falls. An average price can remain stable while the price of an essential item rises sharply. An increase in total employment can coexist with declining hours, real wages or job quality for some groups.

Important distinctions include:

- Mean versus median.
- Total versus per-capita.
- Aggregate versus distribution.
- Within-group versus between-group variation.
- Individual-level versus group-level relationships.
- National versus regional or sectoral conditions.
- Weighted versus unweighted averages.
- Composition changes versus changes within each group.

The **ecological fallacy** occurs when a group-level relationship is assumed to hold for individuals. The related **atomistic fallacy** occurs when an individual-level relationship is assumed to describe group-level outcomes. Both can mislead when composition, context or institutional arrangements matter.

When a result changes, decompose the change where possible. Did each unit change, or did the composition of units change? Did the total increase because activity grew, prices changed, coverage expanded or the classification changed? The correct answer depends on the measurement design and evidence.

## 14. Practical investigation example: a reported increase in business failures

Suppose a news report says business failures have risen sharply. The claim may be important, but it is not yet a verified measurement.

A disciplined investigation would ask:

1. What does “business failure” mean: insolvency, closure, deregistration, missed payments, court filing, inactivity or a survey response?
2. What is the numerator, and what is the denominator? Are we counting events, unique firms, or a rate per active firm?
3. Which firms are covered: registered companies, all operating businesses, sampled SMEs or only court cases?
4. What period is being compared, and are the periods equally long?
5. Did a new registry, reporting rule, enforcement drive or data source change coverage?
6. Is the source counting new failures, cumulative cases or delayed registrations?
7. Are there independent sources, such as insolvency records, tax records, field observations or surveys? What do they each capture?
8. Could a common shock, sector mix, firm age or other factor explain the pattern?
9. Are values provisional and subject to revision?
10. What evidence would distinguish a real deterioration from improved reporting or reclassification?

The analyst might conclude that recorded insolvency filings rose, while the rate of failure among all active businesses remains unknown because the population denominator is unavailable. That is a useful, bounded conclusion. It is more defensible than stating that “business failures rose across the economy” without qualification.

This example is illustrative. It does not assert that business failures have risen in any specific country or period.

## 15. From evidence to decisions: thresholds, loss and proportionality

Evidence rarely removes all uncertainty. A decision still requires a threshold for action, chosen in light of the consequences of being wrong.

A warning system for a low-cost reversible action can reasonably use a different threshold from a system that triggers a large financial commitment, public accusation or irreversible intervention. The relevant factors include:

- The probability and severity of the potential harm.
- The cost of a false alarm.
- The cost of failing to detect a real event.
- Whether the action is reversible.
- How quickly more evidence can be obtained.
- The availability of safer or lower-cost alternatives.
- The distribution of benefits and burdens.
- The cost of delay.

**Evidence and uncertainty → decision threshold → action proportionate to potential consequences → monitor results and revise.**

In plain language, an uncertain signal may justify checking a source or conducting a small test, but not necessarily making a large irreversible commitment. A high-consequence decision may require stronger evidence, independent review or staged action. The appropriate threshold depends on the loss from different errors, not just on whether a signal crosses a convenient numerical cutoff.

The analyst should separate an evidence assessment from a policy or business recommendation. A recommendation adds objectives, trade-offs, risk tolerance and value judgements to the evidence; it is not a purely statistical output.

## 16. A reusable evidence record for Lifeconomist

For each important claim or indicator, record enough information to reproduce the reasoning and update the conclusion.

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Claim or question | Exact proposition being assessed |
| Claim type | Description, comparison, association, prediction, mechanism or causal claim |
| Concept and definition | Meaning, inclusions and exclusions |
| Unit and scope | Unit of analysis, population, geography and period |
| Source | Publisher, source identifier, retrieval location and access conditions |
| Time metadata | Event date, observation date, publication date, retrieval date and update frequency where available |
| Data status | Preliminary, revised, estimated, modelled, reported, independently verified or unknown |
| Method | Collection, calculation, transformation, model or comparison design |
| Provenance | Source lineage and transformations |
| Coverage and missingness | What is observed, omitted, inaccessible or unknown |
| Quality risks | Error, bias, revision, incentives, dependence and comparability concerns |
| Alternatives | Plausible competing explanations or contradictory evidence |
| Uncertainty | Quantitative interval, scenario range or explicit qualitative limitation |
| Confidence rationale | Why the claim is supported to the stated degree |
| Decision relevance | Which decision could change and when |
| Review trigger | New data, threshold, event or date that should reopen the assessment |
| Status | Unchecked, provisional, corroborated, contested, superseded or resolved, with definitions established by the project |

This record is not a universal scoring system. It is a minimum traceability structure that helps the project distinguish raw observations, source statements, analyst interpretations and conclusions.

## 17. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Concept vs indicator | A concept is what we want to understand; an indicator is an observed or constructed signal used to assess it. |
| Indicator vs proxy | An indicator signals a condition; a proxy stands in for a harder-to-observe concept and may be imperfect. |
| Measure vs estimate | A measure follows a defined rule; an estimate infers a value, often for an unobserved population quantity. |
| Metric vs target | A metric records or summarizes a quantity; a target is a desired value or threshold. |
| Observation vs inference | An observation is recorded evidence; an inference is a conclusion drawn from evidence. |
| Stock vs flow | A stock is measured at a point in time; a flow accumulates over an interval. |
| Level vs growth rate | A level is a quantity; growth is a relative change over a defined interval. |
| Nominal vs real | Nominal values use prevailing prices; real values use a specified adjustment to separate price changes for a defined purpose. |
| Gross vs net | The distinction depends on which specified deduction has or has not been applied. |
| Count vs rate | A count totals events or units; a rate relates a count or quantity to a defined denominator or exposure. |
| Mean vs median | The mean uses arithmetic averaging; the median is the middle ordered value and is less sensitive to extreme values. |
| Accuracy vs precision | Accuracy concerns closeness to a reference or truth; precision concerns repeatability or numerical resolution, depending on context. |
| Reliability vs validity | Reliability concerns consistency; validity concerns whether the method measures the intended construct. |
| Missing vs zero | Missing means unknown or unobserved; zero is a measured or defined value. |
| Revision vs manipulation | A revision changes a published value; its cause must be investigated rather than presumed. |
| Correlation vs causation | Association alone does not establish that one variable caused another. |
| Mechanism vs causal evidence | A mechanism explains how an effect could occur; evidence is needed to establish whether it did. |
| Confounder vs mediator | A confounder can create a misleading association; a mediator lies on a causal pathway being studied. |
| Prediction vs explanation | Prediction concerns forecasting outcomes; explanation concerns why a pattern or outcome occurs. |
| Forecast vs scenario | A forecast estimates an outcome; a scenario describes a possible pathway under specified assumptions. |
| Uncertainty vs variability | Uncertainty concerns what is not known; variability is actual variation in the process, though both may coexist. |
| Confidence score vs probability | A score is not a probability unless its meaning and calibration justify that interpretation. |
| Triangulation vs source counting | Triangulation compares distinct evidence; multiple copies of one claim do not provide independent corroboration. |
| Sample size vs representativeness | A large sample can still be biased if the selection or coverage is unsuitable. |
| Statistical significance vs importance | A statistical result does not by itself establish practical, economic or policy importance. |
| Aggregate change vs within-group change | A total can move because individual units change or because the composition of units changes. |
| Leading vs coincident vs lagging | These refer to timing relative to a target condition and must be validated in context. |
| Model fit vs generalization | Fit describes performance on selected data; generalization concerns performance in new or changed conditions. |
| Historical revised data vs real-time data | Revised history may contain information unavailable to the decision-maker at the original time. |
| Evidence assessment vs recommendation | Evidence assesses what is supported; a recommendation adds goals, costs, trade-offs and risk tolerance. |

## 19. Evidence status, limits and open questions

### Established methodological principles

- Measurement depends on concepts, definitions, scope, units and methods.
- An indicator or proxy does not fully embody the concept it represents.
- Missingness, selection, revisions and classification changes can materially affect conclusions.
- Association and plausible mechanisms do not independently establish causation.
- Triangulation requires attention to source independence and error mechanisms.
- Uncertainty should be stated in a form appropriate to the evidence and decision.
- Aggregates can hide distributional and compositional changes.
- Model performance can deteriorate when the environment changes.
- A decision threshold should reflect consequences of false positives, false negatives and delay.
- Reproducible evidence records help separate observations, interpretations and claims.

### Limits

- This note does not define any Lifeconomist core indicator or declare a macroeconomic state.
- It does not provide a universal ranking of evidence sources or a universal confidence score.
- It does not select one causal-inference method for every question.
- It does not estimate the reliability of any particular dataset or current economic claim.
- It does not replace domain-specific accounting standards, survey methods, statistical protocols or legal requirements.
- Formal indicator design, validation tests, source selection and operational thresholds belong in Workstream B and relevant domain-specific specifications.

### Open questions

- Which minimum metadata fields should every Lifeconomist indicator carry, and which are conditional on source type?
- How should the project preserve real-time data vintages alongside revised historical values?
- What source-independence rules should be used when multiple publishers share underlying data?
- How should qualitative evidence, numerical indicators and model outputs be combined without false precision?
- Which claims require formal causal designs, and which can be handled as descriptive or mechanism-based assessments?
- How should the project test robustness to alternative definitions, denominators, deflators, weights and sample boundaries?
- What confidence rubric, if any, can be calibrated across different evidence domains without hiding important differences?
- How should review triggers be set for indicators whose source coverage or data-generating process changes?
- How should conflicting sources be represented when no defensible single estimate is available?

## 20. Short summary

Economic measurement and inference are the processes by which observations are turned into defined quantities and then into claims about economic reality. Reliable analysis requires clear concepts, operational definitions, appropriate units and denominators, transparent scope, awareness of measurement error and revisions, comparability checks, and a distinction between description, association, prediction, mechanism and causation. Source quality, independence, uncertainty, model limitations and aggregation effects all shape what can be concluded. Decisions add objectives, costs and risk tolerance to the evidence and should be proportionate to the consequences of error. For Lifeconomist, every important claim should be traceable from its definition and source through transformations and reasoning to its limitations and decision relevance.

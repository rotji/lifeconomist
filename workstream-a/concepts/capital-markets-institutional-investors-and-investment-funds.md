# Concept 25 — Capital Markets, Institutional Investors and Investment Funds

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; no current fund, investor or market estimates are asserted  
**Date:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 24 — Banking Liquidity, Market Liquidity and System-Wide Liquidity](banking-liquidity-market-liquidity-and-system-wide-liquidity.md)  
**Roadmap connection:** Group 18 — Capital markets, institutional investors and investment funds  
**Purpose:** Explain how investment organizations collect, manage, allocate and return capital; distinguish the money and assets they manage from assets they legally own; and trace mandates, decision rights, funding commitments, capital calls, deployment, valuation and distributions without conflating them.

## 1. The starting question: whose capital is it, and what has happened to it?

A headline may say that a fund has raised $500 million, an investor manages $20 billion, a pension fund owns shares, or a private-equity firm has invested in a company. Those statements can describe very different realities. The amount may be a target, a commitment, assets under management, assets under custody, a market valuation, money already transferred, or capital actually deployed. The named organization may be the legal owner, an agent, an investment manager, a custodian, a general partner or a beneficiary's representative.

Before interpreting an investment claim, ask:

1. **Which entity is being described?** A fund, fund manager, pension plan, insurer, custodian, general partner, limited partner, portfolio company or broader corporate group?
2. **Whose assets are involved?** The manager's own balance sheet, a pooled fund, client portfolios, beneficiaries' assets or assets held in custody?
3. **What authority does the entity have?** Does it own the assets, choose investments, execute instructions, safeguard assets or merely report information?
4. **What stage has been reached?** Target, announced fundraise, signed commitment, capital call, cash receipt, investment purchase, operating deployment, exit or distribution?
5. **What is the denominator and valuation basis?** Gross or net assets, committed capital, called capital, invested cost, fair value, market value or another defined measure?
6. **What restrictions apply?** Investment mandate, benchmark, regulation, liquidity terms, fiduciary duties, concentration limits, geography, asset class or time horizon?
7. **What evidence supports the statement?** Audited accounts, regulatory filing, fund documents, transaction closing notice, portfolio report or an unverified announcement?

The central rule is that **capital managed, capital committed, capital transferred, assets owned and economic influence are not interchangeable**.

## 2. What capital markets and institutional investors do

Capital markets connect entities seeking longer-term financing or investment opportunities with participants allocating savings and other funds into financial claims such as shares and bonds. Institutional investors aggregate, manage or invest resources on behalf of organizations, clients or beneficiaries. Investment funds are legal or contractual arrangements through which assets may be pooled and managed according to stated rules.

These functions can overlap, but they are not the same:

- A **capital market** is an arena and set of institutions for issuing and trading longer-term financial claims.
- An **institutional investor** is an organization that invests assets or manages investment decisions at institutional scale or under a formal mandate.
- An **investment fund** is a pool or arrangement holding investments for investors under governing documents and applicable law.
- An **investment manager** makes or implements investment decisions within delegated authority.
- A **fund sponsor** establishes or promotes a fund and may supply capital, governance, distribution or other support.
- A **custodian** safeguards assets and records holdings or transactions under an agreed legal and operational framework.
- An **administrator** may maintain fund accounting, investor records, valuation processes and reporting.
- An **investment adviser** provides advice within the scope of its agreement and regulatory status.
- A **beneficiary** is the person or group ultimately entitled to specified benefits under the relevant arrangement; the beneficiary is not necessarily the legal holder of each underlying asset.

One institution can perform several roles, and a role may be split across multiple firms. Never infer the legal structure from a brand name alone.

## 3. Main types of institutional investors and funds

### Pension funds and retirement schemes

They invest resources intended to help meet retirement benefits. Their investment horizon, liquidity needs and risk capacity depend on the scheme's obligations, membership, funding status, governing rules and benefit design. A pension fund's assets are not automatically available to its manager for its own use.

### Insurance companies

Insurers invest premiums and other resources while managing claims, policyholder obligations, regulatory capital and asset-liability matching. Their investment choices may be constrained by the timing, currency and uncertainty of future claims. Insurer balance-sheet assets must be distinguished from assets held in separate accounts or on behalf of clients, where applicable.

### Mutual funds and collective investment schemes

These pool investor resources and issue interests or units under specified rules. Investors generally receive an economic interest in the pool, subject to the legal structure, fees, valuation and redemption terms. A fund's daily dealing promise, if offered, depends on its governing documents and the liquidity of its holdings.

### Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)

ETFs are investment funds whose shares or units trade on an exchange. Their structure, creation/redemption process, tracking objective, holdings, fees and market-making arrangements affect how trading prices relate to net asset value. An ETF's exchange-traded status does not mean every underlying holding is equally liquid.

### Sovereign wealth funds and public investment funds

These are state-linked investment arrangements established under specific mandates, such as managing public wealth, stabilization resources, pension obligations or development objectives. Their governance, source of funds, disclosure and accountability vary. A fund described as sovereign is not automatically controlled day to day by a ministry, nor does public ownership by itself establish how investment decisions are made.

### Endowments and foundations

These invest assets to support institutional or charitable purposes. Spending policies, donor restrictions, governance and long-term objectives can influence the balance between liquidity, return and preservation of real purchasing power.

### Venture capital funds

These typically invest in young or growing businesses with uncertain outcomes and the possibility of substantial gains or losses. Capital may be deployed across multiple financing rounds, with follow-on reserves and long holding periods. Announced participation in a round does not establish the amount ultimately transferred or the fund's current ownership percentage.

### Private equity funds

These invest in private companies or acquire significant stakes, sometimes using debt and governance rights to change strategy, operations or capital structure. Strategies differ substantially. An acquisition announcement does not itself prove that a transaction closed, that promised operational improvements occurred or that returns were realized.

### Private credit funds

These provide loans or other credit exposures outside, or alongside, traditional public bond and bank-lending channels. Their instruments, borrower types, leverage, valuation practices, redemption terms and liquidity can vary. A private credit asset's reported value does not guarantee that it can be sold quickly at that value.

### Hedge funds and other alternative funds

These may use short selling, derivatives, leverage, relative-value strategies or less conventional asset classes, depending on mandate and law. “Hedge fund” is a broad label rather than a single strategy or risk profile. Leverage, counterparties, redemption terms and valuation methods must be examined individually.

## 4. Fund structure: investors, managers and governance

Many private funds use a structure in which limited partners (LPs) provide most of the committed investor capital and a general partner (GP) controls the fund under the governing documents. An investment manager may be the GP itself or a separate related entity. This is a common pattern, not a universal structure; legal forms and duties vary by jurisdiction.

- **Limited partner (LP):** typically an investor with rights and obligations defined by the fund documents and applicable law.
- **General partner (GP):** in a limited partnership structure, the entity with specified management authority and responsibilities; its exact legal obligations depend on jurisdiction and documents.
- **Fund manager:** the entity or person making or implementing investment decisions under delegated authority.
- **Management company:** the business that may employ staff, receive management fees and provide services.
- **Fund vehicle:** the legal entity, partnership, trust or other arrangement holding investments and liabilities.
- **Investment committee:** a body that may approve or review investment decisions under internal governance.
- **Advisory committee:** a body that may advise on conflicts, valuations, extensions or other matters; its powers depend on the documents.
- **LP advisory committee:** a common fund-governance mechanism, not necessarily a body with authority over routine portfolio decisions.

The same corporate group may operate the management company, sponsor vehicles and provide services to portfolio companies, but related-party links need evidence. Shared branding or a common address is not enough to establish control, ownership or decision rights.

## 5. Commitments, capital calls, deployment and distributions

Private funds often receive commitments before receiving all the cash. Investors agree to provide capital subject to fund documents; the GP then calls amounts as needed. This creates several distinct stages:

**Target → commitments signed → capital called → cash received → investments deployed → value realized → proceeds distributed.**

In plain language, a fund can announce a target, secure commitments from investors, and only later request the money. Cash received may remain temporarily uninvested, cover permitted expenses, or be deployed over time. Investments may later be sold, refinanced or otherwise realized; only then may proceeds be distributed to investors. Each step requires its own evidence. A commitment is not cash already received, and a portfolio valuation is not the same as realized cash proceeds.

Important terms:

- **Target fund size:** the amount a fund aims to raise.
- **First close / final close:** milestones in accepting investor commitments under the fund's process; their meanings depend on the documents.
- **Commitment:** a contractual promise to contribute capital under specified conditions.
- **Capital call / drawdown:** a request for some or all of a commitment to be funded.
- **Called capital:** the amount requested from investors under the relevant definition.
- **Paid-in capital:** the amount actually contributed, subject to the reporting definition.
- **Uncalled commitment:** the portion of an investor's commitment not yet called, subject to remaining obligations and fund terms.
- **Invested capital / deployed capital:** amounts used for investments, but definitions vary; clarify whether fees, expenses, bridge positions and follow-on reserves are included.
- **Realization / exit:** an event that converts an investment into cash or another realized consideration, subject to deal terms.
- **Distribution:** cash or assets transferred from a fund to investors under the distribution rules.
- **Return of capital:** distribution of contributed capital; not automatically profit.
- **Carry / carried interest:** a performance-linked share of fund profits under specified terms, distinct from ordinary management fees.
- **Management fee:** compensation for managing the fund, calculated according to the fee agreement; the base may change over time.

A funding announcement may combine commitments, co-investment, debt financing, grants or other resources. It should not be described as cash deployed into a company unless the evidence supports that specific stage.

## 6. Assets under management, ownership and custody

**Assets under management (AUM)** is a reporting measure of assets for which an organization provides investment management or related discretionary services under a stated definition. It is not a universal accounting category. Different managers may include or exclude advisory-only assets, leverage, uncalled commitments, cash, affiliated funds or assets managed on a non-discretionary basis.

Other measures include:

- **Assets owned:** assets legally or beneficially owned by a defined entity, based on the applicable legal and accounting boundaries.
- **Assets under custody:** assets for which a custodian provides safekeeping or related services; custody does not by itself imply investment discretion or beneficial ownership.
- **Regulatory assets:** assets counted under a specified regulatory definition.
- **Net asset value (NAV):** assets minus liabilities under a stated valuation methodology and date.
- **Committed capital:** contractual commitments, which may not yet have been funded.
- **Portfolio value:** value attributed to holdings under a stated valuation method and date.
- **Enterprise value / equity value:** valuation concepts for businesses; neither is synonymous with fund AUM or cash available to investors.

A manager may report large AUM while owning only a small amount of the underlying assets on its own balance sheet. Its revenue may come from fees rather than ownership of the entire managed portfolio. Similarly, a custodian may hold securities in its systems without being their beneficial owner.

Before comparing AUM, establish the reporting date, currency, gross/net basis, asset perimeter, discretion, inclusion of affiliated vehicles, treatment of leverage and uncalled commitments, and whether values are market-based or estimated.

## 7. Valuation, performance and realized returns

Private and illiquid investments may not trade frequently. Their reported values can rely on models, comparable transactions, recent financing rounds, appraisals or other assumptions. A reported NAV is therefore an estimate under a method and date, not necessarily the price at which the entire portfolio could be sold immediately.

Performance terms also differ:

- **Gross return:** performance before specified fees, expenses or carry.
- **Net return:** performance after the stated deductions; the precise treatment must be disclosed.
- **Realized return:** return associated with investments or proceeds actually realized under the metric's definition.
- **Unrealized gain/loss:** change in estimated value of holdings not yet sold.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR):** a rate that equates dated cash flows under a particular calculation; it is sensitive to timing and interim cash flows.
- **Multiple of invested capital (MOIC):** a ratio comparing value or proceeds to invested capital under the stated definition; it does not by itself capture timing.
- **Benchmark-relative return:** performance compared with a chosen benchmark; meaningful interpretation depends on benchmark suitability, currency, fees and risk.
- **Risk-adjusted performance:** performance evaluated relative to a specified risk measure or model; no single measure captures all relevant risks.

A high IRR on a small early distribution may not imply a high total cash multiple. A high unrealized valuation may not yet be monetizable. Comparisons across funds need aligned inception dates, vintage years, fee treatment, currency, cash-flow conventions, leverage and asset strategy. Reported performance should not be treated as a promise of future results.

## 8. Mandates, benchmarks and allocation decisions

An investment mandate specifies what the manager is permitted or expected to do. It may define eligible assets, risk limits, geography, sector, liquidity, leverage, responsible-investment restrictions, return objectives and reporting requirements. A benchmark provides a comparison or reference, but does not necessarily define the whole mandate.

Portfolio allocation can involve strategic allocation across asset classes, tactical changes in response to conditions, rebalancing back toward target weights, and selection of individual securities or investments. These are distinct decision levels. A manager may have discretion to rebalance without authority to change the fund's core mandate.

Questions to investigate include:

- Who sets the mandate, and who can change it?
- Is the manager discretionary, advisory or execution-only?
- What are the benchmark and performance period?
- Are assets allocated directly, through external managers or through fund-of-funds structures?
- What limits apply to concentration, leverage, liquidity, geography and counterparties?
- How are conflicts of interest disclosed and handled?
- Who approves valuations, fees, extensions and related-party transactions?
- What triggers rebalancing, redemption restrictions or risk escalation?

An investment decision may be influenced by regulation, liabilities, benchmarks, liquidity needs, governance, incentives and internal capacity. Do not infer a manager's motive solely from a portfolio holding or price movement.

## 9. Investor influence versus ownership and operational control

Investors can influence companies through voting rights, board appointment rights, shareholder agreements, covenants, financing conditions, engagement, proxy voting, or the ability to provide or withdraw capital. The strength of influence depends on the rights, ownership structure, concentration of other shareholders, contracts, law and actual exercise of power.

Keep these roles distinct:

- **Legal owner:** holds the legally recognized title or interest under the relevant system.
- **Beneficial owner:** ultimately enjoys specified benefits or control, according to the applicable legal definition; definitions vary.
- **Voting rights holder:** can exercise specified votes, which may differ from economic ownership.
- **Investment manager:** chooses or implements portfolio decisions under a mandate.
- **Custodian / nominee:** may hold or record assets for another party without being the ultimate beneficiary.
- **Creditor:** holds a claim for repayment; may have covenants or security rights but is not automatically an owner.
- **Board or management:** governs or operates a company under the applicable corporate structure.
- **Beneficiary:** is entitled to benefits under a trust, pension or other arrangement, but may not direct specific investment decisions.

An investor with a minority stake can sometimes exert significant influence through board rights, financing dependence or shareholder agreements. A large economic interest does not automatically prove day-to-day operational control. Claims about beneficial ownership, control or influence should identify the specific rights and evidence.

## 10. How capital reaches an operating business

Capital markets and investment funds are part of a chain that may connect savers, beneficiaries, institutions, managers, funds, intermediaries and operating businesses. The path depends on the instrument and transaction.

**Beneficiaries or investors → fund or investment vehicle → purchase of a financial claim → issuer or seller receives funds → business uses resources under its operating decisions.**

In ordinary language, an investor's money may enter a fund, which then buys newly issued shares or lends money to a business. In a primary issuance or new loan, the business may receive financing. If the fund buys existing shares from another shareholder in a secondary transaction, the seller generally receives the sale proceeds, not the company. After financing reaches a business, it may be used for equipment, payroll, acquisitions, working capital, debt repayment or other purposes. The investment transaction alone does not prove productive investment, new capacity or improved outcomes.

This is why the following stages should be tracked separately: investor commitment, cash transfer, security or loan purchase, recipient of proceeds, corporate receipt, expenditure, asset creation or capability improvement, output, and final economic or social outcome.

## 11. Primary versus secondary transactions and co-investment

In a **primary issuance**, a company or public issuer creates new securities or receives financing under a new transaction. In a **secondary transaction**, an existing holder sells a claim to another investor. The secondary trade can improve liquidity and ownership transfer, but the issuer generally does not receive the buyer's payment.

**Co-investment** commonly means investing alongside a fund or sponsor in a particular transaction, but structures differ. Co-investment may be direct or through a separate vehicle; it may have different fees, rights, timing or information access. A press release listing several investors does not necessarily reveal how much each invested, whether they invested on identical terms, or whether all funds were deployed simultaneously.

**Follow-on investment** is additional financing or investment in an existing portfolio company. It can reflect confidence, a contractual commitment, a need to protect an earlier investment, or other motives; the reason must be investigated rather than inferred.

## 12. Liquidity mismatch and redemption promises

Some funds hold assets that cannot be sold quickly but offer investors periodic redemption. If many investors seek cash at once, the fund may need to use liquid holdings, borrow, sell less-liquid assets, apply permitted gates or suspensions, or follow other actions allowed by its governing documents and law.

A possible mechanism is:

**Redemption requests rise → liquid buffers are used → assets must be sold or funding obtained → costs or discounts rise → remaining investors may be affected.**

In plain language, investors may ask for their money back faster than the fund can sell its underlying investments without losses or delay. The fund's response depends on its legal structure, redemption terms, liquidity tools, asset mix, cash buffers and market conditions. This is a possible mismatch, not evidence that every fund with illiquid assets is unsafe or that every redemption request creates a crisis.

Private funds often have long lockups or limited withdrawal rights; open-ended funds may offer more frequent dealing. Neither label alone proves the true liquidity of the assets or the fund's resilience. The governing documents and portfolio composition matter.

## 13. Fees, incentives and conflicts of interest

Investment arrangements can include management fees, performance fees, carried interest, transaction fees, monitoring fees, administration costs, custody fees and other expenses. Their bases, recipients, timing and treatment vary.

Fees affect investor net returns, but a fee alone does not establish poor performance or misconduct. Incentives can shape decisions: a manager rewarded for assets gathered may prioritize growth in AUM; performance-linked compensation may encourage risk-taking or, under other structures, more careful alignment. The actual effects depend on contract terms, governance, reputation, regulation and the manager's other incentives.

Potential conflicts include allocating scarce investment opportunities among funds, cross-trades, related-party transactions, valuation decisions, fee arrangements, allocation of expenses, follow-on investments and exit timing. These are areas for due diligence, not proof of wrongdoing. Evaluate disclosure, independent oversight, allocation policies, valuation controls and remedies.

## 14. Concentration, interconnectedness and systemic relevance

Institutional investors can be connected through shared holdings, common lenders, prime brokers, custodians, asset managers, clearing systems, collateral markets and redemption or margin obligations. Multiple funds may hold similar assets or rely on the same financing sources. Such connections can create common exposures, but the existence of a connection does not prove that losses will propagate.

Concentration can be assessed by issuer, sector, asset class, geography, counterparty, funding source, strategy, investor base or shared risk factor. A portfolio with many holdings may remain exposed to one common driver. A large institution is not automatically systemically important; size, substitutability, leverage, interconnectedness, market footprint, critical functions and available resolution mechanisms all matter.

When investigating potential transmission, identify the specific exposure, direction, timing, contract, collateral or liquidity mechanism, and evidence of actual propagation. Do not equate shared holdings with coordinated action or infer intent from parallel investment decisions.

## 15. A worked example: interpreting a fund announcement

Assume a fictional venture fund announces a target of $100 million. It later reports $80 million in commitments. It calls $20 million from investors, receives the cash, invests $12 million across several companies, and retains the remainder for permitted expenses and future investments. These figures are entirely illustrative.

What can be said at each stage?

- Target size: the planned fundraising objective was $100 million.
- Commitments: investors contractually agreed to contribute $80 million subject to fund terms.
- Called capital: the fund requested $20 million.
- Cash received: the called amount was actually paid, if supported by records.
- Deployed investment: $12 million was invested under the stated definition.
- Remaining cash: the difference is not automatically idle or freely distributable; permitted reserves, expenses and obligations must be checked.
- Portfolio value: any reported current valuation is an estimate at a stated date and method.
- Investor return: cannot be established from the commitment or investment announcement alone; distributions, remaining value, fees and cash-flow timing are required.

The headline “$100 million fund” would be misleading if presented as $100 million already invested. Even “$80 million raised” may need clarification about whether it means signed commitments, legally closed commitments or paid-in cash.

## 16. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before making a claim about an institutional investor, fund or capital allocation, establish:

1. Legal name and relevant vehicle; distinguish the manager, fund, sponsor and portfolio company.
2. Jurisdiction, reporting date, currency and reporting perimeter.
3. Whether the number is a target, commitment, called amount, paid-in capital, deployed capital, AUM, assets under custody, NAV, valuation or realized proceeds.
4. Whether the amount is gross or net and how leverage, fees, cash, liabilities and uncalled commitments are treated.
5. Who legally owns the asset and who has beneficial rights under the applicable definition.
6. Who has discretionary authority, voting rights, custody, administration and operating control.
7. Which primary documents govern the fund, investor rights, fees, liquidity and distributions.
8. Whether an announced transaction signed, closed, funded and settled.
9. Whether funds reached the issuer, an existing shareholder, a lender, an acquisition vehicle or another recipient.
10. Whether reported investment is new financing or secondary transfer of an existing claim.
11. How valuations are produced, how frequently they are updated and whether there is an observable transaction price.
12. Whether performance is gross/net, realized/unrealized, IRR/MOIC or another measure.
13. Which fees, expenses, carry, leverage and cash-flow timing affect the comparison.
14. What mandate and benchmark govern allocation, and whether the comparison is like-for-like.
15. What redemption terms and asset liquidity constraints apply.
16. Which conflicts of interest and related-party arrangements are disclosed.
17. What evidence supports claims about influence, ownership, control or beneficiary interests.
18. What evidence supports a causal claim that capital improved output, productivity, employment or welfare.
19. What alternative explanations exist, including market repricing, leverage, policy, other investors or business changes.
20. What is unknown, unreported, estimated or disputed?

## 17. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Capital market vs institutional investor | A market is an arrangement for financing/trading claims; an institutional investor is a participant or organization. |
| Investor vs investment manager | An investor supplies or owns investment capital; a manager makes decisions under delegated authority. |
| Fund vs fund manager | The fund is the investment vehicle or pool; the manager is the entity providing management services. |
| AUM vs assets owned | AUM follows a management-reporting definition; assets owned follow legal/accounting boundaries. |
| Assets under custody vs assets under management | Safekeeping/recordkeeping does not automatically confer investment discretion. |
| Beneficial owner vs nominee | A nominee may hold title or records for another party; the beneficial interest depends on the applicable arrangement and law. |
| Commitment vs capital call | A commitment is a promise to fund under conditions; a call requests contribution of a specified amount. |
| Called vs paid-in capital | A request for funds is not proof the money has been received. |
| Paid-in vs deployed capital | Fund cash received may remain uninvested or be used for permitted costs/reserves. |
| Deployed capital vs productive investment | An investment transaction does not prove new productive capacity or successful use. |
| Fund size vs portfolio value | Fund size may mean target, commitments or another measure; portfolio value is a valuation of holdings. |
| NAV vs executable liquidation value | Estimated net asset value does not guarantee immediate sale proceeds. |
| Unrealized vs realized return | An estimated valuation gain is different from proceeds actually realized. |
| IRR vs MOIC | IRR reflects cash-flow timing; MOIC compares value/proceeds with invested capital under a definition. |
| Gross vs net performance | Net performance deducts specified fees and expenses; exact conventions must be stated. |
| Primary vs secondary transaction | Primary financing may provide funds to the issuer; a secondary sale generally pays the existing seller. |
| Fundraising vs financing received by a company | A fundraise can create commitments to a fund, not necessarily cash already provided to a portfolio company. |
| Co-investor vs controlling owner | Participation in a transaction does not alone establish control. |
| Investor influence vs operational control | Voting, board, covenant or funding rights differ from running day-to-day operations. |
| Public ownership vs direct government management | A public fund's ownership does not by itself reveal operational decision-making. |
| Benchmark vs mandate | A benchmark is a reference; the mandate defines permitted or expected investment activity. |
| Diversification vs many holdings | Holdings may share a common driver or counterparty. |
| Liquidity terms vs asset liquidity | Redemption rights offered to investors differ from how quickly underlying holdings can be sold. |
| Fee vs misconduct | Fees can create incentives or conflicts, but their existence alone proves no wrongdoing. |
| Fund return vs economic impact | Financial returns do not by themselves establish effects on output, jobs, productivity or welfare. |

## 19. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions

- Investment organizations perform different roles: ownership, management, custody, administration, advice, financing and benefit are distinct.
- AUM is a management metric under a stated definition, not automatically the manager's owned assets.
- Commitments, capital calls, paid-in funds, deployed investments, valuations, realizations and distributions are separate stages.
- Fund structures, mandates, fees, rights and liquidity terms determine how capital is managed and returned.
- Primary issuance and secondary trading have different implications for the recipient of transaction proceeds.
- Reported valuations and performance measures depend on methodology, timing, fees, leverage and cash-flow conventions.
- Investor influence and operational control require evidence of rights and actual arrangements.
- A capital allocation event does not itself prove productive, social or economic impact.

### Scope limits

- This note does not estimate current AUM, fund flows, investor ownership or investment performance in Nigeria or globally.
- Legal structures, fiduciary duties, beneficial-ownership definitions and regulatory requirements differ by jurisdiction and must be checked against current primary sources.
- Public announcements may not disclose all terms, investor identities, paid-in amounts or allocation details.
- Private asset valuations and performance comparisons may be incomplete, stale or non-comparable.
- No fund's conduct, motive, control or impact is assessed here.

### Questions for evidence-based follow-up

- Which public and regulatory sources provide comparable fund, manager and institutional-investor data across jurisdictions?
- How can committed, called, paid-in and deployed capital be distinguished consistently in reported datasets?
- What disclosure rules apply to institutional holdings, beneficial ownership, private funds and public investment vehicles?
- How should private-fund performance be compared across vintage years, currencies, fee structures and valuation conventions?
- What evidence is required to establish whether a financing transaction added new capital to a business?
- How can fund networks and shared exposures be mapped without treating every common holding as causal interconnection?
- Which investment-flow measures can be linked to productive outcomes without double-counting transfers of existing financial claims?

## 20. Short summary

Institutional investors and investment funds organize the allocation of capital, but their labels and reported figures can obscure who owns assets, who controls decisions, where money has actually moved and what outcomes followed. AUM, commitments, capital calls, paid-in capital, deployment, portfolio valuation, realized proceeds and distributions are different measures or stages. Fund structures allocate rights, duties, fees, risks and decision authority among investors, managers, custodians, vehicles and beneficiaries. Primary financing may deliver funds to an issuer, while secondary trading generally pays an existing holder. Lifeconomist should trace each entity, role, amount, date, legal right and transaction stage separately before drawing conclusions about ownership, influence, performance or economic impact.

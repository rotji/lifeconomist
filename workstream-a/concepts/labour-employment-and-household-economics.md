# Concept 14 — Labour, Employment and Household Economics

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal indicator specifications remain in Workstream B  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 13 — National Income, GDP and Economic Accounting](national-income-gdp-and-economic-accounting.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain how labour, employment, earnings and household resources relate to production and well-being, while keeping distinct concepts such as employment, unemployment, underemployment, labour-force participation, wages, income, consumption, poverty and productivity.

## 1. Why labour and household economics matter

Production depends on people, skills, time, organization, tools, infrastructure and other inputs. Yet total output does not tell us by itself who worked, how much work was available, what people were paid, whether their work was secure, or whether household resources covered basic needs.

Labour statistics describe aspects of people's relationship to paid work and the labour market. Household economics also considers income from multiple sources, taxes and transfers, consumption, saving, assets, debt, care responsibilities and living conditions. These domains overlap but are not interchangeable: a person can be employed and poor; a household can receive income without every member being employed; and national output can rise without gains being evenly distributed.

This note establishes conceptual distinctions. It does not choose an official Nigerian series, estimate current unemployment or poverty, or replace Workstream B's indicator definitions.

## 2. Work, labour and employment are not synonyms

**Work** is a broad concept that can include paid employment, self-employment, unpaid household services, care work, subsistence production, volunteering and other activities. Statistical systems define categories for different purposes.

**Labour** may refer to human effort used in production, the working population in a statistical framework, or the labour services bought and sold in a labour market. Always identify the context.

**Employment** is a statistical status defined by a particular labour-force framework and reference period. In common labour statistics, a person may be classified as employed if they performed a specified amount of work for pay or profit, including qualifying temporary absences from a job. Exact thresholds, reference periods and treatment of different activities depend on the framework in use.

Employment is not identical to having a permanent salaried job. It may include wage employment, self-employment, employers, own-account workers, contributing family workers and other categories under the relevant standard. Conversely, unpaid work within one's own household may be socially and economically important without being classified as employment in the headline labour-force measure.

When comparing statistics, use the definition in the specific release. Definitions of employment and unemployment have evolved, including treatment of own-use production and other forms of work. A change in measured rates can reflect both real conditions and a changed statistical framework.

## 3. The working-age population and labour force

The **working-age population** is the population within the age range used by a statistical system for labour-market analysis. The age range must be stated; it is not universal across every survey or publication.

The **labour force** (also called the economically active population in some contexts) generally includes people who are employed plus people who are unemployed under the specified criteria. It does not include everyone of working age.

People outside the labour force may include students not seeking work, retirees, people with care responsibilities, people unable to work, discouraged jobseekers who do not meet the current search or availability criteria, and others. These categories are heterogeneous; being outside the labour force does not necessarily mean being unwilling to work.

The **labour-force participation rate** is the labour force divided by the relevant working-age population, multiplied by 100. The denominator and age range matter. A rate can fall because employment opportunities deteriorate, because more people enter education, because demographics change, because care constraints change, or because survey definitions and measurement change. The rate alone does not identify the cause.

## 3.1 Demographics, dependency and the demographic dividend

**Age dependency ratios** compare population groups conventionally described as dependants—often children and older people—with a working-age population. The exact age cutoffs and numerator/denominator must be stated. These ratios are demographic indicators, not a direct count of people who are economically dependent: some working-age people do not earn income, and some children or older people work or receive substantial resources.

**Economic dependency** concerns the relationship between people supported by others or by public systems and those supplying income or resources. It depends on employment, earnings, unpaid care, taxes, transfers, health, household structure and institutional arrangements, not age alone. A population with a high share of working-age people can still have low employment or low productivity.

A **demographic dividend** is the potential economic benefit that can arise when the share of working-age people rises relative to dependants. It is a potential, not an automatic outcome. Realizing it depends on health, education and skills, job creation, productive investment, women's participation, infrastructure, institutional capacity and the ability of workers to find productive employment.

**Population ageing** can change labour supply, care needs, pension systems, health spending, saving patterns and demand. These effects vary with productivity, retirement arrangements, migration, household support and public policy. A demographic trend alone does not determine the fiscal or economic result.

When using a dependency or participation statistic, document the age bands, population denominator, source, reference date and whether the measure is demographic or economic. Do not infer employment, household dependence or fiscal sustainability from an age ratio alone.

## 4. Employment, unemployment and underemployment

### Employment
Employment is a status under a defined statistical framework and reference period. It says that a person meets the criteria for employment; it does not by itself establish adequate hours, fair pay, security, safety, skill use or satisfaction.

### Unemployment
In common international labour statistics, a person is classified as unemployed when they are not employed, are available for work, and have taken specified steps to seek work during a defined period, subject to the applicable standard and exceptions. Some people who want work may not be classified as unemployed if they do not meet the search or availability criteria. They should not be treated as though they do not want work.

### Time-related underemployment
Time-related underemployment generally concerns employed people whose working time is insufficient, who want additional hours, and who are available to work more under the applicable criteria. It captures a dimension that the unemployment rate alone misses.

### Other forms of labour underutilization
Broader measures may combine unemployment, time-related underemployment and parts of the potential labour force. These measures can reveal unmet demand for work that a headline unemployment rate misses. The exact components and labels must be taken from the statistical release.

A person working a few hours can be employed under a particular definition while still needing more work. Someone who wants a job but stopped searching may be outside the labour force under a strict unemployment measure while still representing unmet work aspirations. These cases show why a single rate cannot summarize the labour market.

## 5. Unemployment rate, participation rate and employment-to-population ratio

The **unemployment rate** is unemployed people divided by the labour force, not by the entire working-age population. A falling rate can result from more people finding jobs, but it can also fall if unemployed people stop searching and leave the labour force. Therefore, unemployment should be interpreted with participation and employment measures.

The **employment-to-population ratio** is the number of employed people divided by the relevant population, usually a specified working-age group. It measures the share of that population classified as employed, rather than the share of the labour force without work.

The **participation rate** measures the share of the specified population in the labour force. It reflects both employment and unemployment.

These measures answer different questions:
- Unemployment rate: among those in the labour force, what share is unemployed?
- Employment-to-population ratio: what share of the specified population is employed?
- Participation rate: what share of the specified population is in the labour force?

Use the same age range, reference period, population base and statistical framework before comparing rates. Changes in one indicator should be checked against the others.

## 6. Types and quality of employment

Employment status and employment quality are separate dimensions. Useful distinctions include:
- **Employee/wage employment:** work for an employer under an employment relationship.
- **Self-employment:** work in one's own economic activity, with categories depending on whether the person employs others or works on their own account.
- **Employer:** a self-employed person who employs workers under the relevant definition.
- **Own-account worker:** a self-employed person who does not employ employees, as defined by the statistical framework.
- **Contributing family worker:** a person who works in a market-oriented family economic unit under the relevant criteria without the same employment arrangement as an employee.
- **Formal and informal employment:** classifications that depend on the characteristics of the job, enterprise, registration, social protection and applicable statistical definitions; informal employment is not always synonymous with employment in an informal-sector enterprise.
- **Temporary, casual, seasonal or permanent arrangements:** contract and duration categories whose legal and statistical meanings depend on context.

Job quality may involve earnings, hours, safety, stability, social protection, autonomy, bargaining power, skill use, predictability and access to benefits. No single status variable fully measures it.

Formal registration alone does not guarantee high-quality work, and informal status does not mean that the work has no economic value. These terms describe different institutional and employment conditions and should not be used as moral judgements.

## 7. Working hours, time and labour input

The number of employed people is not the same as the amount of labour input. One person working 10 hours and another working 50 hours count as two employed people, but their hours differ substantially.

**Hours actually worked**, **usual hours**, **paid hours** and **contracted hours** may differ. Actual hours can be affected by overtime, absence, seasonality, multiple jobs and disruptions. Statistical definitions specify which hours are included or excluded.

Full-time and part-time status can be based on hours thresholds, employer classification or a person's own description. These definitions are not automatically comparable across surveys or countries.

For productivity and labour-cost analysis, hours worked may be more informative than headcount. For labour-market inclusion, employment-to-population ratios may be useful. For time-related underemployment, the gap between desired and actual working hours matters. The correct measure depends on the question.

## 8. Wages, earnings, compensation and labour income

**Wage or salary** commonly refers to payment received by an employee for work, with definitions varying by whether the measure is hourly, daily, monthly or annual and whether it is gross or net.

**Earnings** may include wages and salaries plus specified additional payments, such as overtime, bonuses or commissions, depending on the source definition.

**Compensation of employees** in national accounts is broader than cash wages alone. It generally includes wages and salaries plus employers' social contributions, actual or imputed, under the applicable framework.

**Labour income** is a broader phrase whose meaning depends on the analysis. It may refer to compensation of employees or include labour earnings from self-employment under an explicitly stated method. For self-employed people, labour and returns to capital can be difficult to separate because the same person may supply both.

**Gross earnings** are measured before specified deductions; **net take-home pay** is after the deductions included in the definition. Employer cost, gross pay and net pay are not interchangeable.

A rise in nominal wages does not automatically mean purchasing power rose. Compare earnings with an appropriate cost-of-living or price measure and consider taxes, hours worked, benefits and household circumstances. An average wage can rise while many workers' wages stagnate if the composition of employment changes.

## 9. Nominal pay, real pay and purchasing power

**Nominal earnings** are expressed in the money units paid during a period. **Real earnings** adjust nominal earnings using a stated price index to approximate changes in purchasing power. The chosen index, household basket, geography and time period affect the result.

If nominal pay rises 8% while the relevant price index rises 12%, the real change is approximately 1.08/1.12 − 1, or about −3.6%. This is an illustrative calculation, not a current wage or inflation estimate for Nigeria.

A single consumer-price index may not reflect every household's experience. Households differ in the share of spending devoted to food, transport, rent, education, health and other categories. Price changes can also vary by location and access to services.

To assess whether households are better off, nominal pay alone is insufficient. Relevant considerations include real earnings, hours worked, other household income, taxes and transfers, debt payments, housing costs, household size and access to public or in-kind services.

## 10. Household income and disposable income

Households can receive resources from multiple sources:
- wages and salaries;
- self-employment and business income;
- property income, such as interest, dividends or rent, under the relevant definitions;
- pensions and social benefits;
- remittances and transfers from other households;
- public transfers and in-kind services, where measured;
- withdrawals or asset sales, which may provide cash but are not necessarily income in the national-accounting sense.

**Household income** must be defined by the survey or accounting framework. **Disposable income** generally refers to income available after current taxes and social contributions and after adding relevant current transfers, under the chosen definition. It is not simply cash in a bank account.

A loan can increase cash available today but also creates a liability; it is not normally treated as income. Selling an existing asset can provide cash without representing newly generated income. A gift or remittance may be a transfer rather than payment for current production. Accounting classifications matter because the source of cash affects sustainability and net worth.

Household totals can hide distribution within the household. Per-capita measures divide by household members, while equivalence scales adjust for household size and composition using a stated method. Neither fully captures differences in needs, care burdens or control over resources.

## 11. Consumption, saving, assets and debt at household level

**Consumption expenditure** is spending on goods and services for current use under a specified definition. It differs from total cash outflow: purchasing a financial asset, repaying loan principal or buying an asset for long-term use is not necessarily household consumption in national-accounting or survey classifications.

**Household saving** is the part of defined disposable income not used for consumption over a period, subject to the accounting framework. It is a flow. **Savings** in everyday speech can refer to accumulated cash or financial balances, which are stocks. The distinction mirrors the earlier Workstream A note on money, wealth and capital.

Households may use saving to build emergency reserves, purchase assets, finance education, make a down payment or reduce debt. Borrowing can support consumption or investment, but it also creates future payment obligations. A household with assets can still face liquidity stress if those assets are difficult to sell or income arrives after bills are due.

Household resilience depends not only on income but also on income stability, liquid assets, debt-service burdens, access to credit, insurance, health, housing, care needs and exposure to shocks. A high-income household with large fixed obligations may be financially fragile; a lower-income household with stable costs and support may be more resilient, although limited resources remain a constraint.

## 12. Poverty, deprivation and living standards

**Poverty** can be measured using monetary thresholds, consumption or income distributions, material deprivation, multidimensional indicators or context-specific definitions. A poverty rate is meaningful only when the threshold, unit of analysis, price adjustment, reference period and population coverage are known.

**Absolute poverty** usually refers to a threshold intended to represent a minimum standard of resources, while **relative poverty** relates to a position within a society's income or consumption distribution. Specific methods vary by country and institution. A poverty line is an operational statistical threshold, not a complete description of hardship.

**Material deprivation** concerns inability to afford specified goods, services or living conditions. **Multidimensional poverty** combines defined indicators across dimensions such as education, health and living standards. These approaches answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Living standards** include material resources and access to goods and services; broader well-being can also involve health, safety, time, social relationships, environment and agency. GDP growth, average income or a national poverty rate cannot reveal every household's experience.

Poverty comparisons require consistent thresholds and price adjustments. Changes in survey design, imputation, household composition, geographic coverage or the definition of welfare aggregate can affect measured rates.

## 13. Household inequality and distribution

Averages do not reveal how resources are distributed. The **median** is the middle observation in an ordered distribution; the **mean** is the arithmetic average. In a skewed income distribution, a relatively small number of high incomes can pull the mean above the median.

Inequality measures such as the Gini coefficient summarize aspects of a distribution, but no single measure describes every feature. Percentile ratios, poverty rates, wealth distribution and measures of access to services may add context.

Income inequality and wealth inequality differ. Income is a flow over a period; wealth is a stock of assets less liabilities at a date. A household may have high wealth but low current income, or high current income but little accumulated wealth. Asset ownership, debt, housing costs, inheritance, business ownership and pension entitlements can shape long-run economic security.

Distributional analysis should consider household size, age, location, gendered care responsibilities, disability, employment status and other relevant characteristics, while protecting privacy and avoiding unsupported assumptions about individuals.

## 14. Labour productivity and pay: connected but not identical

**Labour productivity** measures output relative to labour input, often output per worker or per hour. It is not a direct measure of an individual's effort, and it is affected by capital, technology, organization, skills, demand, infrastructure and measurement.

**Unit labour cost** broadly relates labour compensation to output, often expressed per unit of real output. Its exact definition depends on whether compensation and output are measured per worker, per hour or at aggregate level. It is not the same as a worker's wage.

Productivity growth can create room for higher real pay, lower prices, higher profits, improved quality or shorter working time, but the distribution depends on competition, bargaining, institutions, market power, demand and the cost of other inputs. A rise in productivity does not mechanically guarantee that wages rise at the same rate.

Comparisons require consistent output measures, hours, worker composition, price adjustment and treatment of self-employment. Changes in average productivity can reflect the movement of workers between sectors or changes in hours rather than improvements within every workplace.

## 15. Unpaid care and other work outside headline employment measures

Unpaid care and household services support workers, children, older people and communities. Much of this work is not counted as employment or production in headline labour measures and core GDP, although satellite accounts and time-use statistics can measure it more directly.

Excluding an activity from a headline indicator is a boundary choice, not a claim that it has no value. Changes in care responsibilities can affect labour-force participation, hours available for paid work, education, health and household well-being.

If unpaid care is shifted to a paid provider, measured market production may increase even when the underlying service changes less than the GDP figures suggest. Conversely, reduced market purchases can lower measured production while unpaid household work rises. Interpreting such changes requires looking beyond one aggregate.

## 16. Informality, security and social protection

**Informal employment** and **employment in the informal sector** are related but distinct statistical concepts. The first generally focuses on characteristics of jobs and employment relationships; the second focuses on characteristics of production units. A person working in a registered business may still have an informal job under certain definitions, while a unit classified as informal may engage different types of workers.

Informality can be associated with gaps in registration, tax coverage, social insurance, written contracts, workplace protections or access to formal finance, but these characteristics vary. Do not infer an individual's legal status or working conditions from the label alone.

Social protection, pension coverage, paid leave, occupational safety, predictable income and access to dispute resolution affect the risks workers and households bear. Employment counts do not capture all of these dimensions.

Because informal activity can be difficult to observe, surveys and administrative records may cover different populations. Comparisons should note the source and definition rather than assuming all informal work is missing from statistics.

## 17. Practical examples

### A self-employed shopkeeper
A shopkeeper may be classified as employed under the relevant framework even if daily sales are volatile. Sales revenue is not personal income: stock replacement, rent, transport, losses, debt payments and other business costs must be considered. Cash in the till is not the same as profit or household disposable income.

### A worker with reduced hours
A worker who still has a job but receives fewer hours may remain employed while experiencing lower earnings or time-related underemployment. The unemployment rate alone may not capture the deterioration. Hours, earnings and underemployment measures add information.

### A jobseeker who stops searching
A person who wants work but stops active search may not meet the statistical unemployment criteria in a given framework. A lower unemployment rate alongside falling participation and a weak employment-to-population ratio would require careful interpretation rather than an automatic conclusion that the labour market improved.

### A household receiving remittances
A household may have resources from employment, self-employment and remittances. These flows have different origins and implications. A remittance can support consumption and resilience, but it is not necessarily income generated by domestic production during the same period.

### A productive business with stagnant pay
A firm's output per hour can rise without an equal increase in wages if prices, capital costs, market power, bargaining, worker composition or profit distribution change. Productivity data alone do not explain how gains are divided.

These examples are illustrative and do not assert current Nigerian labour-market or household conditions.

## 18. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Work vs employment | Work is broader; employment is a status under a defined statistical framework. |
| Working-age population vs labour force | The labour force is a subset of the population defined by employment/unemployment criteria. |
| Labour force vs employed population | The labour force includes employed and unemployed people under the relevant standard. |
| Unemployment vs underemployment | Unemployment concerns not having employment and meeting specified criteria; underemployment can affect people already employed. |
| Unemployment rate vs employment-to-population ratio | Their denominators and questions differ. |
| Unemployment rate vs participation rate | One measures unemployment within the labour force; the other measures labour-force participation in a population. |
| Employment vs job quality | Employment status does not establish adequate pay, hours, safety or security. |
| Headcount vs hours worked | The number of workers does not equal the volume of labour input. |
| Wage vs earnings vs compensation | Definitions differ in included payments and employer contributions. |
| Nominal vs real earnings | Real earnings adjust for a stated price measure. |
| Gross pay vs net pay | Gross pay is before specified deductions; net pay is after them. |
| Household income vs disposable income | Disposable income accounts for defined taxes, contributions and current transfers. |
| Income vs cash receipts | Loans and asset sales can generate cash without being income in the same sense. |
| Consumption vs all spending | Asset purchases and debt-principal repayments are not necessarily consumption. |
| Saving flow vs accumulated savings | Saving is a flow; accumulated balances are stocks. |
| Income inequality vs wealth inequality | Income is a flow; wealth is a stock of net assets. |
| Mean vs median income | The mean can be pulled upward by high values; the median describes the middle observation. |
| Informal employment vs informal-sector employment | One concerns jobs; the other concerns production units. |
| Productivity vs effort | Productivity is output relative to measured inputs, not a direct measure of effort. |
| Productivity growth vs wage growth | Productivity gains do not mechanically determine how gains are distributed. |
| Poverty rate vs overall welfare | Poverty measures use a defined threshold or multidimensional method and do not capture all well-being. |

## 19. Measurement and evidence checklist

When interpreting labour or household claims, ask:

1. Which labour-force framework, age range and reference period are used?
2. Is the figure about work, employment, unemployment, underemployment, participation or labour underutilization?
3. What are the numerator and denominator? Are the rates comparable across releases?
4. Are actual hours, usual hours, paid hours or contracted hours being measured?
5. Does employment include self-employment and other categories under the stated definition?
6. Is the job formal or informal under a specified statistical concept, and is the classification about the job or the enterprise?
7. Does pay mean hourly wage, monthly salary, total earnings, compensation or net take-home pay?
8. Is pay nominal or real? Which price index, location and period are used?
9. Does household income include self-employment, property income, pensions, transfers and remittances? Which deductions define disposable income?
10. Is the statistic a household mean, median, per-capita amount or equivalized measure?
11. Is poverty measured using income, consumption, a monetary line or multidimensional indicators?
12. Are survey design, sampling error, non-response, imputation, coverage and revisions documented?
13. Are changes in rates consistent with changes in participation, employment-to-population ratios, hours and earnings?
14. Does the evidence describe a measured association or establish a cause?
15. Are complementary indicators needed to assess distribution, job quality, household resilience or well-being?

## 21. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Employment and unemployment are statistical statuses governed by explicit criteria and reference periods.
- The unemployment rate, participation rate and employment-to-population ratio answer different questions.
- Employment status does not fully describe job quality, working hours, security or earnings.
- Nominal pay and real purchasing power are different.
- Household income, disposable income, consumption, saving, cash receipts, assets and debt are distinct concepts.
- Income and wealth are different flow and stock measures.
- GDP and labour productivity do not by themselves explain the distribution of gains or household welfare.
- Informal employment and employment in the informal sector are distinct concepts under international statistical frameworks.

### Scope limits
- Definitions and implementation depend on the applicable international standards and national statistical methods.
- This note does not state a current Nigerian unemployment rate, wage level, poverty rate or household-income estimate.
- Illustrative examples are not empirical findings.
- The note is conceptual and does not replace formal indicator specifications or legal definitions.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which labour-force standards and definitions does Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics currently apply to each published series?
- How have Nigerian labour-force definitions, survey methods and release series changed over time?
- Which official series cover unemployment, underemployment, participation, hours, informal employment and labour income?
- Which data sources best measure household income, consumption, poverty and inequality, and how are price adjustments handled?
- What revisions, sampling limitations, geographic gaps and comparability breaks should analysts record?
- Which local data can distinguish national trends from the conditions of a specific town, industry or household group?

## 22. Short summary

Labour and household economics ask not only whether an economy produces more, but who works, how much work is available, what people receive, and how households use and sustain their resources. Employment, unemployment, underemployment, participation and hours are different measures. Wages, compensation, household disposable income, consumption, saving, wealth and debt must also be separated. Nominal earnings are not the same as real purchasing power, and productivity growth does not automatically determine wages or welfare. All comparisons require explicit definitions, denominators, periods and attention to distribution.

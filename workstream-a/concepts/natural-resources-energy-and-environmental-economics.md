## 1. Starting point: the economy operates within physical systems

Economic activity depends on materials, land, water, energy and functioning ecosystems. These are not merely inputs that can always be replaced at a fixed price. Some resources regenerate under particular conditions; others are depleted when extracted; some environmental functions are difficult or impossible to replace once damaged. Production also creates residuals—waste, pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions—that may impose costs on people who did not choose or pay for the activity.

A reality-first investigation asks:
- What physical resource or environmental service is involved?
- Is it renewable, non-renewable, slowly replenished or effectively non-substitutable at the relevant scale?
- Who owns, controls, accesses, extracts, transforms, transports and benefits from it?
- What energy, infrastructure, labour, capital and knowledge are required to use it?
- What costs are paid by the producer, and what costs are shifted to other people, places or future periods?
- How are resource stocks, extraction flows, prices, rents, royalties, subsidies and environmental conditions measured?
- Can present production continue without undermining the resource base or ecological functions on which future production depends?

**Core principle:** natural-resource availability, commercial profitability, measured economic output and social or ecological value are different dimensions. One cannot substitute for the others.

## 2. Natural resources, natural capital and ecosystem services

A **natural resource** is a component of the natural world that people use or value, such as minerals, petroleum, sunlight, wind, freshwater, timber, fish, fertile soil or land. A resource may be used as a physical input, provide an energy flow, support a livelihood or supply an environmental service.

**Natural capital** is a framework for describing stocks of natural assets that provide benefits over time. It may include ecosystems, soil, forests, water systems and mineral deposits. The concept is useful but does not imply that every ecological value can be measured adequately in money or that natural systems are interchangeable with produced or financial capital.

**Ecosystem services** are benefits people obtain from ecosystems, such as water filtration, flood regulation, pollination, soil formation, fisheries, carbon storage and recreation. These services can support production without appearing as a direct market transaction. Their loss may create costs elsewhere in the economy.

Distinguish:
- **Stock:** the quantity or condition of a resource at a specified time.
- **Flow:** extraction, regeneration, replenishment, energy generation or service provision over a period.
- **Service:** a function or benefit provided by a resource or ecosystem.
- **Asset value:** an estimate under a specified valuation method and boundary.
- **Economic rent:** a return above the amount required to keep a resource or factor in its relevant use, under a particular framework.
- **Market price:** the observed or negotiated price for a defined commodity or service.
- **Social value:** a broader assessment of benefits and costs to affected people, which may not be captured by market prices.

These terms overlap but are not synonyms. A forest can be timber inventory, a carbon store, a source of watershed protection, a livelihood base and a cultural asset at the same time. Logging revenue measures only part of that bundle of values and costs.

## 3. Renewable, non-renewable and conditionally renewable resources

A **non-renewable resource** is replenished so slowly relative to human time horizons that extraction reduces the remaining stock—for example, a mineral deposit or fossil fuel reserve. Recycling may extend the useful service obtained from extracted material, but it does not make the original geological stock renewable on ordinary economic time scales.

A **renewable resource** can regenerate or be replenished, but use can still exceed the rate of regeneration. Fish stocks, forests, soil fertility and groundwater systems may be renewable or replenishable only under specific ecological and management conditions. Overuse can degrade the resource or cause a collapse in productive capacity.

Solar and wind are often described as renewable energy sources because the relevant energy flows are replenished on human time scales. Their practical output nevertheless depends on location, weather, equipment, grid connections, storage, land use, materials and system management.

The important question is not only whether a resource is renewable. It is how the stock or flow behaves under actual extraction and use.

A simplified resource-accounting relationship is:

**Ending stock = beginning stock + additions − extraction − losses.**

In plain language, a resource stock grows when additions exceed what is removed or lost, and declines when extraction and losses exceed additions. For a fishery, additions may include reproduction and migration; for a mineral deposit, additions may reflect discoveries or revised geological estimates rather than new physical formation. Real accounts may also require reclassification, reappraisal and other adjustments.

## 4. Resources, reserves, extraction and depletion

In extractive industries, **resources** and **reserves** are technical categories, not interchangeable descriptions of everything underground. Definitions vary by commodity and reporting code, but reserves generally refer to a subset considered recoverable under specified technical, economic, legal and operating conditions at a particular time. A discovery does not automatically become a commercially recoverable reserve.

A deposit may be physically present but uneconomic to extract at prevailing prices, inaccessible with current technology, restricted by law, lacking infrastructure, or unacceptable under environmental and social constraints. Higher prices or new technology can change reserve estimates without changing the underlying geology.

Distinguish:
- Geological occurrence from a measured resource estimate.
- Resource estimate from a reserve classification.
- Reserve classification from a development decision.
- Announced project from constructed and commissioned capacity.
- Installed capacity from actual production.
- Production from exports.
- Export receipts from public revenue.
- Public revenue from public spending.
- Spending from effective service or development outcomes.

**Discovery → appraisal → reserve classification → investment decision → construction → production → sale → revenue and distribution.**

In plain language, finding a deposit is only an early step. The resource must be evaluated, shown to be recoverable under defined conditions, financed, permitted, developed and operated. Even after production begins, transport, contracts, prices, taxes, royalties, ownership and costs determine who receives the value. Each stage needs its own evidence; an announcement at one stage is not proof of completion at the next.

Depletion should be considered in physical terms and, where appropriate, economic terms. Extraction may reduce the remaining stock, increase extraction costs, damage ecosystems or create future liabilities. Commodity revenue is not automatically a measure of net national wealth created.

## 5. Energy: source, conversion, delivery and useful service

Energy is an enabling input to nearly every modern production and household system. However, energy supply contains several distinct stages:

1. **Primary energy source:** energy in a natural form, such as crude oil, natural gas, sunlight, wind, uranium or flowing water.
2. **Conversion:** transforming a source into a more usable form, such as generating electricity from gas or solar energy.
3. **Transmission and distribution:** moving electricity or fuels to users.
4. **Storage and balancing:** matching supply and demand over time and maintaining system reliability.
5. **End-use equipment:** motors, pumps, refrigeration, cookstoves, vehicles, industrial processes and appliances.
6. **Useful energy service:** lighting, cooling, mobility, heat, computing, pumping or mechanical work.

A system may have substantial energy resources but still deliver unreliable or unaffordable service if generation, grid capacity, fuel logistics, maintenance, payment arrangements or end-use equipment are inadequate.

**Energy source → conversion → networks and storage → end-use equipment → useful service.**

In plain language, having fuel underground or installing a power plant does not mean households and businesses receive dependable electricity. Energy must be converted, moved, balanced and delivered through equipment that works. Reliability and affordability depend on the entire chain, not only on the source or installed generating capacity.

Important distinctions:
- **Capacity versus generation:** capacity is a rate of potential output under specified conditions; generation is the energy actually produced over a period.
- **Generation versus delivered electricity:** transmission losses, outages, curtailment and network constraints separate production from what reaches users.
- **Access versus reliability:** a connection does not guarantee continuous service.
- **Reliability versus affordability:** service may be dependable but too costly, or affordable but unreliable.
- **Energy versus power:** energy is an amount over time; power is a rate of energy transfer or use.
- **Energy efficiency versus conservation:** efficiency provides a given service with less energy; conservation reduces use, potentially by changing activity or demand.
- **Electrification versus decarbonization:** replacing direct fuel use with electricity is not automatically low-carbon if the electricity supply is emissions-intensive.
- **Installed renewable capacity versus dependable supply:** variable generation requires system integration, transmission, flexibility, storage or other balancing arrangements.

Energy productivity also depends on how energy is used. A factory can consume more electricity because it produces more, because equipment is inefficient, because processes change or because energy-intensive production shifts into the measured boundary. Energy consumption alone does not reveal productive performance.

## 6. Resource rents, royalties, taxes and ownership

Natural resources can generate **economic rents** because access to a scarce, high-quality or strategically located resource may produce returns above the relevant costs and required returns. The calculation depends on the resource, prices, extraction costs, risk, capital costs, timing and definition used.

A **royalty** is generally a payment associated with extracting or producing a resource, often calculated by volume, value or another contractual basis. A **tax** is a compulsory public levy under law. A **dividend** is a distribution to an owner. A **production-sharing entitlement** or other contractual allocation can determine which parties receive produced quantities or sale proceeds. The applicable law and contract must be examined rather than inferring payment rights from labels alone.

A resource's gross export value is not the same as the income retained domestically or received by the government.

**Gross sales value → operating and transport costs → contractual claims and taxes/royalties → owner and investor returns → public revenue and private income.**

In plain language, the value of exported oil, gas or minerals is a starting figure. Producing and delivering it costs money, and contracts allocate the remaining value among operators, investors, governments and other parties. Government revenue then depends on the fiscal regime, collection, timing, compliance and prices. Gross export figures alone cannot establish public receipts or how much households benefit.

For analysis, distinguish the roles of legal resource owner, licence holder, operator, technical contractor, financier, trader, exporter, transport intermediary, tax or royalty recipient, beneficial owner, local community, worker, affected household, regulator and public authority. The same actor may hold multiple roles, but that must be established rather than assumed. A company operating a field does not necessarily own all the resource, finance all operations or receive all proceeds.

## 7. Commodity prices and the wider economy

Commodity prices can affect economies through export income, import bills, government budgets, exchange rates, domestic energy costs, input prices, inflation, investment and expectations. Effects vary according to whether a country is a net exporter or importer, its contracts, price pass-through, fiscal rules, currency regime, market structure and ability to adjust.

For a resource exporter, a price increase may improve export receipts and public revenue, but the gain depends on export volume, contract terms, costs, production disruptions, tax design and how quickly prices pass through to receipts. For an importer, higher prices may raise the cost of fuel, transport, food production and electricity, worsening external balances or household purchasing power.

**Commodity-price shock → export/import receipts and production costs → fiscal and foreign-exchange effects → domestic prices, investment and spending → wider output and distributional effects.**

In plain language, a global price change can move through several connected channels. Exporters may receive more foreign currency, while importers may have to spend more of it. Governments and businesses then adjust spending, investment and prices. The final effect is not mechanically determined: buffers, subsidies, contracts, exchange-rate movements and policy responses can amplify or soften it.

A commodity boom can create concentration and volatility. Resource revenue may rise rapidly during a price upswing and fall sharply during a downturn, while public spending, debt commitments and expectations adjust more slowly. Budgeting based on temporary peak prices can create future fiscal stress.

## 8. Resource dependence, diversification and Dutch disease

Resource dependence describes a situation in which a significant share of exports, public revenue, production or employment relies on a limited set of natural resources. It is not identical to resource abundance. A country may possess large deposits but produce little; another may depend heavily on resource exports even if reserves are limited.

**Dutch disease** is a framework for examining how a resource boom can affect other tradable sectors. A boom may increase demand and income, raise domestic costs or the real exchange rate, and draw labour and capital toward the booming resource sector and non-tradable activities. Other tradable sectors may become less competitive. The outcome depends on how revenue is spent, labour and capital mobility, exchange-rate arrangements, productivity, trade exposure and policy responses. It is a possible mechanism, not an automatic consequence of every resource discovery.

Diversification can mean broadening exports, production, public revenue or employment. These are different targets. A country can diversify its export products but remain dependent on a single tax base; government revenue can diversify while employment remains concentrated; domestic manufacturing can expand while still relying on imported energy or intermediate goods.

Assess diversification using multiple indicators and exposure measures, including concentration, domestic value added, employment, productivity, fiscal sources, import dependence and sensitivity to price shocks. A larger number of product codes alone does not establish resilient capability.

## 9. Environmental externalities and social costs

An **externality** occurs when an activity affects people or entities outside the transaction and that effect is not fully reflected in the relevant decision-maker's costs or benefits. Pollution, greenhouse-gas emissions, noise, congestion and ecosystem damage can create negative externalities. Knowledge spillovers or some ecosystem benefits can create positive externalities.

A simplified relationship is:

**Social cost = private cost + external cost.**

In plain language, a firm may pay for labour, fuel, equipment and transport while some pollution-related health costs are borne by nearby households or future generations. The firm's accounting cost may therefore be lower than the total cost to society. Estimating the gap requires evidence about exposure, physical effects, affected populations, time horizons and valuation assumptions. Some environmental effects are uncertain, non-market, distributionally unequal or not credibly reducible to one monetary number.

Environmental damage may also create direct business costs through worker health, water scarcity, crop loss, infrastructure damage, insurance, litigation, regulation, supply interruption or reduced asset value. External costs can return to firms and governments over time.

## 10. Environmental policy instruments and their limits

Policy responses can include standards, permits, taxes, royalties, emissions trading, subsidy reform, protected areas, disclosure requirements, liability rules, public investment, resource rights, monitoring and enforcement. Instruments have different information requirements and distributional effects.

- **Environmental tax or charge:** places a price on a defined activity or pollutant. Effectiveness depends on rate, coverage, measurement, enforcement and available alternatives.
- **Cap-and-trade or permit system:** constrains a defined total and allows permits to be traded. Results depend on the cap, allocation, monitoring, market design and enforcement.
- **Performance standard:** requires a specified technology, process or outcome. It can be direct but less flexible across firms with different abatement costs.
- **Subsidy:** can support desired investment or reduce access barriers, but can also encourage overuse, create fiscal costs or benefit groups that do not need support.
- **Protected area or extraction restriction:** limits specified uses in a defined area; effectiveness depends on boundaries, rights, alternatives, monitoring and compliance.
- **Disclosure and verification:** can improve information, but reporting is not the same as physical performance or enforcement.
- **Restoration or compensation:** may address some damage, but not all ecological functions are substitutable or recoverable.

No instrument works solely because it exists in law. Impact depends on institutional capacity, incentives, monitoring, compliance, political economy, distribution and alternative livelihoods. A policy can reduce one harm while shifting activity elsewhere, creating rebound effects or imposing costs disproportionately on low-income households.

## 11. Energy transition, stranded assets and transition risk

An **energy transition** is a change in the mix of energy sources, technologies, infrastructure, uses and institutions. It can be driven by technology, costs, security concerns, climate policy, resource availability, consumer demand or geopolitical conditions. Transitions are not simply replacements of one fuel with another; they involve infrastructure, skills, financing, supply chains, grids, storage, industrial processes and household equipment.

**Transition risk** refers to financial, economic or operational risks arising from changes in policy, technology, market preferences or legal expectations associated with a transition. **Physical risk** arises from direct impacts of environmental and climate conditions, including acute events and chronic changes. These risks may interact.

A **stranded asset** is an asset that loses economic value or becomes underused earlier than expected because of changing market, policy, technological, environmental or physical conditions. The label requires a defined asset, valuation basis, expected life and cause; it should not be applied merely because an asset is old or associated with a particular technology.

Transition costs and benefits are distributed unevenly. Workers, regions, consumers, utilities, fuel producers, manufacturers, lenders and governments may face different adjustment pressures. Analysis should consider reliability, affordability, energy access, industrial capability, employment, public revenue, environmental outcomes and resilience together rather than optimizing one metric in isolation.

## 12. Measurement: physical accounts, money values and environmental indicators

Resource and environmental analysis requires more than one measurement system.

**Physical measures** include tonnes extracted, barrels produced, cubic metres of water used, hectares of forest cover, megawatt-hours generated, capacity in megawatts, emissions in tonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent, and pollutant concentrations. Definitions, boundaries, time periods and methods must be specified.

**Economic measures** include market prices, operating costs, royalties, taxes, export receipts, capital expenditure, resource rents and estimates of damages. These depend on prices, contracts, exchange rates, valuation methods and accounting boundaries.

**Distributional and exposure measures** include household energy spending, outage frequency, exposure to pollution, employment dependence on a resource sector, regional revenue dependence and access to water or clean cooking. National averages can conceal large differences by location, income, occupation or vulnerability.

The UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) provides internationally agreed frameworks for organizing information about the environment and its relationship to the economy. Physical flow accounts and monetary accounts answer different questions; monetary valuation is not a complete substitute for physical ecological indicators.

Do not combine physical quantities, monetary values and indices into one score without explicit definitions, normalization, weights and a defensible interpretation. An increase in monetary resource value can reflect price changes even when physical stocks or ecological condition deteriorate.

## 13. Evidence chain: from resource presence to social outcome

A defensible investigation separates at least these claims:

1. A resource or ecosystem exists at a defined location.
2. Its quantity, condition or potential has been measured using a specified method.
3. Access or extraction rights are held by a particular actor.
4. Infrastructure and permits allow a specified activity.
5. Production or energy delivery actually occurred.
6. Sales, transport or exports occurred at a specified volume and time.
7. A contract or fiscal rule determines claims on the value.
8. A payment, tax or royalty was actually received by the relevant party.
9. Public spending, private investment or household income followed.
10. The claimed economic, social or environmental outcome occurred.
11. The observed outcome can be attributed to the activity rather than merely correlated with it.

Each step needs evidence at the right resolution. Satellite imagery may show a facility or land-cover change but not by itself prove ownership, output volume or revenue. A company announcement may indicate a planned investment but not completed construction. Export data may show shipment values but not net public receipts. Budget allocation does not prove payment or effective service delivery. A reported environmental policy does not establish compliance or ecological recovery.

Useful source families include geological surveys and resource reports; energy regulators and system operators; metered generation and delivery data; customs and trade statistics; company filings and audited statements; licence and contract records; public budgets and revenue reports; environmental monitoring; satellite imagery; household and labour surveys; and independent research. Source quality, incentives, revision practices, spatial coverage and definitions must be assessed for each claim.

## 14. Worked example: a resource discovery

Imagine a fictional region announces a large mineral discovery. The announcement may be geologically significant, but several questions remain open:
- Is the estimate independently verified, and what confidence category applies?
- Is the mineral recoverable using available technology?
- What are the extraction, processing, energy, water, transport and restoration costs?
- What rights, permits and community agreements apply?
- Is infrastructure available, and who pays to build it?
- Has financing closed, or has only an intention been announced?
- What share of the value is expected to go to workers, suppliers, owners, lenders and government?
- What water, waste, land and health impacts may occur?
- What local capabilities could be developed, and what inputs may still be imported?
- How sensitive is the project to global prices, exchange rates, taxes and delays?
- What would happen to public revenue and local employment if the project closed early?

The discovery alone proves none of the downstream claims. It is a reason to investigate them, not a substitute for the evidence.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Natural resource vs natural capital | A resource is a natural component used or valued; natural capital frames stocks of natural assets that provide benefits over time. |
| Resource stock vs resource flow | A stock is measured at a point in time; a flow is measured over a period. |
| Resource vs reserve | Resource estimates and reserve classifications use different technical and economic criteria; definitions depend on the reporting framework. |
| Resource abundance vs resource dependence | Physical endowment differs from how much production, exports, employment or public revenue depends on the resource. |
| Renewable vs inexhaustible | Renewable resources regenerate under conditions; overuse can still degrade or deplete them. |
| Installed capacity vs actual generation | Capacity is potential output rate; generation is energy actually produced over time. |
| Generation vs delivered supply | Network constraints, losses and outages can separate production from end-user service. |
| Energy vs power | Energy is an amount; power is a rate. |
| Energy access vs energy reliability | Having a connection differs from receiving dependable service. |
| Energy efficiency vs energy conservation | Efficiency changes energy required per service; conservation reduces use or demand, possibly by changing activity. |
| Gross export value vs government revenue | Export value is not the same as fiscal receipts after costs, contracts, taxes, ownership and timing. |
| Revenue vs resource rent | Revenue is a receipt measure; rent is a return above specified required costs and returns. |
| Royalty vs tax vs dividend | These are distinct contractual, fiscal and ownership-based payment types. |
| Extraction vs production capacity | Capacity is potential; actual extraction or production is observed flow. |
| Pollution vs externality | Pollution is a physical release or condition; externality describes an effect not fully reflected in the decision-maker's incentives or costs. |
| Private cost vs social cost | Private cost borne by the decision-maker can exclude costs imposed on others. |
| Environmental price vs environmental value | A price is observed or administratively set; value may include non-market benefits and harms. |
| Mitigation vs adaptation | Mitigation reduces causes of climate change, especially emissions; adaptation adjusts to actual or expected impacts. |
| Physical risk vs transition risk | Physical risk arises from environmental impacts; transition risk arises from policy, technology, market and legal changes. |
| Energy transition vs simple fuel substitution | Transition involves systems, infrastructure, institutions, supply chains, workers and end uses, not only the generation mix. |
| Stranded asset vs depreciated asset | Depreciation is an accounting allocation or value change; stranding refers to unexpected underuse or loss of economic value before anticipated life ends. |
| Resource discovery vs commercial project | A discovery does not prove recoverability, financing, permits, construction, production or profitability. |
| Resource extraction vs sustainable development | Extraction can generate current income while reducing stocks or creating costs that threaten future welfare. |
| Carbon emissions vs all environmental impacts | Carbon metrics capture a specific dimension and do not summarize water, biodiversity, toxicity, land or local pollution. |
| Reporting vs verification | A published claim is evidence that someone reported it; independent verification tests whether the underlying claim is supported. |

## 17. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual points
- Resource stocks, extraction flows, energy services and environmental conditions are distinct objects.
- Commercial reserves and project viability depend on defined technical, economic, legal and operating assumptions.
- Resource value is distributed through ownership, costs, contracts, taxes, royalties, financing and timing.
- Commodity-price changes can affect trade, public finances, exchange rates, costs and household purchasing power through multiple channels.
- Energy service depends on conversion, networks, balancing, end-use equipment and reliability—not only on resource availability or installed capacity.
- Externalities arise when effects on others are not fully reflected in relevant decisions and costs.
- Physical environmental indicators and monetary valuations answer different questions.
- Announcements, installed assets, actual production, receipts, spending and outcomes require separate evidence.

### Scope limits
- This note does not estimate current Nigerian resource reserves, energy shortages, emissions, resource rents or environmental damage.
- It does not assign monetary values to ecosystems or assert that all environmental effects can be monetized.
- It does not recommend a specific energy mix, tax rate, extraction policy or climate instrument.
- Commodity and project examples are conceptual; no actual project outcome is inferred.
- Technical classifications and country-specific rules must be verified against the relevant official standard, regulator, contract or data source before use.

### Questions for follow-up
- Which Nigerian data sources allow resource production to be reconciled with exports, fiscal receipts and public spending?
- How should uncertainty in resource estimates and reserve classifications be represented?
- What data are available for actual versus installed electricity generation, delivered supply, outage frequency and cost?
- How can resource-dependent regions track diversification, local value added and household exposure to price shocks?
- Which physical and monetary indicators can jointly describe depletion without pretending that one fully captures the other?
- How should ecological thresholds and irreversible losses be represented when monetary estimates are weak?
- What evidence is needed to distinguish formal environmental regulation from compliance and measured outcomes?
- Which energy, water, waste or exposure problems are sufficiently specific and costly to justify a practical intelligence service?

## 18. Short summary

Natural resources and ecosystems support production and livelihoods, but resource presence is not equivalent to commercially recoverable reserves, actual output, public revenue or development. Energy must be converted and delivered through functioning infrastructure to provide useful service. Resource rents and commodity income are allocated through costs, contracts, ownership and fiscal rules, while environmental externalities can shift costs onto others. Physical accounts, financial values and social outcomes should remain distinct and be connected with evidence at each stage. Lifeconomist should investigate the full chain from natural stock and infrastructure through actual activity, financial flows, environmental effects and distribution without treating announcements or plausible mechanisms as proof.

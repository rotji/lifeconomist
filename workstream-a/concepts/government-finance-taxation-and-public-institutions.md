# Concept 15 — Government Finance, Taxation and Public Institutions

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal fiscal indicators and Nigerian data selection remain in Workstream B  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 14 — Labour, Employment and Household Economics](labour-employment-and-household-economics.md)

## 1. Purpose

Government affects economic life through taxation, spending, transfers, regulation, public services, asset ownership, borrowing and guarantees. These activities interact but are not one undifferentiated pool of money. A budget is a plan and authorization framework; cash is a payment resource; expenditure is an accounting flow; debt is a liability stock; and a service outcome is a result that must be measured separately.

This note establishes conceptual distinctions. It does not state current Nigerian fiscal figures, assess a particular administration, or replace formal indicator definitions and source checks in Workstream B.

## 2. Government and the public sector

“Government” may refer to federal, state or local government, or a particular public entity. “Public sector” can be broader, potentially including general government and public corporations, depending on the statistical framework. A government-owned company may be classified separately from general government; ownership alone does not settle the statistical boundary.

Reports may cover the federal government alone, consolidated general government, budgetary central government, public enterprises or the wider public sector. These totals are not automatically comparable. When consolidating levels or entities, intergovernmental transfers and other internal flows may need to be eliminated to avoid double counting.

Always identify the institution, legal entity, level of government, accounting basis and consolidation boundary behind a number.

## 3. Public revenue, taxes and non-tax receipts

**Government revenue** is an aggregate defined by the relevant fiscal framework. It can include taxes, social contributions, grants and other revenue categories, depending on the boundary and definition.

**Tax revenue** consists of compulsory, unrequited payments classified as taxes under the relevant framework. Taxes may apply to income, profits, payroll, property, goods and services, trade or other bases.

**Non-tax revenue** may include fees, charges, royalties, dividends, interest receipts and other categories. Not every cash receipt is revenue: borrowing creates a liability, and selling a financial asset or collecting loan principal may be classified as a financing or balance-sheet transaction rather than revenue.

Distinguish **revenue assessed**, **revenue collected** and **cash received**. Amounts assessed may not be collected in full or in the same period. Exemptions, arrears, disputes, refunds, remissions, compliance and timing affect the relationship. Revenue can change because the tax base, prices, economic activity, enforcement, exchange rates or rules change—not only because tax rates change.

## 4. Tax liability and tax incidence

**Tax liability** is the amount legally due from a taxpayer. **Tax incidence** concerns who ultimately bears the economic burden after prices, wages, returns and behaviour adjust.

A tax legally remitted by a seller may be partly reflected in consumer prices, lower supplier payments, lower profits or some combination. The outcome depends on market conditions, elasticities, competition, bargaining power and time horizon. Legal responsibility for remitting a tax does not alone identify its final economic bearer.

Statutory, marginal, average and effective tax rates answer different questions. A stated rate alone does not establish the effective burden, distributional impact or revenue yield. Tax policy may pursue revenue, redistribution, stabilization, behaviour change or financing public goods; effects require evidence and context.

## 5. Government expenditure, purchases and transfers

Government expenditure may include current spending, capital spending, transfers, interest expense and other categories under the reporting framework.

- **Government final consumption** concerns goods and services treated as final consumption in national accounts.
- **Capital expenditure/public investment** refers to spending classified as acquiring or creating non-financial assets under a particular budget or accounting system.
- **Current expenditure** is spending classified as current under that system.
- **Transfers** redistribute resources without a direct exchange for an equivalent current good or service.
- **Interest expense** is the cost of borrowing and differs from repayment of principal.
- **Subsidies** are forms of support classified according to the instrument and reporting rules.

Budgetary categories and national-accounting categories may not align exactly. Transfers are not the same as direct government purchases of current output. A recipient may later spend a transfer, but that later transaction is recorded under its own accounting treatment.

Capital expenditure is not proof that an asset was completed, is operational, is maintained or has delivered public value. Current spending such as maintenance and staffing can be necessary for an existing asset to provide services.

## 6. Budget, appropriation, commitment and payment

A **budget** sets out planned revenue, spending and financing for a period. An **appropriation** or authorization grants legal authority to spend for specified purposes under the applicable system. Neither means the money has already been paid.

A spending process may include proposal, approval, release of spending authority or cash, commitment, contract, delivery, verification, recognition of an obligation, invoice processing, payment and outcome evaluation. The precise stages and terminology differ across systems.

A budget allocation, signed contract, certified obligation, cash payment, completed asset and functioning public service are different states. **Budget execution** concerns implementation of the approved budget. An execution rate is only interpretable if its numerator (commitments, obligations, payments or another measure) and denominator (original or revised budget) are specified.

A gap between appropriation and payment may reflect timing, procurement delays, cash constraints, capacity, project changes, savings, weak planning or other causes. Investigate before assigning blame.

## 7. Deficit, surplus and fiscal balance

A **fiscal deficit** generally occurs when defined expenditure exceeds defined revenue over a period; a **surplus** occurs when revenue exceeds expenditure under the same definition. Exact balances depend on institutional coverage, accounting basis and which items are included.

A deficit is a **flow** over a period. Public debt is a **stock** of liabilities at a point in time. A deficit often requires financing, but the reported deficit and change in debt can differ because of exchange-rate valuation, arrears, recognition of liabilities, financial-asset transactions, privatization receipts, reclassification and other stock-flow adjustments.

The **primary balance** usually excludes interest expense from the fiscal balance under the selected definition. It can help distinguish current fiscal operations before interest from legacy debt costs, but does not alone establish sustainability. A **structural** or **cyclically adjusted balance** estimates the fiscal position after adjusting for economic-cycle effects; it depends on models and assumptions and is not directly observed.

Deficits are not inherently proof of irresponsibility, nor are surpluses automatically proof of good policy. Purpose, economic context, financing terms, spending composition, distributional effects and medium-term sustainability matter.

## 8. Public debt, borrowing and debt service

**Public debt** is a stock of recognized liabilities for a defined public-sector boundary at a point in time. Reports may differ in whether they include direct government debt, guaranteed debt, public-sector debt, arrears or other liabilities. Coverage must be clear before comparing totals.

**Borrowing** is a financing flow that creates a liability. Debt stock changes reflect borrowing, repayment, valuation changes, recognition and other adjustments. **Principal** is the amount borrowed or outstanding; **interest** is the cost of borrowing. **Debt service** may mean interest alone in some reports or principal plus interest in others; verify the definition.

Domestic/external, local-currency/foreign-currency and resident/non-resident classifications are not always interchangeable. A foreign-currency obligation is not necessarily external under every statistical definition, and locally issued debt can be held by non-residents. Currency, creditor, maturity, interest-rate structure and governing terms matter.

Debt-to-GDP compares a debt stock with a production flow over a period. It is useful as a scale indicator but does not by itself measure immediate cash availability, affordability, repayment capacity or default probability. Debt dynamics depend on interest rates, growth, the primary balance, exchange rates, maturity and contingent liabilities.

## 9. Financing, borrowing and money creation

**Financing** describes how a deficit or transaction is funded—for example, issuing debt, drawing down deposits, selling assets or using other financial operations. Financing is not the same as revenue. A bond sale generally creates a liability rather than tax or operating revenue.

Government financing interacts with banks, investors, interest rates, liquidity and monetary policy. Commercial-bank deposits, central-bank reserves, government deposits and Treasury securities are different balance-sheet items. The effects of public spending and borrowing depend on institutional arrangements, central-bank operations, bank responses, investor demand and the state of the economy.

Do not reduce the relationship to “government simply prints money,” nor assume borrowing has no monetary consequences. Central-bank financing, where legally permitted and used, differs from market borrowing and can have distinct inflation, exchange-rate and governance implications. These effects must be investigated in context.

## 10. Cash position, liquidity and arrears

A government may face short-term cash pressure even if its longer-run fiscal position appears sustainable. **Liquidity** concerns the ability to meet obligations when due; **solvency** concerns longer-run sustainability under a defined assessment. Public-sector solvency is complex because future tax capacity and public assets are not equivalent to liquid cash.

A **cash balance** is a stock at a date; cash flow is movement during a period. A fiscal balance calculated on another accounting basis can differ from the change in cash due to timing, accruals and financing operations.

**Arrears** are overdue obligations under the applicable definition. They can arise from cash shortages, disputed claims, administrative delays or other causes. Arrears can shift financial pressure to contractors, suppliers, workers or subnational governments and affect private-sector liquidity. Their existence alone does not establish why they arose or whether each claim is valid.

## 11. Guarantees and contingent fiscal risks

A **direct liability** is an obligation recognized as belonging to the reporting entity under the applicable rules. A **contingent liability** is a possible obligation whose occurrence or amount depends on future events, or an exposure not recognized as direct debt under the framework.

Potential examples include government guarantees, certain legal claims, public-private partnership risks, and support that may be needed by public enterprises or financial institutions. A guarantee does not necessarily become direct government debt when issued; it creates exposure that may become a fiscal cost if called under its terms.

Fiscal-risk analysis may need to consider public enterprises, guarantees, arrears, legal claims, pension or other long-term obligations and public-private partnerships. Boundaries and valuation assumptions must be explicit. Adding every possible exposure at face value can also misstate expected cost.

## 12. Public investment and outcomes

Public projects should be analysed across distinct stages:
1. **Allocation:** planned resources.
2. **Commitment:** contractual or other binding undertaking.
3. **Disbursement/payment:** cash paid.
4. **Physical completion:** work or asset delivered.
5. **Operational readiness:** staff, supplies, systems and maintenance are in place.
6. **Service output:** the asset or service is actually used.
7. **Outcome:** the intended change, such as improved access or health, is observed.
8. **Impact:** wider long-term effects, often requiring stronger evaluation.

A completed building without staff or equipment may deliver little service. A road can be built but deteriorate without maintenance. Public value may involve access, reliability, equity, resilience and service quality as well as monetary returns. Spending is an input, not proof of outcome.

## 13. Intergovernmental finance

Federal, state and local governments may have different tax powers, spending responsibilities and service-delivery roles. **Intergovernmental transfers** move resources between levels to share revenue, equalize fiscal capacity, fund delegated functions or support specified services.

A transfer may be expenditure for one level and revenue for another. When consolidating the whole public sector, internal transfers are handled to avoid double counting; gross figures can still be useful to understand intergovernmental relationships.

The level that allocates funds may not be the one that collects the revenue, procures the service, operates the asset or bears ultimate accountability. Trace these roles separately rather than assuming they coincide.

## 14. Fiscal policy and the wider economy

**Fiscal policy** describes government choices about revenue, expenditure and financing that affect economic conditions and public objectives. It may support demand, provide public goods, redistribute resources, stabilize activity or invest in productive capacity.

Effects depend on the timing and composition of measures, economic slack, import content, monetary conditions, exchange rates, household and firm responses, administrative capacity and financing. **Automatic stabilizers** change revenue or spending with economic conditions without a new discretionary decision; **discretionary measures** involve deliberate policy changes. Fiscal multipliers are context-sensitive and should not be applied mechanically.

Fiscal policy interacts with inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, public debt markets and financial-system liquidity. A deficit alone does not establish the direction or magnitude of these effects.

## 14.1 Fiscal space, sustainability and sovereign wealth funds

**Fiscal space** is the government's practical capacity to increase spending, reduce taxes or absorb a shock without undermining its fiscal sustainability or other policy objectives. It is not a single universally defined number. Assessment may consider revenue capacity, borrowing terms, debt-service burdens, maturity and currency structure, liquid financial assets, spending commitments, economic conditions, institutional credibility and the likely consequences of adjustment.

**Debt sustainability** asks whether a government can meet its obligations over time under plausible assumptions about growth, interest rates, primary balances, exchange rates, maturities and contingent liabilities. A debt-to-GDP ratio is one indicator, not a standalone verdict. A government can have a moderate debt ratio but acute cash or refinancing pressure; a higher ratio may be more manageable under different financing, currency, maturity and revenue conditions. The analysis must specify assumptions and stress scenarios.

A **sovereign wealth fund (SWF)** is a state-owned investment vehicle established under a particular mandate. Funds may have different objectives, including stabilization against revenue volatility, saving for future generations, development financing or managing public assets. These objectives can conflict if the mandate, funding rules, withdrawal conditions and governance are unclear.

A fund's headline asset value is not automatically money available to the budget. The assets may be illiquid, encumbered, held in foreign currency, subject to withdrawal rules or intended for a different purpose. To assess the public-sector position, distinguish the fund's assets and liabilities, the government's direct debt, guarantees and other contingent claims, and any transfers between the fund and the budget. Consolidation can prevent internal public-sector claims from being counted twice, while separate entity accounts remain useful for accountability.

**Fiscal space → feasible policy choices → budget decisions → actual execution and outcomes.**

In plain language, the resources a government appears to have on paper do not necessarily translate into funds it can spend immediately or use without consequences. Debt maturities, cash availability, legal mandates, revenue volatility and implementation capacity can constrain the choices. Even when funding is available, the eventual public benefit depends on procurement, delivery, operation and service quality.

For applied work, state whether fiscal space is being assessed as a conceptual judgement, a debt-sustainability scenario, a cash/liquidity measure, a borrowing-capacity estimate or a specific official indicator. These are related but not interchangeable.

## 15. Transparency, accountability and evidence

Fiscal evidence can come from budget laws, execution reports, treasury statements, debt reports, audited accounts, procurement records, legislative oversight and public-enterprise statements. Sources may differ in timing, coverage, accounting basis and reliability.

For every fiscal claim, record the reporting institution and boundary, period and publication date, cash or accrual basis, gross or consolidated presentation, currency and valuation date, original or revised budget, whether figures are audited or provisional, and any revisions or reconciliation items.

A missing record does not by itself prove wrongdoing. A deficit, project award, cost or delay alone does not establish corruption, waste or causal failure. These may motivate questions, but findings require specific evidence and assessment of alternatives.

## 16. Illustrative example

Suppose a government approves a project budget of ₦100 million, signs a contract for ₦80 million, pays ₦30 million during the year, and the contractor completes only part of the work by year-end. These figures are hypothetical.

The appropriation is spending authority, not proof that ₦100 million was paid. The contract is a commitment, not necessarily cash expenditure. The payment is a cash-flow fact, but its meaning depends on what was delivered, certified and recognized. Partial completion is a physical-output fact, not by itself a verdict about value for money or misconduct.

Further assessment would require contract and amendment records, payment certificates, procurement documents, delivery evidence, schedules, quality checks, remaining obligations, asset readiness and eventual service outcomes.

## 17. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Government vs public sector | Public sector may include public corporations beyond general government. |
| Revenue vs financing | Borrowing and many asset transactions are financing, not revenue. |
| Tax liability vs tax incidence | Legal remittance responsibility differs from who bears the economic burden. |
| Budget vs appropriation | A budget is a fiscal plan; appropriation is spending authority under the applicable system. |
| Appropriation vs commitment vs payment | Authorization, a binding undertaking and cash paid are different stages. |
| Capital expenditure vs completed asset | Classification does not prove completion, operation or maintenance. |
| Expenditure vs transfer | Transfers redistribute resources; they are not all direct purchases of output. |
| Deficit vs debt | Deficit is a flow; debt is a liability stock. |
| Primary vs overall balance | The primary balance excludes interest under a specified definition. |
| Borrowing vs debt-stock change | Stock changes also reflect repayment, valuation and other adjustments. |
| Debt service vs interest | Debt service may mean interest alone or principal plus interest. |
| Public debt vs contingent liabilities | Direct debt and possible future exposures are different. |
| Cash balance vs fiscal balance | Timing, accounting basis and financing can make them differ. |
| Liquidity vs solvency | Ability to pay when due differs from longer-run sustainability. |
| Public spending vs outcome | Spending is an input; service outputs and outcomes need separate evidence. |
| Gross vs consolidated fiscal figures | Internal transfers may be double counted unless consolidation rules are applied. |
| Deficit vs mismanagement | A fiscal balance alone is not a causal or ethical verdict. |

## 18. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before using a public-finance figure, ask:
1. Which government level, entities and public-sector boundary are included?
2. Is the amount revenue, expenditure, financing, a stock, a flow or a contingent exposure?
3. What accounting basis and classification are used?
4. Is it budgeted, appropriated, released, committed, accrued, paid, outstanding or audited?
5. Is the budget original or revised, and what is the denominator for an execution rate?
6. Does revenue mean assessed, collected or cash received?
7. Are transfers, interest, principal repayment and public corporations treated consistently?
8. Is the fiscal-balance definition explicit, and are stock-flow adjustments explained?
9. What debt instruments, currencies, residency categories and entities are included?
10. Are guarantees and contingent risks disclosed separately and valued appropriately?
11. Are assets complete, operational and maintained, and are outcomes independently evidenced?
12. Are gross and consolidated figures being compared consistently?
13. Can fiscal figures be reconciled with debt, cash, arrears and audited accounts?
14. Does the evidence support a measured fact, a plausible mechanism or a causal allegation?
15. What independent records and alternative explanations should be checked?

## 20. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Fiscal concepts depend on institutional boundary, accounting basis, timing and classification.
- Revenue differs from financing; borrowing creates a liability and is not automatically revenue.
- Deficit and debt are flow and stock measures.
- Authorization, commitment, payment, physical delivery and outcomes are separate stages.
- Tax remittance liability and economic incidence can differ.
- Direct debt, guarantees and contingent fiscal risks are not identical.
- Public spending does not by itself establish service outcomes or value for money.
- Fiscal figures alone do not prove misconduct or explain causal effects.

### Scope limits
- Exact definitions vary by statistical framework, budget law and reporting institution.
- This note provides no current Nigerian revenue, deficit, debt, tax-burden or budget-execution figures.
- The worked project example is hypothetical.
- Official indicator selection, calculation and validation remain in Workstream B.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which current Nigerian fiscal reports cover federal government, general government, states, local governments and public corporations?
- How do budget, treasury, debt and national-accounting publications define balances and debt boundaries?
- Which budget-execution stages and cash/accrual conventions are reported?
- How are intergovernmental transfers consolidated?
- Which guarantees, public-enterprise risks, arrears and contingent exposures are disclosed?
- What records trace appropriations through procurement, payment, completion and service outcomes?
- Which fiscal indicators are useful for monitoring without conflating stocks, flows, financing and outcomes?

## 21. Short summary

Government finance involves distinct flows, stocks, authorizations, liabilities and outcomes. Revenue is not financing; tax liability is not tax incidence; a deficit is not debt; and an appropriation, contract, payment, completed asset and service outcome are different stages. Public-debt analysis needs a clear institutional boundary and attention to interest, currency, maturity, guarantees and stock-flow adjustments. Fiscal statistics require consistent definitions, coverage and independent evidence before drawing conclusions about sustainability, performance or misconduct.

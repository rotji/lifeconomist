# Concept 23 — Economic Systems, Institutions and Structural Change

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; country- and sector-specific claims require separate, source-backed investigation  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 22 — Trade, Supply Chains and Distribution Networks](trade-supply-chains-and-distribution-networks.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain how economies organize production, ownership, allocation, coordination and distribution; how institutions shape behavior; and how economic structures persist or change.

## 1. The starting question: how is an economy organized?

An economy is not simply a collection of people buying and selling things. It is a connected arrangement through which people and organizations produce goods and services, allocate resources, own and control assets, finance activity, exchange outputs, distribute income, and manage risk.

Those activities occur within formal rules, informal norms, organizations, networks, technologies and physical constraints. These arrangements influence what actors can do, what it costs, which risks they face, and who can benefit from economic activity.

To investigate an economic system, ask:

1. **What is organized?** Production, exchange, ownership, financing, employment, distribution, public provision, or another activity?
2. **Who participates?** Households, firms, workers, investors, lenders, public bodies, intermediaries, communities or external actors?
3. **Who owns, controls, finances, operates and benefits?** These roles may belong to different actors.
4. **What coordinates decisions?** Prices, contracts, corporate authority, public directives, networks, customs or a combination?
5. **Which rules operate in practice?** Distinguish written rules from implementation, informal practice and enforcement.
6. **What changes over time?** Separate short-term fluctuations from lasting changes in the composition or organization of activity.
7. **What evidence supports the explanation?** Distinguish rules, observed behavior, measured outcomes, plausible mechanisms and causal conclusions.

The word *system* should not be used as a vague explanation by itself. State the components, relationships, boundaries and mechanisms that make the system work.

## 2. Economic system, institution, organization and economic structure

These terms are related, but they describe different things.

- **Economic system:** The overall arrangement through which production, allocation, exchange, ownership, financing and distribution are organized.
- **Institution:** A formal rule or informal norm that structures interaction and shapes expectations or behavior. Definitions vary across economic traditions, so the intended meaning should be stated.
- **Organization:** A coordinated group or entity, such as a company, bank, cooperative, regulator or government department.
- **Economic structure:** The composition and arrangement of activities and relationships in an economy, including sector shares, firm types, employment patterns, ownership arrangements and production networks.
- **Economic organization:** The practical way decisions and activities are coordinated, for example through markets, hierarchies, public allocation, networks or collective governance.
- **Structural change:** A lasting change in the composition or organization of economic activity, rather than merely a short-term movement in output or prices.

A court is an organization; rules for enforcing contracts are institutions. A central bank is an organization; the monetary and legal arrangements governing its powers are institutional arrangements. A market is an exchange mechanism or institutional arrangement depending on the context, not necessarily a single physical place or organization.

The distinctions matter because evidence about one category does not automatically establish claims about another. A law can exist without being consistently enforced. A registered company can exist without being operationally active. A sector's share of GDP can rise because its output grew, because other sectors contracted, or because relative prices changed.

## 3. How economic systems coordinate activity

Economies combine several coordination mechanisms. They should not be reduced to a single label such as “market” or “state.”

### Markets and prices

Markets coordinate some decisions through offers, bids, prices, competition and voluntary exchange. Prices can communicate information about scarcity and demand, but their signals may be affected by market power, external effects, taxes, subsidies, information gaps, regulation and unequal ability to pay.

### Hierarchies and organizations

Within firms and other organizations, authority, budgets, procedures and management decisions coordinate work. A firm may use market prices to buy inputs while assigning tasks internally through hierarchy.

### Public allocation and administration

Governments allocate resources through budgets, procurement, public employment, regulation, transfers and direct provision. Public decisions may pursue goals not captured by private profitability alone, but their actual effects depend on design, implementation, accountability and capacity.

### Networks and informal arrangements

Repeated relationships, trust, reputation, shared information and customary practices can coordinate activity, including where formal services are inaccessible or costly. Networks can lower search and enforcement costs, but may also exclude outsiders or concentrate access in gatekeepers.

### Collective arrangements

Cooperatives, mutual organizations and other collective forms can pool resources or govern shared activity. Their performance depends on governance, incentives, member participation, scale and the specific task.

Most economies use all of these mechanisms. A more useful question than “Which system is this?” is: **which mechanism coordinates which activity, at what scale, under whose authority, and with what consequences?**

## 4. Economic systems have multiple dimensions

Labels such as capitalism, socialism, state-led development or a mixed economy summarize broad patterns, but they can hide important differences. Compare systems along explicit dimensions:

| Dimension | Investigative question |
|---|---|
| Ownership | Who holds legal or beneficial interests in land, firms, infrastructure, natural resources and financial assets? |
| Control | Who can make or veto decisions about the use of those assets? |
| Allocation | How are scarce resources assigned among competing uses? |
| Coordination | Are activities coordinated through prices, contracts, hierarchy, public decisions, networks or collective rules? |
| Financing | Who supplies funding, and on what maturity, price, collateral and risk terms? |
| Distribution | Who receives wages, profits, interest, rents, taxes, transfers or other benefits? |
| Risk allocation | Who bears losses when projects fail, prices change or counterparties do not perform? |
| Entry and competition | Who can enter a market, obtain licences, reach customers or access essential inputs? |
| Accountability | Who can challenge decisions, enforce obligations or replace decision-makers? |
| External dependence | Which critical inputs, capabilities, markets or financing sources are controlled outside the system's boundary? |

Two economies with similar ownership patterns may perform differently because infrastructure, enforcement, competition, financial access, administrative capability or skills differ. Two firms in the same country may also face very different effective conditions.

A system label is therefore a starting classification, not a complete causal explanation.

## 5. Institutions: formal rules and informal norms

Institutions shape interaction by affecting expectations, incentives, constraints, rights and the costs of coordinating activity.

### Formal institutions

Examples include laws, regulations, property-rights frameworks, contract rules, bankruptcy procedures, tax systems, monetary arrangements, licensing requirements and official standards.

### Informal institutions

Examples include business customs, trust, professional norms, family obligations, community expectations, reputation systems and unwritten practices for resolving disputes.

Formal and informal arrangements may reinforce one another, substitute for one another or conflict. A written contract may be backed by courts, by repeated dealings and reputation, or by both. Where formal enforcement is slow or inaccessible, businesses may rely more heavily on advance payment, trusted intermediaries or relationship-based credit.

Informal arrangements are not automatically superior or inferior. They can enable transactions where formal services are absent, while also limiting access for newcomers or making rights difficult to enforce. Formal institutions likewise can protect participants but impose costs or be applied unevenly.

### Rules on paper versus rules in operation

A statute or policy document establishes something about the formal rule, its scope and stated intent. It does not by itself establish that the rule is implemented, enforced consistently, accessible to all participants or effective in producing its intended result.

For institutional analysis, distinguish:

1. The formal rule or announced policy.
2. The organization responsible for implementation.
3. The resources, incentives and authority available to that organization.
4. The behavior actually observed.
5. The outcomes measured.
6. The mechanism proposed to connect the rule to those outcomes.
7. Alternative explanations and evidence that could disconfirm the claim.

This distinction prevents an official announcement from being treated as proof of implementation or success.

## 6. How institutions affect economic decisions

Institutions do not mechanically determine outcomes. They change the incentives, constraints, information and risks under which people and organizations make decisions.

Consider a business deciding whether to buy a machine. Its decision may depend on the machine's cost, expected demand, access to credit, electricity reliability, the availability of skilled workers, import procedures, contract enforcement and confidence about future rules.

A possible mechanism is:

**More reliable infrastructure and predictable rules → lower operating uncertainty → investment becomes more attractive → productive capacity may expand.**

In ordinary language, a business may be more willing to invest when it expects the equipment to work, financing to remain manageable and the rules to be sufficiently predictable. Investment is not guaranteed: demand may be weak, the machine may be unsuitable, or the expected return may still be too low.

The reverse conditions can make an otherwise useful investment unattractive. Unreliable power may increase operating costs; expensive credit can raise financing costs; unpredictable procedures may delay inputs. These constraints can interact rather than operate independently.

A visible outcome such as low investment may therefore have several possible explanations. Institutional analysis should investigate the mechanism and relative importance of each rather than attribute the outcome to one broad label.

## 7. Ownership, control, financing, benefit and risk

Economic power is not a single role. An actor may own an asset without operating it, operate it without owning it, finance it without controlling it, or benefit from it while bearing only part of its risks.

Distinguish the following roles:

- **Owner:** holds a legal or beneficial ownership interest under the applicable rules.
- **Controller or decision-maker:** can direct or veto specified decisions.
- **Operator:** performs or manages day-to-day activity.
- **Financier:** supplies debt, equity or other funding.
- **Regulator:** holds specified public authority to set or enforce rules.
- **Intermediary or gatekeeper:** connects parties or controls access to a resource, market, service or process.
- **Beneficiary:** receives income, services, protection or another advantage.
- **Risk bearer:** is exposed to losses under the relevant economic, contractual or public arrangement.

These roles can overlap, but should not be presumed identical. The relevant evidence may include ownership records, governance documents, contracts, financial statements, guarantees, regulatory rules and actual operating records.

For example, a public authority may own infrastructure, a private firm may operate it, lenders may finance it, and users may pay fees. A public guarantee may shift some specified downside risk to taxpayers without transferring ownership of the asset to them.

A possible mechanism is:

**Public guarantee → lender's expected downside may fall → financing may become easier or cheaper → project may proceed → government retains contingent exposure.**

In ordinary language, a guarantee can make a lender more willing to provide funding because the public authority promises to absorb defined losses if specified conditions occur. That can support useful investment, but it also creates a potential public liability. The actual effect depends on the guarantee's terms, borrower performance and whether the guarantee is called.

A relationship map should therefore separate ownership, control, operation, financing, payment, benefit and risk. One type of connection does not prove the others.

## 8. Structural change: what changes in an economy?

Structural change is a lasting shift in the composition or organization of economic activity. It can involve changes in what is produced, how it is produced, where workers and capital are employed, how firms are organized, or how domestic and external networks connect.

Common forms include:

- **Sectoral change:** shifts among agriculture, manufacturing, construction and services.
- **Technological change:** adoption of machinery, software, automation, new energy technologies or production methods.
- **Organizational change:** shifts among household production, informal firms, formal companies, platforms, franchises, cooperatives and corporate groups.
- **Geographic and external change:** relocation of production, changing trade relationships, import dependence or integration into cross-border production networks.
- **Financial and institutional change:** shifts in payment systems, access to credit, property-rights arrangements, taxation, regulation or business formalization.
- **Capability upgrading:** movement toward more productive, technically complex or higher-value activities, when supported by appropriate evidence.

Structural change is not identical to economic growth. Growth concerns an increase in a defined measure of output, commonly real GDP. Structural change concerns composition or organization. An economy can grow without substantially diversifying, and its structure can change during a period of weak or falling total output.

A rising share of services, for example, does not alone prove higher productivity or improved living standards. The share could rise because services expand, because other sectors contract, or because relative prices change. Interpretation requires absolute output, price-adjusted measures, employment, productivity, distribution and other relevant evidence.

## 9. Related terms that must remain distinct

- **Economic growth:** an increase in a specified measure of output over time, usually real GDP for aggregate growth claims.
- **Economic development:** a broader concept concerning capabilities and human and economic outcomes; it is not reducible to GDP growth alone.
- **Economic diversification:** reduced concentration across specified products, sectors, markets or revenue sources. The relevant measure and boundary must be stated.
- **Industrialization:** an increase in the role, scale, capability or complexity of industrial production, depending on the definition used.
- **Formalization:** movement of activities or relationships into recognized legal, regulatory or administrative frameworks. Formalization does not automatically improve productivity.
- **Upgrading:** movement toward more capable, productive or higher-value activities within a firm, sector or production network.
- **Modernization:** a broad label for technological, organizational or institutional change; it needs a specified meaning and cannot serve as a self-evident measure of progress.
- **Resilience:** capacity to withstand, adapt to and recover from disruption, given a defined system and shock.
- **Structural transformation:** a substantial and persistent reorganization of an economy's activities or capabilities; its meaning should be operationalized for measurement.

These concepts can overlap but are not interchangeable. An economy can formalize without diversifying, industrialize while remaining dependent on one commodity, or diversify without moving into higher-productivity activities.

## 10. Path dependence, institutional persistence and lock-in

Past decisions can shape present options because assets, infrastructure, skills, supplier relationships, regulations and expectations accumulate over time. Changing direction may require replacing or adapting several connected parts of a system.

A simplified mechanism is:

**Past investment → specialized assets and capabilities → dependence on established activity → high switching costs → persistence of the existing structure.**

In ordinary language, a region built around a particular industry may have trained workers, specialized machinery, transport routes, suppliers and public revenue tied to that industry. Moving to a different activity requires more than one firm deciding to change; other capabilities and relationships may also need to change.

Keep the following terms distinct:

- **Path dependence:** earlier developments influence present choices and future possibilities.
- **Institutional persistence:** rules, norms or organizational arrangements continue over time.
- **Lock-in:** reinforcing dependencies or switching costs make an alternative difficult to adopt.
- **Sunk cost:** an expenditure already incurred that cannot be recovered by the current decision; it should not be treated as a forward-looking cost simply because it was large.
- **Hysteresis:** a temporary shock can leave persistent effects after the original shock has passed, under a specified mechanism.
- **Transition cost:** resources or disruption required to move from one arrangement to another.

Persistence is not proof of inefficiency. Specialization can deliver economies of scale or accumulated expertise. The investigation should compare the benefits of continuing with the costs, risks and feasibility of alternatives.

## 11. Institutions and structural change influence one another

Institutions shape which activities are feasible and attractive, while changes in technology, production, employment and exchange can create pressure for institutional adaptation.

For example, wider use of digital payments can increase demand for payment-system rules, consumer protections, cybersecurity arrangements and settlement infrastructure. Those arrangements can then affect the cost, reach and design of digital services.

A conceptual feedback loop is:

**Institutional change → altered incentives and capabilities → structural change → new economic and social pressures → further institutional change.**

In ordinary language, rules help determine which activities can grow. When those activities become more important, they may expose gaps in existing rules or create new demands. Institutions may adapt, adapt slowly, respond unevenly or be shaped by actors who benefit from the existing arrangement. The outcome is not necessarily progressive or predictable.

This is a feedback framework, not proof that any specific reform caused a particular structural outcome. The timing, implementation, mechanism, alternative explanations and evidence must be examined in each case.

## 12. Institutions, market power and access

Economic arrangements affect who can participate and on what terms. Relevant sources of influence include ownership, control of scarce resources, access to finance, legal authority, market position, information, standards and control over essential infrastructure.

Useful questions include:

- Who can enter the market, and what permissions, capital or connections are required?
- Who controls essential inputs, routes, platforms, standards or distribution channels?
- Who can set terms, delay access or refuse service?
- Are alternatives available, and what does switching cost?
- Who receives the benefits and who bears the downside risks?
- Do observed differences reflect productive efficiency, market power, regulation, scarcity or another mechanism?

Concentration alone does not prove abuse or fragility. A concentrated supplier base may reflect scale economies or specialized requirements; it may also create dependence where substitutes are limited. Evidence about entry barriers, pricing, service, alternatives and switching is needed to distinguish the explanations.

Likewise, the fact that an actor benefits from an arrangement does not establish that the actor deliberately created it. Benefit, influence, causal contribution and intent are separate claims requiring different evidence.

## 13. Worked example: a retailer facing persistent operating constraints

Consider a hypothetical retailer that wants to expand but repeatedly delays investment in additional storage and inventory.

Possible factors include expensive credit, unreliable electricity, uncertain demand, supplier minimum-order requirements, transport delays, inconsistent contract enforcement and limited access to suitable premises.

A weak explanation would choose one factor—such as “poor institutions”—and treat it as the complete cause. A stronger investigation would map the decision and test each plausible mechanism.

| Question | Evidence to seek | What it would not prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Is finance binding? | Loan offers, interest and fees, collateral terms, rejected applications, cash-flow timing | That finance is the only constraint |
| Is infrastructure binding? | Outage records, backup costs, spoilage or downtime records | The total contribution of infrastructure to weak growth |
| Is supply reliability binding? | Orders, lead times, stockouts, supplier terms and alternatives | That a supplier intentionally caused the problem |
| Are institutions binding? | Relevant rules, processing times, documented disputes and enforcement experience | That every business faces the same problem |
| Is demand uncertain? | Sales patterns, customer orders, seasonality and forecast errors | That observed past sales will continue |
| Would expansion help? | Incremental costs, capacity use, expected sales and downside scenarios | That a favorable forecast will be realized |

The example illustrates how institutional and structural analysis can guide evidence collection. It does not assert anything about a particular Nigerian retailer or estimate the prevalence of these constraints.

## 14. Evidence: rules, implementation, behavior and outcomes

Different sources establish different layers of reality.

| Evidence source | May establish | Does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Law or regulation | Formal rule, authority and stated scope | Consistent implementation or effectiveness |
| Policy announcement | Stated objective or intended intervention | Actual implementation or effect |
| Budget or appropriation | Authorized allocation under a defined framework | Disbursement, completion or service outcome |
| Company registration | Recorded legal status | Active operation, profitability or productive capacity |
| Survey | Reported experience within the sampled population | Conditions for every actor or causal effect |
| Administrative records | Events captured by the relevant system | Events that were not captured or reported |
| Economic statistics | Outcomes measured under defined methods | A complete causal explanation |
| Interview or field observation | Experience, practice and possible mechanism | National prevalence or causation without corroboration |

For consequential claims, record what is directly observed, what is reported, what is inferred, what remains unknown and what evidence could disconfirm the hypothesis. Data gaps should remain visible rather than being replaced with confident assumptions.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Economic system vs economic structure | The system includes organizing arrangements and relationships; structure describes composition and configuration. |
| Institution vs organization | An institution is a rule or norm; an organization is an entity or coordinated group. |
| Formal rule vs effective practice | A written rule does not prove consistent implementation. |
| Ownership vs control | Holding an ownership interest does not always confer day-to-day authority or decisive control. |
| Operator vs owner | Operating an asset does not establish ownership. |
| Financier vs beneficiary | Providing funding does not mean receiving all benefits or bearing all risks. |
| Market coordination vs market economy | Markets are one coordination mechanism; a whole economy combines several mechanisms. |
| State ownership vs state control | Legal ownership and practical decision-making authority may differ. |
| Economic growth vs structural change | Growth concerns change in output; structural change concerns composition or organization. |
| Structural change vs development | A changed composition does not automatically establish better capabilities or welfare. |
| Diversification vs upgrading | Broader activity distribution is different from moving into more capable or productive activities. |
| Formalization vs productivity | Formal recognition does not automatically increase output per input. |
| Path dependence vs lock-in | Earlier paths influence options; lock-in refers to stronger persistence caused by reinforcing dependencies or switching costs. |
| Sunk cost vs future cost | A sunk cost cannot be recovered by the current decision; a future cost can still change with the decision. |
| Benefiting vs causing | Receiving a benefit does not prove that an actor caused the arrangement. |
| Correlation vs institutional mechanism | Related movements do not establish the process connecting them. |
| Policy announcement vs implementation | Intent or authorization does not prove that action occurred. |
| Implementation vs outcome | An intervention can be implemented without achieving its intended result. |
| Concentration vs abuse of power | Concentration can create risks but needs contextual evidence to establish its effects. |

## 16. Connections to other Workstreams

- **Workstream A:** Connects production, value added, capital formation, money, credit, markets, labour, government finance, ownership, risk and supply networks through the arrangements that organize economic activity.
- **Workstream B — Measurement:** Sector shares, productivity, concentration, formalization and structural change require explicit units, boundaries, time periods, price treatment and definitions. This note does not specify a new indicator or assert current national values.
- **Workstream C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** Track formal announcements, implementation, observed behavior and outcomes as distinct events. Record source provenance, delays, revisions and alternative explanations.
- **Workstream D — Footprint Intelligence:** Map ownership, control, operation, financing, regulation, facilities and dependencies as separate relationship types. A relationship must be supported by appropriate evidence; co-occurrence is not proof of control or causation.
- **Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application:** Institutional or structural constraints may create practical problems for firms or households, but any proposed service requires evidence of a real problem, accessible data, useful action and willingness to pay. This concept is not market-demand evidence.
- **Global Financial System Exploration Map:** Connect institutional rules to financial claims, funding, capital allocation, ownership, external dependence and transmission mechanisms without double-counting financial claims and underlying economic activity.

## 17. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions

- Economic systems combine multiple coordination mechanisms.
- Institutions include formal rules and informal norms; organizations are actors or structured groups.
- Ownership, control, operation, financing, benefit and risk are distinct roles.
- Formal rules, implementation, behavior and outcomes require separate evidence.
- Economic growth and structural change answer different questions.
- Path dependence and switching costs can help explain persistence, but do not prove that an existing arrangement is inefficient.
- Institutions and economic structure can influence one another through feedback mechanisms.
- A plausible mechanism is not by itself proof of causal effect or intent.

### Scope limits

- This note does not classify or rank any country's institutions or economic system.
- No current Nigerian institutional-performance, productivity, diversification or sectoral estimates are asserted.
- No specific reform is claimed to have caused a particular economic outcome.
- Legal rights and obligations require jurisdiction- and date-appropriate sources.
- Structural-change claims require consistent time series and attention to prices, sector boundaries, employment and measurement changes.

### Questions to revisit

- How can effective institutional performance be measured when formal rules and observed practice diverge?
- Which methods can distinguish market power from efficient specialization?
- How should Lifeconomist trace control and influence when ownership is fragmented across entities or intermediaries?
- What evidence is needed to establish that an institution constrained investment rather than merely coincided with weak investment?
- How can structural transformation be assessed without treating any one sector share or formalization measure as a sufficient proxy for development?
- How can the costs of institutional change be compared with the risks of persistence without assuming that the alternative is feasible?
- Which country and sector case studies can demonstrate these mechanisms with verifiable evidence?

## 18. Short summary

An economy is organized through a combination of markets, organizations, public authority, networks, informal norms and other coordination mechanisms. Institutions shape the rules and expectations under which actors own, control, finance, operate and exchange resources. Structural change describes lasting shifts in the composition or organization of activity, while economic growth concerns changes in output. Past investments and relationships can make structures persist, and institutional arrangements can both shape and respond to economic transformation. For Lifeconomist, the task is to identify the relevant actors, rules, mechanisms, outcomes and evidence without confusing formal policy with implementation, correlation with causation, or a plausible explanation with a demonstrated one.

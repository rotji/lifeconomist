# Concept 12 — Markets, Prices and Exchange

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational concept note; definitions vary across economic theory, everyday use, accounting and financial markets  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Investment, Saving, Financial Markets and Capital Allocation](investment-saving-financial-markets-and-capital-allocation.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain how markets coordinate exchange and how prices emerge, while distinguishing price from value, supply from quantity supplied, demand from quantity demanded, and goods markets from financial markets.

## 1. What is a market?

A **market** is a set of arrangements through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods, services, assets or claims. It does not have to be a physical location. A market may operate in a street, shop, wholesale network, online platform, commodity exchange, foreign-exchange venue, procurement system or through negotiated contracts.

A market has participants, goods or claims being exchanged, rules, information, mechanisms for matching counterparties, and a way to agree on terms. The exact structure differs: some markets use posted prices; others use auctions, tenders, bilateral negotiation, dealer quotes or long-term contracts.

A **marketplace** is often the physical or digital venue where exchange occurs. A **market** can be broader, including the participants, substitutes, institutions, logistics, regulation and competing offers beyond any single venue.

Markets vary in:
- **Scope:** local, regional, national or global.
- **Product definition:** a market for a specific good, service, grade, location, delivery date or financial claim.
- **Timing:** spot transactions, forward agreements or longer-term contracts.
- **Organization:** informal exchange, posted-price retail, auction, dealer market, exchange or platform.
- **Access:** who can participate, under what conditions and at what cost.
- **Competition:** number and strength of alternatives, entry barriers and bargaining power.

The boundary chosen affects conclusions. For example, a shop may face little direct competition within one street but substantial competition from online sellers or substitutes. A market definition should be appropriate to the question rather than assumed from geography or a business label.

## 2. Exchange: what changes hands?

An **exchange** is a transaction in which parties transfer goods, services, money, assets or claims under agreed terms. A simple retail purchase may exchange a product for payment. A labour transaction exchanges work under an employment arrangement for compensation. A bond trade transfers a financial claim and payment between parties.

To understand an exchange, identify:
- the parties and their roles;
- the item, service or claim being transferred;
- the price and other contractual terms;
- timing, location, quality and delivery conditions;
- payment and settlement arrangements;
- transaction costs, taxes and fees;
- the rights, risks and obligations that remain after the transaction.

The stated price may not capture the full economic cost of exchange. Transport, search time, financing, taxes, platform charges, quality uncertainty, delays, spoilage and enforcement costs can matter. Two offers with the same posted price may therefore have different total costs or usefulness to a buyer.

An exchange can benefit both parties relative to their alternatives without implying that benefits are equal. Bargaining power, information, urgency, market access and available alternatives influence how the gains are divided.

## 3. Price, value and cost are different concepts

**Price** is the amount of money quoted, charged or paid for a specified item or transaction under stated conditions. The phrase should clarify whether it means a posted price, offer, bid, transaction price, average price or reference price.

**Value** is broader and context-dependent. It may refer to usefulness, willingness to pay, market valuation, accounting value, social value or another specified concept. A buyer's personal assessment of usefulness is not the same as a market price; a company's accounting carrying value is not automatically its current market value.

**Cost** is the resources or expenditure incurred to produce, acquire, sell or use something. Different costs answer different questions:
- **Production cost:** resources used to produce output, measured under a stated boundary.
- **Acquisition cost:** amount and associated costs paid to obtain an asset or product.
- **Opportunity cost:** value of the best forgone alternative.
- **Transaction cost:** cost of searching, negotiating, contracting, paying, transporting or enforcing an exchange.
- **Marginal cost:** additional cost associated with producing one more unit under a specified short-run or long-run context.
- **Average cost:** total cost divided by a defined quantity.
- **Social cost:** costs borne across affected parties, potentially including external costs not reflected in the market price.

A price can be above, below or equal to a seller's cost depending on competition, strategy, scarcity, quality, taxes, subsidies and market conditions. A low price does not always mean low cost or high value; a high price does not prove high quality or high social benefit.

## 4. Transaction price, quoted price and reference price

A **quoted price** is an offer or indication available under stated conditions. It may not be a completed transaction. A **transaction price** is the price agreed in an actual exchange. A **reference price** is used for comparison, valuation, index construction or contractual adjustment; it may not be the price any particular buyer can obtain.

Examples:
- A retailer's shelf price is an offer; a discount or negotiation may change the final transaction price.
- A wholesale quotation may depend on quantity, delivery date, payment terms and quality grade.
- In financial markets, bid and ask quotes can differ; the executable price depends on direction, size, venue and time.
- An average market price may conceal differences in location, grade, contract terms or the mix of items sold.

When comparing prices, match the item, quality, quantity, location, date, currency, tax treatment, delivery terms and payment conditions. Otherwise, apparent price differences may not be meaningful.

## 5. Nominal prices, real prices and relative prices

A **nominal price** is expressed in the money units of the period being discussed. A **real price** adjusts for a general price index or another stated price basis, allowing comparisons of purchasing power across time. The exact result depends on the deflator or index selected.

If a product's nominal price rises by 10% while the relevant general price index rises by 15%, its inflation-adjusted price falls approximately 4.3% using the ratio 1.10/1.15, even though the amount of money paid is higher. This is an illustrative calculation, not a claim about a current product or Nigerian inflation.

A **relative price** compares the price of one good or asset with another price or index. Relative prices can change even when the general price level is stable. For example, food may become more expensive relative to transport because of a supply shock specific to food, changes in demand, or changes in production and distribution costs.

General inflation and relative-price changes should not be conflated. An index can rise while some individual prices fall. Real-price comparisons require consistent units, a relevant index, a defined base period and attention to changes in product quality.

## 6. Supply and quantity supplied

In economics, **supply** usually describes the relationship between the price of a good and the quantities producers are willing and able to offer for sale over a period, holding other relevant factors constant. A **supply curve** represents that relationship under the model's assumptions.

**Quantity supplied** is the amount producers are willing and able to sell at a particular price and at a particular time, given other conditions. A change in the good's own price typically causes a movement along a given supply curve in the basic model. A change in input prices, technology, taxes, subsidies, weather, expectations, the number of sellers, logistics or productive capacity may shift supply.

Supply is not identical to physical production. Goods can be produced and stored rather than offered for sale; sellers can release inventories; imports can add to market availability; and output may be lost during transport or spoilage. For perishable products, the timing of supply is especially important.

The model's “willing and able” condition matters. A producer may want to sell more but lack working capital, electricity, equipment, transport, labour, inputs or access to buyers. Stated intention alone is not effective supply.

## 7. Demand and quantity demanded

**Demand** usually describes the relationship between price and the quantities buyers are willing and able to purchase over a period, holding other relevant factors constant.

**Quantity demanded** is the amount buyers are willing and able to purchase at a particular price under the prevailing conditions. In the basic model, a change in the good's own price causes a movement along a given demand curve. Changes in income, preferences, population, expectations, credit availability, prices of substitutes and complements, or the number of buyers can shift demand.

Wants are not automatically effective market demand. A person may want a product but lack the income, credit or access needed to buy it at the prevailing price. Effective demand includes both willingness and ability to purchase under the model's definition.

Demand differs by segment. Households, institutions, businesses and government buyers may respond differently to price changes and have different procurement cycles, quality requirements and payment terms. Aggregate demand can hide these differences.

## 8. Movements along curves versus shifts in curves

A foundational distinction in the standard supply-and-demand model is:
- A change in the good's own price, with other conditions held constant, changes quantity supplied or quantity demanded along the existing curve.
- A change in another determinant may shift the supply or demand curve.

For example, if the price of a product rises, sellers may offer more of it along a given supply curve. If the price of electricity rises and electricity is an input, the supply curve for an electricity-intensive product may shift because production costs changed. If household income rises and the product is a normal good under the relevant circumstances, demand may shift outward.

These are model-based relationships, not universal predictions independent of context. Real-world observations can involve simultaneous shifts, capacity constraints, expectations, regulation, market power, time lags and measurement problems. A price increase alone does not identify whether demand increased, supply fell, market power changed, costs rose, or several mechanisms operated together.

## 9. Market equilibrium and disequilibrium

In a basic competitive supply-and-demand model, **equilibrium price and quantity** are where planned quantity supplied equals planned quantity demanded under the model's assumptions. At that point, there is no excess supply or excess demand in the simplified model.

If the price is above the model's equilibrium, planned quantity supplied may exceed quantity demanded, creating excess supply. If the price is below it, quantity demanded may exceed quantity supplied, creating excess demand. Actual adjustment may occur through prices, queues, rationing, inventory changes, delivery delays, contract terms, quality changes or other mechanisms.

Observed markets do not always clear instantaneously. Prices may be sticky; sellers may maintain posted prices; buyers may be rationed; contracts may fix terms; inventories may absorb temporary imbalances; and regulation can constrain adjustment. Equilibrium is a useful analytical concept, not a claim that every observed market is continuously balanced or fair.

Equilibrium also does not automatically imply a socially optimal outcome. Market failures, externalities, unequal bargaining power, missing information, public goods, market power and distributional concerns may matter.

## 10. Competition, market power and bargaining

**Competition** concerns the constraints firms face from existing rivals, potential entrants, substitutes and buyers. It is not determined only by the number of firms. Product differentiation, switching costs, entry barriers, access to distribution, regulation, network effects, scale economies and control of key inputs can affect competitive pressure.

**Market power** is the ability to influence prices or other terms relative to a competitive benchmark. It may arise from limited alternatives, differentiated products, control of scarce resources, intellectual property, network effects, switching costs, coordination or barriers to entry. Market power is a matter of degree and context, not simply a label for a large company.

**Bargaining power** concerns a party's ability to influence negotiated terms. A small retailer may have weak bargaining power against a concentrated supplier even when several retailers operate nearby. A supplier may have weak power when buyers can easily switch and compare alternatives.

Market concentration can be relevant evidence, but concentration alone does not prove abusive conduct or establish the degree of market power. The market definition, entry conditions, substitution possibilities, contracts, costs and observed behaviour matter.

## 11. Market structure is not one single scale

Common analytical categories include:
- **Perfect competition:** a benchmark model with many price-taking participants and strong assumptions.
- **Monopolistic competition:** many sellers with differentiated products and some discretion over price.
- **Oligopoly:** a market where a small number of significant firms may affect one another's decisions.
- **Monopoly:** one seller within a defined market, subject to the availability of substitutes and potential entry.

These categories are simplified models. Real markets may not fit neatly into one category, and the same firm may face different competitive conditions across products, locations and customer groups. A market with few sellers is not automatically a monopoly; a market with many sellers is not necessarily highly competitive if they coordinate, face common bottlenecks or offer poor substitutes.

## 12. Price discovery, spreads and transaction costs in financial markets

Financial markets use prices to express trading terms for claims such as shares, bonds, currencies and derivatives. **Price discovery** is the process through which participants' orders, information, expectations and trading constraints contribute to market prices. A price is an observed outcome of market processes, not a perfect summary of all available information.

A **bid** is the price a buyer is willing to pay under stated conditions; an **ask/offer** is the price a seller is willing to accept. The **bid–ask spread** is the difference between them. **Market depth** describes available orders or liquidity at different prices, depending on the market's data and conventions. A quoted price may not be executable for a large order without moving the market.

Trading costs can include spreads, brokerage, exchange fees, taxes, financing costs, slippage, market impact and settlement friction. In foreign exchange, quoted rates can vary by provider, size, currency pair, time, transfer method and customer eligibility. A displayed rate is not necessarily the rate a user can actually obtain after fees and restrictions.

Market liquidity, transaction volume and price stability are different. A market can have high volume but poor depth at a particular moment; a stable price can coexist with low trading; and during stress, apparent liquidity can disappear. Do not infer executable profit from two displayed prices without matching timestamps, sizes, fees, settlement, access and risk.

## 13. Goods markets versus financial markets

Goods and services markets exchange products or services. Their prices can reflect production costs, scarcity, quality, demand, logistics, taxes, market power and expectations. Physical capacity, perishability, storage and transport can constrain adjustment.

Financial markets exchange claims or contracts whose values depend on expected future payments, risks, interest rates, liquidity, information, legal rights and market conditions. Financial prices can change rapidly without an immediate change in the current physical output of the issuer. A share price can rise because expected future profits improve, discount rates fall, risk appetite changes or supply and demand for the share shift.

Both types of markets involve buyers, sellers, terms and price formation, but the object exchanged and the mechanisms differ. A financial asset's price is not a direct measure of the issuer's current output, the social value of its activity, or the amount of productive capital created in the period.

## 14. Market failures, externalities and public goods

A market transaction may omit costs or benefits borne by people outside the transaction. An **externality** is an effect on others not fully reflected in the relevant market decision or price. Pollution is a common example of a possible negative externality; knowledge spillovers can be a positive externality.

A **public good** in economic theory is typically non-rival and non-excludable to a meaningful degree. Such characteristics can make private provision difficult, although real goods and services vary and institutions can change access and exclusion.

Other potential market failures include information asymmetry, incomplete markets, coordination problems, market power and missing insurance or credit markets. Identifying a possible market failure does not by itself prove that a particular intervention will improve outcomes. Policy design, administrative capacity, incentives, costs, unintended consequences and distributional effects also require evidence.

## 15. Price signals and their limits

Prices can convey information about scarcity, willingness to pay, costs, risk and expectations. They can influence production, consumption, investment and resource allocation. Yet prices are shaped by institutions and constraints, and they do not encode every socially relevant fact.

A price may fail to reflect:
- environmental or health costs imposed on third parties;
- unpaid work or non-market contributions;
- unequal ability to pay;
- missing information or misleading quality signals;
- market power and barriers to entry;
- risks not understood or priced by participants;
- public benefits or long-term consequences that are difficult to capture.

A price increase can be evidence of a change in market conditions, but it is not a complete causal explanation. Investigators should test alternative mechanisms and use independent evidence before assigning causes.

## 16. Practical examples

### Food retailer
A shop's purchase price for a carton of goods differs from its shelf price, final transaction price, delivery cost and margin. A supplier price increase may shift the retailer's cost conditions, but whether the shop raises its price depends on competitors, customer demand, inventory, cash constraints and expectations. The price change alone does not establish the supplier's motive or the retailer's market power.

### Local service
A mechanic may quote a price, but the final transaction depends on the work scope, parts, warranty, delivery time and negotiation. A cheaper quote may omit necessary parts or provide different quality. Comparing offers requires matching scope and terms.

### Seasonal agricultural market
A harvest can increase physical availability and place downward pressure on prices, all else equal. But transport disruption, storage losses, export demand, fuel costs or market access can offset the effect. A local price movement alone cannot identify the cause.

### Foreign exchange
Two providers may display different currency rates. To compare them, match the currency pair, direction, amount, timestamp, fees, settlement speed and actual eligibility. A displayed spread does not prove that an arbitrage can be executed after costs and risk.

### Financial asset
A share price can rise after new information about future earnings or because trading conditions change. The price move is evidence of market repricing, not proof that the company has already increased production or that the market's expectations will be correct.

These examples are illustrative and do not assert current market conditions in Nigeria or elsewhere.

## 17. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Market vs marketplace | A market includes participants, rules, substitutes and exchange arrangements; a marketplace is often the venue. |
| Price vs value | Price is a monetary transaction or quote; value depends on the concept and valuation basis. |
| Price vs cost | Price is paid or received in exchange; cost concerns resources or expenditures under a defined boundary. |
| Quoted vs transaction price | An offer or indication is not necessarily a completed trade. |
| Nominal vs real price | Real price adjusts for a specified general price index or price basis. |
| General inflation vs relative-price change | The overall price level can rise while individual prices move differently. |
| Supply vs quantity supplied | Supply is a relationship across prices; quantity supplied is the amount at one price and set of conditions. |
| Demand vs quantity demanded | Demand is a relationship across prices; quantity demanded is the amount at one price and set of conditions. |
| Movement vs shift | Own-price change usually moves along a curve in the basic model; another determinant can shift the curve. |
| Equilibrium vs fairness | Market-clearing conditions do not automatically imply an equitable or socially optimal outcome. |
| Competition vs number of firms | Entry barriers, substitutes, switching costs and conduct also matter. |
| Concentration vs market power | Concentration can be evidence, but does not alone prove power or abuse. |
| Bid vs ask | Bid is a buyer's quoted price; ask is a seller's quoted price. |
| Trading volume vs liquidity | Volume over a period differs from the ability to trade promptly at limited price impact. |
| Financial price vs productive capacity | Asset repricing does not by itself establish a change in physical output or capacity. |

## 18. Measurement and evidence checklist

When analysing a market or price movement, ask:

1. What market is being studied, and what goods, services, claims, geography, participants and time period define it?
2. Is the observed number a posted price, quote, bid, ask, transaction price, average, index or modelled value?
3. Are the compared items equivalent in quality, grade, quantity, location, timing, tax, delivery and payment terms?
4. Is the price nominal or adjusted? Which price index or deflator and base period are used?
5. Are we discussing supply/demand relationships or observed quantities at a specific price?
6. What other determinants could have shifted supply or demand?
7. Are inventories, imports, transport, capacity, contracts, regulation or rationing affecting availability?
8. What evidence supports claims about competition, concentration, market power or bargaining?
9. For financial markets, are timestamps, trade sizes, fees, settlement, access and market impact matched?
10. Could externalities, information asymmetry or other market failures make price an incomplete measure of social cost or benefit?
11. Does the evidence establish a price movement, a plausible mechanism, or a causal explanation? These are different levels of claim.
12. Are sources independent, current enough for the question and based on consistent definitions?

## 20. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Markets are exchange arrangements and need not be physical venues.
- Price, cost and value answer different questions.
- Supply and demand are relationships; quantity supplied and quantity demanded are amounts at specific prices and conditions.
- Standard supply-and-demand curves depend on assumptions and do not identify the cause of every observed price movement.
- Equilibrium in a model is not proof of fairness or optimal social outcomes.
- Market concentration, price movement and trading volume each provide limited evidence and require context.
- Financial prices can change without a corresponding immediate change in physical production.

### Scope limits
- Market definitions and price concepts vary by question and institutional setting.
- Supply-and-demand models are analytical simplifications; real markets can involve contracts, rationing, sticky prices, market power, expectations and simultaneous shocks.
- This note does not estimate current Nigerian prices, market concentration, inflation, spreads or arbitrage opportunities.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which official Nigerian price indices and producer/consumer price series are appropriate for different questions?
- How are weights, sampling, quality adjustment, revisions and missing prices handled in official indices?
- Which Nigerian sectors have reliable public data on quantities, costs, market concentration and access?
- How should retail prices be compared when package size, quality, location and credit terms differ?
- Which market microstructure concepts are necessary for later FX and capital-market investigations?

## 21. Short summary

A market is an arrangement for exchange, not merely a physical place. Prices are monetary terms, while cost and value are different concepts. Supply and demand describe relationships; quantities supplied and demanded refer to specific prices and conditions. Prices can coordinate activity but are influenced by costs, scarcity, institutions, expectations, market power and transaction frictions. A price change alone does not reveal its cause or establish social value, productive growth, market abuse or an executable trading opportunity.

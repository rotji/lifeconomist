# Concept 4 — Costs and Prices

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concepts:** [Production](production.md); [Output and Intermediate Inputs](output-and-intermediate-inputs.md); [Economic Value, Usefulness and Exchange Value](economic-value-usefulness-and-exchange-value.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain what costs and prices mean, why they differ, how they influence decisions and production, and why no single cost or price figure answers every economic question.

## 1. Starting point

A **cost** is the value of resources used, committed, or given up to achieve an outcome, defined under a particular perspective and method. A **price** is the amount quoted, agreed, or observed for an exchange of a specified good, service, asset, or input.

They are related but not interchangeable. A seller's price is not necessarily the seller's cost; the buyer's price is not necessarily the buyer's full cost; and neither automatically measures the good's usefulness or total social cost.

Before interpreting a cost or price, ask:
- Cost or price **to whom**—buyer, seller, producer, household, government, or society?
- Which item, quantity, quality and period are covered?
- Is the number observed, recorded in accounts, estimated, or hypothetical?
- Does it include taxes, transport, fees, financing, time, risk or external effects?
- Is it an average, marginal, fixed, variable, nominal or inflation-adjusted measure?
- What decision is the number intended to inform?

## 2. Different meanings of cost

### Resource or economic cost

Economic analysis often asks what resources an activity uses and what alternatives are sacrificed by using them here rather than elsewhere. Resource costs may include labour time, materials, energy, land, machinery use, information and other scarce resources. A resource can have an opportunity cost even when no cash payment occurs.

### Accounting cost

An accounting cost is recorded under an accounting system and its recognition, classification and valuation rules. It may differ from an economic cost because accounting reports have defined purposes and may not record every forgone alternative or external effect. The treatment of costs depends on the applicable accounting standards and context.

### Explicit and implicit costs

- **Explicit costs** involve recorded or observable payments, such as wages paid, electricity bills or purchased materials.
- **Implicit costs** represent the value of resources used without a corresponding explicit payment in the same sense—for example, an owner's time or the use of a building the owner already owns. How these are measured depends on the analysis.

An implicit cost is not automatically a cash expense in the accounts. It can still matter when comparing alternatives.

### Opportunity cost

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the best forgone alternative when a resource or decision is committed to one use. It is not the sum of every possible alternative and is not necessarily money paid.

Example: a shop owner spends four hours doing stock checks instead of delivering goods. If the best realistic alternative use of those hours would have produced a net benefit of ₦3,000, that amount may illustrate the opportunity cost of the time. The figure is hypothetical and depends on the feasible alternative; it is not automatically an accounting expense.

### Private cost and social cost

A **private cost** is borne by the decision-maker or party being analyzed. A **social cost** considers costs borne across the relevant affected parties, including external effects where the analysis can defensibly identify them. Private and social costs can differ, but the size and direction of any difference require evidence. Do not assign a monetary value to an external effect without stating the method and assumptions.

## 3. Common cost classifications

| Cost concept | Meaning | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed cost | Cost that does not vary with output over a specified range and time period | Fixed does not mean permanent or unchanged in every circumstance |
| Variable cost | Cost that changes with output or activity over the relevant range | It may not change in a perfectly proportional way |
| Total cost | Sum of the cost categories included in a stated definition | State which costs are included |
| Average cost | Total cost divided by the quantity or output measure selected | Depends on the denominator and period |
| Marginal cost | Change in total cost associated with producing one additional unit, or a small additional amount of output | Must define the unit and production context |
| Direct cost | A cost traceable to a particular product, service, project or activity under the chosen accounting method | Traceability depends on the costing system |
| Indirect/overhead cost | A cost shared across products or activities rather than directly assigned to one unit | Allocation methods can change reported unit costs |
| Sunk cost | A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered by the current decision | Usually should not drive a forward-looking choice merely because it was paid |
| Avoidable cost | A cost that can be avoided if a specified option is not taken | Avoidability depends on the decision and time horizon |
| Incremental cost | Difference in cost between two alternatives or scenarios | Include all relevant differences, not only obvious cash payments |
| Replacement cost | Cost of replacing an asset or input under specified current conditions | Not necessarily equal to book value or historical purchase cost |
| Financing cost | Cost associated with obtaining and using finance, such as interest and fees | Treatment differs across accounting, investment and economic analyses |

These categories can overlap. A cost can be both fixed over a short horizon and avoidable over a longer horizon; an overhead cost may be fixed or variable; and an explicit cash payment can also be an opportunity cost if it commits a resource to one use.

## 4. Prices: what a quoted number tells us

A price should be tied to a specific exchange or quotation. It may be:
- an asking or offer price;
- a bid price;
- a negotiated transaction price;
- a wholesale, retail, producer or import price;
- a list price that no buyer actually pays;
- a contract price with particular delivery and payment terms;
- a market reference price or index;
- a regulated or administered price;
- a tax-inclusive or tax-exclusive price.

Two quoted prices are not comparable until quantity, quality, location, delivery terms, date, currency, taxes, fees and payment terms are checked. A listed price may not be an executable price; a reference or midpoint price may not be the price at which a particular buyer can transact.

### Price versus cost

For a retailer, the purchase price of goods is one component of the retailer's costs. The final selling price may also reflect rent, wages, transport, spoilage, financing, fees, taxes, competitive conditions and the retailer's intended margin. The final price is not guaranteed to cover all costs, and the difference between selling price and purchase price is not automatically profit.

For a household, the posted price may not represent the full cost of obtaining a product if transport, waiting time, installation, maintenance or financing are material. These should be included only when relevant to the question and measured transparently.

## 5. A simple retailer example

Suppose a shop buys one item for ₦8,000 and sells it for ₦10,000.

- Purchase price: ₦8,000.
- Selling price: ₦10,000.
- Gross difference before other costs: ₦2,000.

The ₦2,000 is not necessarily net profit. The shop may incur transport, packaging, payment fees, rent, staff costs, spoilage or theft, and other expenses. Some costs may be shared across many products and require allocation. Timing also matters: the shop may sell on credit or pay the supplier later.

The example also does not establish whether ₦10,000 is the best price. That depends on customer demand, competing products, quality, availability, price sensitivity, inventory risk, and the seller's objectives and constraints.

All figures here are hypothetical and are used only to distinguish concepts.

## 6. Average cost and marginal cost

Average cost and marginal cost answer different questions.

- **Average cost** asks how much cost is attributed to each unit on average under the chosen definition: total cost divided by the number of units.
- **Marginal cost** asks how much additional cost arises from producing an additional unit or amount of output.

Illustration: a small producer incurs total cost of ₦50,000 to produce 100 units. The average cost is ₦500 per unit. If producing one additional unit raises total cost from ₦50,000 to ₦50,350, the marginal cost of that additional unit is ₦350 under this simplified discrete example.

The average cost of ₦500 does not mean the next unit costs ₦500 to produce. Conversely, the marginal cost of ₦350 does not mean all units cost ₦350. These values are illustrative, and real cost curves depend on capacity, input prices, production technology, quality and the time horizon.

## 7. Fixed costs, variable costs and the time horizon

A rent payment may be fixed with respect to how many items a shop sells this week. Over a longer horizon, the shop may be able to move premises or renegotiate the lease. A worker's salary may be fixed over a short period but change if staffing or working hours change. Electricity costs may include a fixed connection charge and a usage-related component.

Therefore, “fixed” and “variable” are not intrinsic labels that apply independently of context. They depend on:
- the time period;
- the range of output or activity;
- the contract and technology;
- the decision being considered;
- which costs are included.

For short-run production decisions, some resources are fixed. Over longer horizons, more inputs may be adjustable. A sound analysis states the relevant time horizon rather than assuming all costs can be changed immediately.

## 8. Sunk costs and forward-looking decisions

A **sunk cost** has already been incurred and cannot be recovered through the current choice. If a shop has already paid for an advertisement that cannot be refunded, that payment is sunk for the decision about whether to run a separate new campaign tomorrow.

The original spending may matter for evaluating past performance or accountability, but it should not automatically determine the next decision. The forward-looking choice should compare the future costs and benefits of available alternatives.

Not every past expenditure is sunk: a refundable deposit, resalable inventory, or asset that can be redeployed may have recoverable value. Determine what can actually be recovered rather than labelling all historical spending as sunk.

## 9. Price formation: supply, demand and institutions

In many markets, prices emerge from interactions among buyers and sellers. Supply reflects producers' willingness and ability to offer goods or services at different prices; demand reflects buyers' willingness and ability to purchase at different prices. The price and quantity observed depend on both sides, as well as market structure and institutions.

Price formation may be affected by:
- scarcity and production conditions;
- buyer preferences, income and available substitutes;
- competition and market concentration;
- bargaining power and information asymmetry;
- transport, storage, search and transaction costs;
- taxes, subsidies, regulation and administered prices;
- seasonality, expectations, uncertainty and contractual arrangements;
- market access, credit availability and payment risk.

The introductory supply-and-demand model is useful, but it is not a universal claim that every observed price is formed in a frictionless competitive market. Some markets have few sellers, regulated prices, long-term contracts, rationing, informal bargaining or limited information.

## 10. Marginal cost is not automatically the market price

Under particular assumptions, competitive firms' supply decisions are connected to marginal costs. But observed prices need not equal a particular firm's marginal cost. Market power, capacity constraints, taxes, subsidies, fixed costs, product differentiation, regulation, contracts and short-run shocks can affect the relationship.

A price above marginal cost is not by itself proof of excessive profit or wrongdoing. A price below average total cost is not by itself proof of an irrational decision; a firm may be covering variable costs in the short run, clearing inventory, meeting a contract or pursuing another objective. Interpretation requires the relevant time horizon and cost definition.

Likewise, a firm's accounting cost is not enough by itself to predict market price. Prices depend on demand, competition, alternatives and institutional conditions as well as costs.

## 11. Real and nominal prices

A **nominal price** is expressed in the currency amount at the time of observation. A **real price** adjusts a nominal price using a stated price index to compare purchasing power or prices across time. The appropriate deflator depends on the question; a broad consumer price index may not represent the cost change for a particular input or household.

If the price of an item rises from ₦1,000 to ₦1,200, the nominal increase is 20%. Whether its real price rose by 20%, rose by less, or fell depends on the relevant change in the price level used for adjustment. No real-price conclusion can be drawn without choosing an appropriate deflator and period.

For comparisons, record the currency, date, unit, quality, market and price-index method. Exchange-rate movements may also matter when comparing imported goods or prices expressed in different currencies.

## 12. Cost, price, value and profit: a comparison

| Concept | Main question |
|---|---|
| Usefulness/value to a person | What benefit or function does it provide in context? |
| Cost | What resources are used or sacrificed under the chosen perspective and method? |
| Opportunity cost | What is the value of the best forgone alternative? |
| Price | What amount is quoted or exchanged for the specified item? |
| Revenue | What amount is recognized as revenue under the applicable accounting basis? |
| Gross margin | What remains after deducting the defined cost of goods sold or direct input measure from sales/revenue? Exact definitions vary by context |
| Profit | What remains under a specified profit measure after the costs and accounting items included in that measure? |
| Value added | Output less intermediate consumption under national-accounting rules |
| Social cost | Costs borne by the relevant affected parties under a stated scope, including external effects where properly assessed |

A single transaction may be described using several of these concepts, but each requires its own definition. The terms should not be used as substitutes.

## 13. Practical decision checklist

When using a cost or price figure in an analysis, record:

1. **Perspective:** Whose cost or price is it?
2. **Object and unit:** What is being purchased, produced, or compared?
3. **Time:** Which date or period?
4. **Basis:** Accounting, economic, quoted, negotiated, or actual transaction value?
5. **Scope:** Which taxes, fees, delivery, financing, labour/time and external effects are included?
6. **Classification:** Fixed/variable, direct/indirect, sunk/avoidable, average/marginal, nominal/real?
7. **Counterfactual:** What alternative is being compared, if opportunity or incremental cost is claimed?
8. **Market context:** What competitors, substitutes, constraints and rules matter?
9. **Evidence quality:** Is the number observed, reported, estimated or hypothetical?
10. **Uncertainty:** What could materially change the result?

## 14. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Production and output:** Costs concern resources used; output concerns what is produced. Purchases, input use and production may occur in different periods.
- **Economic value and usefulness:** A price is not a complete measure of usefulness, willingness to pay or social welfare.
- **Revenue, income and receipts:** Price and quantity may inform sales value, but revenue recognition and cash timing require separate treatment.
- **Value added and profit:** Costs enter different calculations differently. Intermediate consumption, employee compensation, depreciation, financing costs and taxes must not be collapsed into one undifferentiated cost total.
- **Workstream B:** Formal price indices, deflators and cost indicators require explicit units, coverage, sources and methodology.
- **Workstream C:** Observed price changes can be monitored, but comparisons require comparable products, quality, dates, markets and units.
- **Workstream D:** Invoices, price lists, purchase records and observed transactions can be footprints, but no single record proves a complete cost structure or the cause of a price change.
- **Workstream E:** Cost/price analysis may support business or intelligence opportunities, but examples in this note do not establish customer demand or commercial viability.

## 15. Evidence status and limitations

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Cost and price answer different questions.
- Opportunity cost concerns a forgone alternative and need not equal an explicit payment.
- Average and marginal cost are different measures.
- Fixed and variable cost classifications depend on the time horizon and range of activity.
- A price observed in one transaction does not by itself reveal total cost, profit, usefulness or social cost.

### Illustrative only
- All naira figures and examples are hypothetical.
- The retailer example does not estimate actual Nigerian retail margins.
- The examples do not establish any business's optimal price, cost curve or profitability.

### Questions to revisit
- Which cost categories should be used for different Lifeconomist business and household applications?
- How should opportunity cost be estimated when alternatives are uncertain or not monetized?
- Which price indices are appropriate for different products, households and business inputs?
- How should informal-market price observations be validated and compared?
- When is a social-cost estimate sufficiently evidenced for a decision?

## 16. Short summary

Cost describes resources used or sacrificed under a specified perspective; price describes an amount quoted or exchanged. Accounting cost, economic cost, opportunity cost and social cost are not identical. Fixed, variable, average, marginal, sunk and incremental costs answer different questions and depend on the decision and time horizon. Prices are influenced by supply, demand, competition, institutions and frictions, not just a seller's costs. Always specify what the figure measures and what it excludes.

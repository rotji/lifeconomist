# Concept 10 — Credit, Debt, Banking and Financial Intermediation

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational concept note; institutional details depend on jurisdiction and accounting framework  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Money, Wealth, Capital and Financial Resources](money-wealth-capital-and-financial-resources.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain credit, debt, lending, deposits, bank balance sheets, money creation, interest, repayment and credit allocation while distinguishing accounting mechanisms from economic outcomes.

## 1. The central distinction

**Credit** is an arrangement that allows a person or institution to obtain funds, goods, services or purchasing power now in exchange for payment or performance later. **Debt** is an obligation to repay money or otherwise meet a liability. Credit describes the arrangement or provision of credit; debt describes the obligation from the debtor's perspective.

A loan is a common form of credit, but credit can also include supplier trade credit, credit-card borrowing, bonds, overdrafts and other arrangements. An approved but unused credit limit is not the same as outstanding debt.

Credit is not identical to money, although commercial-bank lending can create bank deposits and increase commonly measured deposit money. Debt is not automatically wealth creation: a borrower may receive cash while incurring an offsetting liability. Nor does every loan finance productive investment; it may finance consumption, working capital, asset purchases, refinancing or public spending.

## 2. Core terms

- **Lender / creditor:** provides credit or holds a claim for repayment.
- **Borrower / debtor:** receives credit and owes repayment.
- **Principal:** the amount borrowed or outstanding before interest and other charges, as defined by the contract.
- **Interest:** the contractual price paid for the use of funds or return received by the lender.
- **Repayment:** payment that reduces principal, pays interest or fees, or settles another obligation. Track principal and interest separately.
- **Credit limit:** the maximum amount that may be borrowed under a facility, not necessarily the amount borrowed.
- **Outstanding balance:** the amount currently owed under a stated definition.
- **Collateral:** an asset or right pledged to support an obligation. It may support recovery but does not eliminate credit risk.
- **Default:** failure to meet an obligation as defined by contract, law or reporting framework.
- **Credit risk:** the risk that a borrower or counterparty will not perform as agreed.
- **Maturity:** the date an obligation falls due; a facility may have multiple repayment dates.
- **Refinancing:** replacing or restructuring existing financing. It can change cost, maturity and risk without creating equivalent new productive investment.

## 3. Debt is a stock; borrowing and repayment are flows

Debt outstanding is a **stock** measured at a point in time. New borrowing, principal repayment and interest payments are flows over a period; write-offs, accrued interest, reclassification and valuation or exchange-rate changes may also affect reported balances.

A simplified relationship is:

**Ending debt ≈ beginning debt + new borrowing − principal repaid − debt written off ± other changes**

This is a conceptual aid, not a universal accounting formula.

**Example:** A business starts with ₦1,000,000 of outstanding principal, borrows another ₦200,000, and repays ₦150,000 of principal. Ignoring fees, accrued interest, write-offs and other changes, its ending principal is ₦1,050,000. Interest paid is a separate cash outflow and is not subtracted from principal merely because it was paid.

## 4. The loan transaction and balance sheets

A loan creates linked positions:
- The borrower receives funds or purchasing power and incurs a repayment obligation.
- The lender acquires a claim for principal and possibly interest.
- If funds are disbursed into a bank account, the borrower's deposit balance may rise.
- The lender records the loan asset and the relevant funding or settlement effects.

When a non-bank lender transfers an existing deposit to a borrower, the borrower's deposit may rise while the lender's falls. This transfer alone need not increase aggregate deposit money. The loan asset and debt liability arise between the parties.

When a commercial bank grants a loan by crediting a deposit, the mechanics differ. The bank records a loan asset and a matching deposit liability. Each party's accounting perspective matters: an asset for one party may be a liability for another. Transactions can change balance sheets without creating equivalent net financial wealth for the economy as a whole.

## 5. How commercial-bank lending can create deposit money

In a typical modern banking system, when a commercial bank grants a loan and credits the borrower's deposit:
- **Bank asset:** a loan receivable from the borrower.
- **Bank liability:** a deposit owed to the borrower.
- **Borrower asset:** a bank deposit.
- **Borrower liability:** the loan owed to the bank.

This is a key way commercial-bank lending creates deposit money. It is not best described as a bank simply taking an unchanged pile of a particular customer's deposits and handing those exact deposits to the borrower.

This does **not** mean banks can lend without limit. Lending depends on creditworthy demand, expected profitability, borrower risk, capital requirements, liquidity and funding management, regulation, collateral and legal rules, payment outflows, operational capacity, and macroeconomic and monetary conditions. Banks must also settle payments to other banks.

The accounting mechanism is distinct from the decision to lend and from the eventual use of the loan. Deposits may be spent or transferred; loans may be repaid, written off or transferred.

## 6. Deposits, reserves and settlement

**Customer deposits** are liabilities of commercial banks to their customers. **Central-bank reserves** are balances that eligible financial institutions hold at the central bank. Reserves play a role in interbank settlement and monetary-policy implementation under the relevant system. Households and most ordinary businesses do not ordinarily hold reserve accounts directly at the central bank.

If a customer of Bank A pays a customer of Bank B, the payer's deposit at Bank A falls and the recipient's deposit at Bank B rises. The banks settle the interbank obligation through the relevant payment system, which may involve central-bank reserves or other settlement assets. The exact process varies by country and payment rail.

If a bank creates a deposit through lending and the borrower pays someone at another bank, the lending bank may experience a settlement outflow. It must manage that outflow through liquidity, funding, asset management and access to payment-system facilities. Deposit creation does not remove the need to manage funding and liquidity.

## 7. What happens when a loan is repaid?

When principal is repaid to the commercial bank holding the loan, the bank's loan asset and the borrower's debt liability decline. If payment comes from a deposit at that bank, the bank's deposit liability also declines. In this simplified case, repayment reverses part of the original balance-sheet expansion and reduces deposit money.

Actual arrangements can be more complex: loans may be sold, refinanced, securitized, repaid from another bank, written off or subject to fees and accrued interest. Interest generally represents income to the lender and an expense to the borrower, subject to the applicable accounting framework. Debt forgiveness or write-off is not the same as cash repayment.

## 8. Credit creation is not automatically net wealth creation

A loan can increase a borrower's deposit balance and create a bank asset, but it also creates a liability for the borrower. At origination, borrowing does not automatically increase net worth by the full amount borrowed when measured consistently.

If the borrower uses the loan to buy a machine, it may exchange a deposit for a non-financial asset while retaining the debt. Net worth then depends on valuation, costs and later outcomes. Whether capacity, productivity or welfare improves depends on asset selection and use.

A loan for consumption provides purchasing power now but must be serviced from future resources. A working-capital loan may bridge the time between paying suppliers and collecting sales receipts. Refinancing can change maturity or interest cost without creating equivalent new productive investment.

Financial claims and liabilities between domestic sectors often offset when sectors are consolidated. Credit nevertheless matters because it changes who controls purchasing power, when spending occurs, which projects proceed and how risk is distributed.

## 9. Interest and borrowing costs

**Interest** is the contractual payment for credit, commonly expressed as an interest rate. Comparing loans requires more than comparing a headline percentage.

- **Nominal interest rate:** quoted rate before adjustment for inflation.
- **Real interest rate:** inflation-adjusted concept; ex-ante estimates use expected inflation, while ex-post measures use observed inflation under a stated method.
- **Fixed rate:** remains unchanged over the defined fixed-rate period.
- **Variable/floating rate:** changes under a benchmark or contractual formula.
- **Simple versus compound interest:** different calculation methods; contract terms determine which applies.
- **Effective borrowing cost:** may include interest, fees and mandatory charges under the relevant comparison method.
- **Debt service:** scheduled principal and interest payments and, depending on the definition, other debt-related outflows.

A lower headline rate may not mean a cheaper loan if fees, penalties, repayment timing, collateral costs or variable-rate exposure differ. Disclosure rules and annual-percentage-rate methods vary by jurisdiction.

## 10. Credit risk, collateral and underwriting

Lenders assess whether a borrower is likely and able to repay. Depending on the product, they may examine income or cash flow, debt burden, credit history, collateral, business conditions, loan purpose, maturity and legal enforceability.

Collateral may fall in value, take time to sell or be difficult to enforce. A secured loan can still default and cause losses. **Creditworthiness** is not identical to wealth: a wealthy borrower may lack timely cash flow, while a borrower with few assets may have reliable income but little collateral. Credit scores, where used, are only one input.

Credit involves information asymmetry: borrowers may know more about their circumstances or projects than lenders. Screening, monitoring, covenants, collateral and relationship information can help manage this problem but cannot eliminate uncertainty.

## 11. Credit allocation and economic outcomes

Credit allocation directs financing among borrowers and uses. Credit can support household purchases, SME inventory and working capital, productive equipment, government financing, trade and refinancing.

Outcomes depend on purpose, terms, timing and context. Productive lending can support output when projects are viable and complementary inputs exist. Poorly allocated credit can support unsustainable debt, speculative booms or projects whose returns cannot cover financing costs. Credit can also be rationed away from useful borrowers when lenders lack information, collateral, funding or risk appetite.

A rise in credit does not by itself prove productive investment, economic growth or improved welfare. More borrowing may reflect opportunity, distress, inflation, refinancing or changing credit supply. Purpose and outcome require additional evidence.

## 12. Leverage and debt sustainability

**Leverage** describes the use of borrowed funds or the relationship between debt and another balance-sheet or income measure. It can magnify gains but also losses and fixed payment obligations.

Debt sustainability depends on more than the debt amount. Consider debt relative to income, revenue, assets or repayment capacity; interest rates; maturity and refinancing needs; currency denomination; cash-flow volatility; asset quality and liquidity; guarantees and contingent liabilities; and economic conditions.

Debt-to-income, debt-to-equity, interest-coverage and debt-service ratios answer different questions. The denominator, time period, sector and accounting definition must be explicit. Government analysis may also need to distinguish domestic from external debt, local-currency from foreign-currency obligations, central government from general government, gross from net debt, and debt stock from annual deficit and debt service.

## 13. Banks as financial intermediaries

Banks connect depositors, borrowers, payment users and markets. They commonly transform:
- **Maturity:** shorter-term liabilities support longer-term assets.
- **Liquidity:** transferable or withdrawable claims coexist with less-liquid loans.
- **Risk:** underwriting and portfolio diversification redistribute, but do not eliminate, risks.
- **Information:** screening and monitoring help assess borrowers.
- **Payments:** deposits and settlement services support transactions.

These functions expose banks to credit, liquidity, market, operational and legal risks. Banks operate under prudential regulation and supervision, whose details vary by jurisdiction. Other intermediaries—such as microfinance institutions, finance companies, credit unions, bond markets and trade-credit networks—operate with different liabilities, risks and regulatory regimes.

## 14. Examples across sectors

### Household
A household borrows ₦500,000 to pay school fees. Funds available now rise and a repayment obligation is created. This may address an immediate need, but future debt service reduces money available for other uses. Borrowing is not automatically income or a net-wealth gain.

### Small retailer
A retailer receives inventory on 30-day supplier credit. This is credit even though there is no bank loan. Inventory and the payable may rise; sales receipts must arrive in time to meet the obligation. The main risk may be cash-flow timing.

### Commercial bank
A bank approves and disburses a ₦2 million loan by crediting a customer's deposit. In the simplified initial entry, the bank records a loan asset and a deposit liability. If the customer pays a supplier at another bank, settlement and liquidity management become relevant. Approval alone is not the same as disbursement or the full amount being outstanding.

### Government
A government issues a bond. Investors acquire a financial claim and the government incurs a liability. Proceeds may finance infrastructure, current spending, refinancing or other purposes. The bond issue alone does not prove the final use or the outcome.

All figures are hypothetical, not Nigerian credit statistics or borrowing advice.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Credit vs money | Credit is an arrangement to obtain value now and settle later; money serves payment, accounting and store-of-value functions. |
| Credit limit vs debt outstanding | Approved capacity is not the amount actually borrowed. |
| Debt stock vs borrowing flow | Debt is measured at a date; borrowing is measured over a period. |
| Principal vs interest | Principal is the amount owed; interest is the contractual cost or return. |
| Approval vs disbursement | Approval authorizes a facility under conditions; disbursement transfers funds or makes credit available as defined. |
| Deposits vs reserves | Deposits are commercial-bank liabilities to customers; reserves are balances eligible institutions hold at the central bank. |
| Deposit creation vs net wealth creation | A loan may create a deposit and matching debt; the deposit alone does not imply an equal net-wealth gain. |
| Lending vs productive investment | A loan can fund many purposes; outcomes depend on actual use and results. |
| Liquidity vs funding | Liquidity concerns payment capacity and asset conversion; funding concerns sources and structure of finance. |
| Collateral vs repayment capacity | Collateral may support recovery but does not guarantee cash flow or prevent default. |
| Refinancing vs new investment | Refinancing changes existing financing; it need not add productive assets. |
| Debt vs deficit | Debt is a stock; a deficit is a flow shortfall over a period under a defined measure. |

## 16. Measurement checklist

When interpreting a credit or debt claim, ask:

1. Who is the borrower and who is the creditor?
2. Is it a proposed facility, approved limit, commitment, disbursement or outstanding balance?
3. What instrument is involved: loan, bond, overdraft, supplier credit, lease, credit card or another arrangement?
4. Is the number a stock at a date or a flow over a period?
5. Does it include accrued interest, fees, arrears, guarantees or contingent liabilities?
6. Are principal, interest, fees and debt service separated?
7. What are the currency, maturity, interest-rate basis and repayment schedule?
8. Is the debt secured, and what are the limitations of the collateral?
9. Can the borrower repay from cash flow, or does repayment depend on refinancing or asset sales?
10. For a bank, are loan assets, customer deposits, reserves, capital and funding distinguished?
11. What was the use of proceeds, and is there evidence of actual expenditure, delivery and productive use?
12. Are definitions, sector boundaries and reporting dates consistent?

## 18. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Credit arrangements and debt obligations are related but distinct.
- Debt is a stock; borrowing and repayment are flows, while write-offs and other changes can affect balances.
- Principal repayment differs from interest payment.
- Commercial-bank lending can create deposit money through a loan asset and matching deposit liability.
- Deposit creation does not imply unlimited lending or remove constraints from capital, liquidity, settlement, regulation, risk and creditworthy demand.
- Customer deposits and central-bank reserves are different instruments.
- More credit does not automatically mean more productive investment or greater net wealth.

### Scope limits
- Banking and payment-system mechanics differ by jurisdiction, institution and instrument.
- Simplified entries omit fees, taxes, provisioning, capital rules, settlement timing and detailed accounting treatments.
- No Nigerian credit aggregate, lending rate, default rate or debt-sustainability measure is estimated here.

- How should the project distinguish announced facilities, approved loans, disbursements, outstanding credit and actual use of proceeds?
- Which debt-sustainability indicators suit households, SMEs, banks and government respectively?

## 19. Short summary

Credit allows value or purchasing power to be obtained now with obligations or settlement later; debt is the corresponding obligation from the debtor's perspective. Debt stock differs from borrowing and repayment flows, and principal differs from interest. Commercial-bank lending can create deposits, but banks remain constrained by risk, capital, liquidity, settlement, funding, regulation and credit demand. Credit can support consumption, working capital, asset purchases or productive investment; purpose and outcome cannot be inferred from a loan balance alone.

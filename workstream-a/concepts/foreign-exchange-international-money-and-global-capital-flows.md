# Concept 17 — Foreign Exchange, International Money and Global Capital Flows

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal external-sector indicators and Nigerian data validation remain in Workstream B  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 16 — Inflation, Interest Rates and Purchasing Power](inflation-interest-rates-and-purchasing-power.md)

## 1. Purpose

Foreign exchange connects domestic prices, trade, investment, debt, savings, financial markets and international balance sheets. Yet several terms commonly used together describe different things: an exchange rate is a price between currencies; a currency's purchasing power concerns what it buys; a cross-border flow is a transaction during a period; and an external asset or liability is a position measured at a point in time.

This note builds the conceptual vocabulary for later studying international capital movements and Nigeria's external position. It does not report current Nigerian exchange rates, reserves, foreign investment, debt or balance-of-payments values. Workstream B remains responsible for formal series definitions, source selection and calculations.

## 2. Currency, foreign exchange and exchange rates

A **currency** is a monetary unit used under a defined legal and institutional framework. **Foreign exchange (FX)** usually refers to foreign-currency money, claims or transactions, and the market in which currencies are exchanged.

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency expressed in units of another. The quote convention must be explicit. If a quote is Nigerian naira per US dollar (NGN per USD), it states how many naira are exchanged for one dollar. If the quote is dollars per naira, the numerical direction is reversed.

For example, if a hypothetical quote moves from NGN 1,000 per USD to NGN 1,100 per USD, more naira are needed to buy one dollar: the naira has depreciated against the dollar under this quote convention. This example is illustrative, not a current rate.

An exchange rate is a relative price between two currencies. It does not alone measure a currency's value against every good, service or currency, nor does it directly reveal a country's wealth, productivity or welfare.

## 3. Currency value and purchasing power

The phrase **currency value** is ambiguous unless the reference is specified. It may mean:
- value relative to another currency (an exchange rate);
- domestic purchasing power over goods and services;
- international purchasing power relative to a basket of foreign goods and services;
- a market valuation of currency claims under particular conditions.

Domestic purchasing power is affected by domestic prices. If the domestic price level rises while nominal income or cash holdings remain unchanged, their purchasing power falls relative to that basket. The exchange rate can also affect the local-currency cost of imports, but domestic inflation and the exchange rate are not identical measures and need not move one-for-one.

A currency may appreciate against one currency and depreciate against another. It may strengthen nominally while its domestic purchasing power declines because domestic prices rise. Always identify the comparison: against which currency or basket, over what period, and under what measure?

## 4. Appreciation, depreciation, revaluation and devaluation

**Appreciation** means a currency's market value rises relative to another currency under a stated quote convention; **depreciation** means it falls. These terms commonly describe movements in market-determined exchange rates.

**Revaluation** and **devaluation** usually refer to an official upward or downward adjustment of a currency's declared parity or central value under a fixed or managed exchange-rate arrangement. Usage can vary, so describe the actual policy regime and action rather than relying on the label alone.

Under an NGN-per-USD quote:
- a fall in the quote means fewer naira buy one dollar and therefore naira appreciation against the dollar;
- a rise in the quote means more naira buy one dollar and therefore naira depreciation against the dollar.

Under the inverse USD-per-NGN quote, the numerical movement runs in the opposite direction. This is why any claim that a currency “rose” or “fell” should state the quotation convention.

Exchange-rate movement can reflect many interacting forces: relative interest rates, inflation expectations, trade and income flows, portfolio allocation, risk perceptions, central-bank operations, capital controls, market liquidity, fiscal expectations and global financial conditions. A single observed movement does not establish which factor caused it.

## 5. Bilateral, nominal effective and real effective exchange rates

A **bilateral exchange rate** compares two currencies, such as NGN/USD. It is simple and useful for transactions priced in that currency pair, but it cannot summarize all of a country's trading or financial relationships.

A **nominal effective exchange rate (NEER)** is typically a trade-weighted or otherwise weighted index of a currency against a group of partner currencies. Its coverage, weights, construction and index convention depend on the publisher. It summarizes a basket relationship rather than a single bilateral rate.

A **real effective exchange rate (REER)** adjusts an effective exchange-rate measure for relative price or cost changes between the home economy and partners. Depending on the index convention, a rise can be interpreted as real appreciation, but users must check the publisher's definition and direction. Real effective rates can help assess relative price or cost competitiveness, but they are not a direct, complete measure of export performance, productivity or welfare.

NEER and REER comparisons require attention to weights, base period, price deflator, partner coverage, frequency, revisions and index direction. Different institutions can publish different measures without necessarily contradicting one another if methods differ.

## 6. Spot, forward and other FX transactions

A **spot exchange rate** applies to a foreign-exchange transaction settled under the relevant market's spot convention. Settlement may not be instantaneous; the convention depends on currency pair and market rules.

A **forward exchange rate** is agreed today for an exchange of currencies on a specified future date. A forward contract can help manage uncertainty about future conversion costs. Its rate is not automatically a forecast of the future spot rate; it is shaped by the spot rate, interest-rate differentials, market pricing and contract conditions.

FX transactions can also include swaps, options and other derivatives. A currency swap or FX swap has contract-specific legs and settlement arrangements; it should not be confused with a simple spot purchase. Derivatives can hedge exposure or create exposure, depending on how they are used.

A forward rate's relationship to spot rates and interest rates depends on currency, maturity, collateral, funding and market conventions. Do not infer a directional forecast from a forward premium or discount without considering these factors.

## 7. Currency denomination, residence and nationality

International economic statistics classify transactions and positions according to formal rules. Three distinctions are especially important:

- **Currency denomination:** the currency in which a claim, contract or liability is expressed.
- **Residence:** the economic territory with which an institutional unit has its centre of predominant economic interest under the applicable statistical standard.
- **Nationality or ownership:** the legal or ultimate ownership relationship, which may differ from residence.

A Nigerian-resident firm can owe a debt denominated in US dollars. A foreign-owned company can be resident in Nigeria for national-accounting purposes. A Nigerian citizen living abroad is not automatically treated as a Nigerian resident for every external-statistics purpose. Exact treatment depends on the applicable standards and institutional facts.

These distinctions matter because a domestic-currency liability can be owed to a non-resident, and a foreign-currency liability can be owed to a resident. “Foreign currency,” “foreign-owned,” “external” and “offshore” are not interchangeable.

## 8. Trade, income and financial flows

Cross-border transactions include more than buying and selling physical goods. They can involve services, wages, investment income, transfers, lending, borrowing, equity purchases, deposits and derivative contracts.

The **current account** in the balance of payments broadly records transactions in goods, services, primary income and secondary income between residents and non-residents. The exact categories follow the applicable international statistical framework.

The **capital account** has a narrower formal meaning in balance-of-payments statistics than everyday phrases such as “capital flows”: it covers specified capital transfers and transactions in non-produced, non-financial assets.

The **financial account** records transactions in financial assets and liabilities between residents and non-residents. Categories commonly include direct investment, portfolio investment, financial derivatives and employee stock options, other investment, and reserve assets, subject to the reporting framework.

Everyday discussion often uses “capital flows” broadly for cross-border financial transactions. When precision matters, specify whether the term means the full financial account, a subset such as portfolio flows, direct investment, bank flows, or a broader private-sector estimate.

## 9. Gross flows, net flows and two-way transactions

A **gross inflow** and a **gross outflow** measure transactions in opposite directions under a stated perspective and accounting convention. A **net flow** is a balance between defined gross components. It can hide large two-way movements.

For example, if residents acquire 100 units of foreign assets while non-residents acquire 80 units of domestic assets, a net statistic may show a small balance even though gross transactions in both directions were substantial. The precise balance-of-payments sign convention must be checked before interpreting a reported number. This example is illustrative.

Netting can be useful for showing a balance, but it can obscure investor types, maturities, currencies, instruments and risk exposures. A small net flow does not imply little cross-border activity, and a large gross flow does not necessarily mean that the country gained an equivalent amount of net wealth.

Always specify whose perspective is used, which account or instrument is covered, the sign convention, and whether the figure is gross or net.

## 10. Flow versus stock: the central distinction

A **flow** is measured over a period, such as new direct investment during a quarter, exports over a year, or interest paid in a month.

A **stock** or **position** is measured at a point in time, such as the value of external debt at quarter-end, foreign assets held by residents, or reserve assets on a given date.

Flows can change stocks, but they do not fully explain stock changes. A simplified reconciliation is:

`Closing position = Opening position + transactions + valuation changes + other volume changes/reclassifications`

Valuation changes can result from exchange rates or market prices. Other changes may include write-offs, reclassifications and changes in coverage. The exact categories and treatment depend on the statistical framework.

If the dollar value of a foreign asset changes because its market price rises, that change is not necessarily a new cross-border investment flow. Similarly, a currency depreciation can raise the domestic-currency value of foreign-currency debt without any new borrowing. A change in the reported stock therefore must not automatically be described as new money entering or leaving the country.

## 11. Direct investment, portfolio investment and other investment

**Direct investment** generally describes a cross-border investment relationship involving a lasting interest and a significant degree of influence in an enterprise, as defined by the applicable international standard. The commonly used statistical threshold is a benchmark, not a complete description of every control relationship; follow the source's formal definition.

**Portfolio investment** generally covers cross-border holdings of equity and debt securities that are not classified as direct investment or reserve assets under the applicable framework. Portfolio positions can change quickly with market conditions and investor decisions, but actual liquidity and reversibility depend on the instrument and market.

**Other investment** is a residual functional category that commonly includes deposits, loans, trade credit, currency and deposits, and other specified claims and liabilities. Its exact content depends on the reporting standard.

**Reserve assets** are external assets controlled by monetary authorities and available to meet balance-of-payments financing needs, intervene in exchange markets or serve related official purposes, subject to the formal definition. Not every foreign asset held by a public institution qualifies as an official reserve asset.

These categories describe statistical functions, not necessarily the motives or social effects of a transaction. A direct-investment entry does not automatically prove new productive capacity, technology transfer or net job creation. A portfolio flow does not automatically indicate speculation or instability. The transaction, instrument, counterparty and subsequent outcomes need to be examined.

## 12. Balance of payments and international investment position

The **balance of payments (BOP)** summarizes transactions between residents and non-residents over a period. It is an accounting framework with linked accounts, not simply a record of cash entering and leaving through banks.

The **international investment position (IIP)** records the value of residents' external financial assets and liabilities at a point in time. The difference between external assets and liabilities is the net international investment position (NIIP), under the applicable definitions.

The BOP is primarily a flow statement; the IIP is a stock statement. The IIP changes because of financial transactions, price and exchange-rate changes, and other changes in volume or classification. The BOP and IIP should be read together when asking how a country's external balance sheet evolves.

The NIIP is not identical to national wealth, government net worth or foreign-exchange reserves. It captures a specified set of external financial positions and does not include every domestic asset, non-financial asset or liability. A negative NIIP does not by itself prove imminent crisis; composition, maturity, currency, income flows, institutions and resilience matter.

## 13. Foreign-exchange reserves

Official **reserve assets** are a defined category of external assets held and controlled by monetary authorities. Common components can include monetary gold, reserve positions at the IMF, SDR holdings, and foreign-currency deposits or securities meeting the relevant criteria. Exact composition and valuation depend on the official series.

Gross reserves, net reserves, usable reserves and adequacy measures are not interchangeable. “Net reserves” can have different definitions depending on which liabilities, swaps, encumbrances or other adjustments are deducted. “Usable” reserves depends on access, liquidity, legal constraints and the measure's methodology.

A reserve figure must be read with its valuation date, currency conversion, gross/net definition, asset coverage, encumbrances and associated short-term obligations. A rise in reserves can reflect transactions, valuation changes, borrowing or other factors; the headline movement alone does not establish the source or sustainability of the change.

Reserves can support external payments and market operations, but the adequacy question depends on the exchange-rate regime, external financing needs, short-term liabilities, import coverage, capital-flow exposure and policy context. No single rule is appropriate for every economy or regime.

## 14. Exchange rates, trade and pass-through

A depreciation can make imports more expensive in local currency and exports less expensive to foreign buyers, all else equal. But trade volumes and competitiveness do not necessarily adjust immediately or proportionately.

Outcomes depend on invoicing currency, contracts, input import dependence, inventories, pricing power, capacity, demand elasticities, transport costs, trade finance, tariffs and how quickly buyers and sellers respond. Firms that import inputs may face higher costs even if they export. Contracts and supply-chain relationships can delay the effect.

A depreciation can increase the domestic-currency value of foreign-currency debt and debt service. It may also increase the domestic-currency value of foreign-currency assets or export receipts. Net effects depend on each entity's currency-matched assets, liabilities, income and expenses.

**Exchange-rate pass-through** refers to how exchange-rate movements affect domestic prices, such as import prices or consumer prices. Pass-through can be incomplete and vary by product, sector, time horizon and market conditions. A depreciation does not mechanically translate into an equal percentage increase in every domestic price.

## 15. Interest-rate differentials, carry and capital movement

Investors compare expected returns across currencies and instruments, taking account of interest rates, expected exchange-rate changes, credit risk, liquidity, taxes, capital controls and transaction costs.

A **carry trade** commonly involves borrowing or funding in a lower-yielding currency or instrument and investing in a higher-yielding one. It can generate gains when conditions are favourable, but exchange-rate changes can erase interest advantages and produce substantial losses. A high nominal interest rate in one currency does not automatically imply a high risk-adjusted real return.

Interest-rate parity relationships describe links between exchange rates, forward rates and interest differentials under specified assumptions and market conventions. They should not be interpreted as guarantees that exchange rates will move in a particular way. Frictions, risk premia, regulation, balance-sheet constraints and changing expectations matter.

Capital movements are not driven by interest differentials alone. Global risk appetite, funding currency availability, benchmark rebalancing, commodity revenues, political and policy uncertainty, liquidity and investor mandates can all affect cross-border positions.

## 16. Currency mismatch, external debt and financial vulnerability

A **currency mismatch** occurs when an entity's assets, income or repayment capacity are denominated in a different currency from its liabilities. A firm earning local currency but owing large amounts in foreign currency may face greater debt-service pressure after depreciation, especially if it cannot hedge or raise prices.

Currency exposure should be assessed at the entity level and consolidated level. Relevant dimensions include denomination, maturity, fixed/variable interest, refinancing schedule, collateral, hedges, income currency and the ability to pass costs through. A foreign-currency liability is not automatically unsafe if it is matched by reliable foreign-currency income or hedging; a domestic-currency liability can still be risky for other reasons.

External vulnerability also depends on short-term obligations, liquid external assets, export and income receipts, rollover conditions, capital-flow volatility, institutional capacity and policy choices. A single exchange rate, reserve number or external-debt ratio is not a complete vulnerability assessment.

## 17. Exchange-rate regimes and controls

Exchange-rate arrangements range from hard pegs and currency boards through conventional pegs, crawling arrangements and managed floats to more freely floating systems. Labels can conceal important operational differences; assess the actual policy framework, intervention practices, market access and rules.

**Foreign-exchange intervention** refers to official transactions intended to influence currency-market conditions or exchange-rate dynamics. Intervention can affect reserves and liquidity, but its effects depend on scale, credibility, market depth, sterilization, policy consistency and expectations.

**Capital controls** are measures that restrict or influence cross-border financial transactions. Their coverage, implementation, circumvention, legal framework and effects vary. They should not be treated as synonymous with foreign-exchange intervention, exchange-rate fixing or every administrative rule affecting FX access.

Multiple exchange-rate windows or official and parallel-market quotations may reflect different access rules, eligible transactions, liquidity, timing and transaction costs. A reported rate is meaningful only when its market, transaction type, date and quote convention are known. Rates from different market segments should not be combined as if they were one freely accessible price.

## 17.1 Cross rates, executable spreads and conversion friction

A **cross rate** is an exchange rate between two currencies derived directly from a quoted pair or calculated through a third currency. For example, if a currency is quoted against the US dollar, the two dollar quotes can be combined to estimate the cross rate between those currencies. The pair orientation must be consistent: a quote of units of currency B per unit of currency A is the reciprocal of units of A per unit of B.

A cross rate calculated from reference or mid-market rates is a **theoretical reference**, not necessarily a rate at which a participant can trade. An executable conversion depends on the bid and ask available to that participant, transaction size, timing, dealer/platform access, settlement arrangements, fees, limits and any legal or operational restrictions.

**Conversion friction** is the collection of costs and constraints that prevent a quoted exchange rate from being a frictionless, costless conversion. It can include the bid–ask spread, explicit commissions, bank or transfer charges, delays, minimum amounts, cash availability, compliance checks, counterparty risk and settlement risk. In markets with restricted access or multiple exchange-rate windows, the relevant quote must match the transaction's eligibility and route.

A simple two-leg conversion may be represented as:

**Currency A → intermediary currency → Currency B**

In plain language, converting through a third currency means executing two conversions, not one magical conversion at the displayed cross rate. Each leg may have a different bid or ask, fee, timing and access constraint. The result can differ materially from a calculation using reference rates alone.

A triangular price discrepancy is therefore only a candidate arbitrage signal. To establish an executable opportunity, verify simultaneous or sufficiently synchronized quotes, correct pair orientation, available size, transaction sequence, fees, funding, access, settlement finality and the risk that prices move before all legs complete. Never describe a theoretical cross-rate difference as guaranteed profit.

## 17.2 Remittances, capital flight and settlement constraints

**Remittances** are cross-border transfers commonly associated with individuals sending resources to households or other recipients. The exact statistical category depends on the balance-of-payments framework and can differ from total money-transfer activity, foreign-exchange turnover or all transfers between related parties. A transfer channel, a payment instruction and a completed transfer are different events.

**Capital flight** is a term used for certain forms of resident capital outflow or asset acquisition abroad, often with an implication of avoidance, instability or loss of domestic confidence. It is not one universally defined statistical category. Legitimate outward investment, portfolio diversification, trade payments, repayment of external debt, errors and omissions, unrecorded flows and illicit transfers are not interchangeable explanations. A rise in gross outflows or a fall in reserves alone does not establish capital flight.

**Settlement constraints** arise when a participant cannot complete a currency or financial transaction at the expected time or in the expected form. Causes can include insufficient currency liquidity, correspondent-bank limits, payment-system cutoffs, compliance review, counterparty failure, mismatched operating hours, capital controls, documentation requirements or restrictions on repatriation. A transaction may be agreed or recorded but still be delayed, rejected or unsettled.

When investigating cross-border movements, distinguish the order or agreement, payment initiation, currency conversion, settlement, receipt by the beneficiary, and the statistical recording of the underlying economic event. These stages may occur at different times and may be recorded by different systems.

## 18. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Exchange rate vs purchasing power | One compares currencies; the other concerns goods and services that money buys. |
| Bilateral vs effective exchange rate | A bilateral rate compares two currencies; an effective index combines a basket of partners. |
| Nominal vs real effective exchange rate | The real measure adjusts for relative prices or costs under a defined method. |
| Appreciation vs revaluation | Appreciation commonly describes a market movement; revaluation often denotes an official parity adjustment. |
| Depreciation vs devaluation | Depreciation commonly describes a market movement; devaluation often denotes an official downward parity adjustment. |
| Spot vs forward rate | Spot applies to near-term settlement under market convention; forward is agreed today for a future exchange. |
| Forward rate vs forecast | A forward price is not automatically a prediction of future spot. |
| Currency denomination vs residence | Contract currency differs from the statistical residence of the parties. |
| Residence vs nationality/ownership | Economic residence is not determined solely by citizenship or ultimate ownership. |
| Trade flows vs financial flows | Goods/services and income transactions differ from transactions in financial claims and liabilities. |
| Gross vs net flows | Net balances can conceal substantial two-way gross activity. |
| Flow vs stock | A transaction over a period differs from a position at a point in time. |
| Transaction vs valuation change | Market-price or FX revaluation can change a stock without a new transaction. |
| Direct vs portfolio investment | Direct investment implies a lasting interest/influence under formal criteria; portfolio covers other securities holdings. |
| BOP vs IIP | Balance of payments records period transactions; IIP records external positions at a date. |
| NIIP vs national wealth | NIIP covers external financial assets and liabilities, not the whole national balance sheet. |
| Gross vs net/usable reserves | Reserve measures depend on included assets, deductions, encumbrances and access. |
| FX intervention vs capital controls | Official market transactions differ from restrictions on cross-border transactions. |
| High nominal yield vs high real return | Inflation, exchange-rate changes, risk and costs can offset nominal yield. |
| Depreciation vs automatic export gain | Trade response depends on elasticities, capacity, inputs, contracts and timing. |

## 19. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before interpreting an FX or capital-flow claim, ask:

1. Which currency pair and quote convention are used?
2. What is the market, transaction type, date and settlement convention?
3. Is the measure bilateral, nominal effective or real effective?
4. What are the index direction, partner weights, deflator and base period?
5. Does “appreciation” refer to a market move or an official parity change?
6. Are the parties classified by residence, nationality, ownership or currency denomination?
7. Is the figure a current-account transaction, financial-account flow, reserve transaction or broader estimate?
8. Is the flow gross or net, and what sign convention applies?
9. Is the measure a transaction during a period or a stock at a date?
10. Have valuation changes, write-offs and reclassifications been separated from transactions?
11. What exactly is included in direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment or reserves?
12. Is the claim about gross reserves, net reserves, usable reserves or a specified adequacy metric?
13. Are currency, maturity, liquidity, hedging and rollover risks included?
14. What evidence supports a proposed causal explanation, and what alternative explanations exist?
15. Are official, parallel and other market quotations being compared on equivalent terms?
16. Are data vintages, revisions, valuation methods and reporting standards documented?

## 21. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Exchange rates are relative prices and require an explicit quotation convention.
- Currency purchasing power and exchange-rate value are related but different.
- Gross flows, net flows, positions and valuation changes are distinct.
- Balance of payments is a period transaction framework; the international investment position is a point-in-time external balance sheet.
- Residence, currency denomination and nationality/ownership are separate classifications.
- Reserve adequacy depends on definitions, liquidity, obligations and external financing conditions, not only a headline total.
- Cross-border transaction categories describe accounting classifications, not automatically economic impact or intent.

### Scope limits
- No current Nigerian FX, reserve, external debt, capital-flow or balance-of-payments values are asserted.
- Definitions and sign conventions must be checked against the relevant statistical manual and publishing institution.
- A change in a reported stock cannot be attributed to transactions without a stock-flow reconciliation.
- The note does not claim a single exchange-rate regime or market quotation captures every transaction accessible to every participant.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which Nigerian official sources publish bilateral and effective exchange-rate series, and how are they defined?
- How are Nigeria's balance-of-payments and international investment position accounts presented and revised?
- What definitions are used for gross, net and usable official reserves?
- How do the currency and maturity composition of external debt affect vulnerability?
- Which flow measures best distinguish direct investment, portfolio investment, bank flows and other external financing?
- How do valuation changes and exchange-rate movements affect reported external stocks?
- What data are needed to assess exchange-rate pass-through and foreign-currency mismatch in specific sectors?

## 22. Short summary

An exchange rate is a relative price between currencies; it is not the same as purchasing power or national wealth. A cross-border flow is measured over a period, while an external asset or liability is a stock measured at a date. Stocks change through transactions, valuation effects and other adjustments. Balance-of-payments accounts describe transactions, while the international investment position describes external financial positions. Currency denomination, residence and ownership must be kept separate, and reserve or capital-flow figures should not be interpreted without their definitions and accounting basis.

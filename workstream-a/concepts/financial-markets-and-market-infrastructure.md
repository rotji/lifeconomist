# Concept 18 — Financial Markets and Market Infrastructure

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal market indicators and any Nigeria-specific data validation remain in Workstream B  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 17 — Foreign Exchange, International Money and Global Capital Flows](foreign-exchange-international-money-and-global-capital-flows.md)

## 1. Purpose

Financial markets connect people and institutions that issue, buy, sell, borrow, lend, hedge and hold financial claims. But the existence of trading is not identical to new financing, productive investment or an improvement in social welfare. A share can change hands many times without the company receiving new funds; a security can be issued to raise funds without the proceeds becoming productive capacity immediately; and a quoted price does not necessarily mean that every holder could sell the full position at that price.

This note separates financial instruments, markets, intermediaries, trading, market infrastructure, pricing and real-economy outcomes. It is conceptual: it does not report current Nigerian market prices, turnover, capitalization, issuance or liquidity indicators.

## 2. Financial claim, instrument and security

A **financial claim** is a contractual or legally recognized claim on cash flows, assets or another economic obligation. One party's financial asset commonly corresponds to another party's liability or equity claim, subject to the instrument and accounting framework.

A **financial instrument** is the contract or arrangement that creates financial assets for one party and financial liabilities or equity instruments for another under the applicable accounting definition. Examples include deposits, loans, bonds, shares, derivatives and trade credit.

A **security** is a tradable financial instrument or class of instruments under the applicable legal and market definition. The precise legal definition varies by jurisdiction. Not every financial claim is a security: an ordinary bank loan and a publicly traded bond are both financial claims, but they typically have different transfer, disclosure, trading and settlement arrangements.

Instrument labels matter because rights, cash flows, seniority, voting rights, collateral, maturity, convertibility and legal enforceability differ. “Financial asset” is an accounting or balance-sheet category, not a guarantee of easy sale or low risk.

## 3. Primary issuance versus secondary trading

The **primary market** is where a new financial instrument is issued and funds are raised by the issuer or seller of the newly created claim. Examples include a company issuing new shares or an entity issuing a new bond.

The **secondary market** is where existing instruments are traded between investors. In a typical secondary share trade, the buyer pays the selling shareholder; the issuing company does not automatically receive the transaction proceeds.

Secondary markets can still benefit issuers indirectly by providing liquidity, price discovery and an exit route for investors, potentially making future issuance more attractive or less costly. But those indirect benefits are different from the cash proceeds of a new issue.

A company may also raise funds through retained earnings, bank loans, private placements or other arrangements that do not resemble a public exchange-traded share offering. The financing route must be identified before inferring how much new money reached the issuer.

## 4. Trading activity versus financing and productive investment

**Trading** transfers or creates exposure to financial claims. **Financing** provides funds or purchasing power to a borrower, issuer or economic activity under a defined arrangement. **Productive investment** in national accounting usually concerns acquisition or creation of produced assets such as equipment, structures, software and certain other assets, rather than every purchase of a financial security.

These activities can be connected but are not interchangeable:
- Secondary trading in existing shares usually transfers ownership rather than providing direct new cash to the issuer.
- New equity or debt issuance can provide financing, but the use of proceeds and eventual results must be investigated separately.
- A bank loan provides finance to a borrower, but the borrower may use it for working capital, refinancing, consumption, acquisition of existing assets or new productive assets.
- Buying an existing building transfers ownership; constructing a new building creates a produced asset, though the accounting treatment depends on the transaction and sector.
- A high volume of trading does not by itself demonstrate high investment in new productive capacity.

The path from financial transaction to economic outcome requires tracing the instrument, counterparty, recipient, use of funds, timing and result. A transaction label alone does not establish job creation, productivity growth, innovation or public benefit.

## 5. Exchanges and over-the-counter markets

A **financial exchange** is an organized venue or system for trading specified instruments under rules for listing or admission, order handling, trading, disclosure and oversight. Exchanges may use order books, market makers or hybrid models.

An **over-the-counter (OTC) market** involves transactions arranged directly between counterparties or through dealers and electronic networks outside a centralized exchange order book. OTC markets can be highly organized and electronically connected; “OTC” does not necessarily mean informal, unregulated or opaque in every respect.

Exchange-traded and OTC instruments can differ in standardization, price transparency, counterparty exposure, execution, reporting, collateral and settlement. OTC arrangements may allow customization but can introduce complexity and bilateral exposures. Actual characteristics depend on the market, instrument, regulation and clearing arrangements.

A market's structure affects how prices are formed and observed. One venue's quote or transaction may not represent the price available for every size, counterparty or time.

## 6. Market participants and intermediaries

Participants and intermediaries have different roles. They can include:
- **Issuers and borrowers:** raise funds or create claims.
- **Investors and lenders:** provide funds or acquire claims.
- **Brokers:** arrange or execute transactions for clients, subject to their mandate and rules.
- **Dealers and market makers:** may trade as principals and quote prices at which they are willing to buy or sell.
- **Asset managers and investment funds:** manage pooled or delegated assets under mandates.
- **Banks:** provide deposits, loans, payments, underwriting, market-making or custody-related services depending on permissions and business model.
- **Exchanges and trading platforms:** operate systems and rules for matching or executing orders.
- **Clearing houses and central counterparties (CCPs):** calculate obligations and, for a CCP, interpose itself between counterparties for cleared trades, subject to its rulebook.
- **Central securities depositories (CSDs):** maintain securities records and support book-entry transfers under their framework.
- **Custodians:** safeguard or administer assets and records for clients.
- **Regulators and supervisors:** set and enforce rules within their statutory remit.

One organization can perform multiple roles, but roles should not be conflated. A broker is not automatically the beneficial owner of a client's assets; an exchange is not normally the issuer of the securities traded there; and a custodian's recordkeeping role does not alone settle every question of legal title or beneficial ownership.

## 7. Orders, order books, bid and ask

An **order** communicates an instruction to buy or sell under specified conditions. A **market order** generally seeks execution promptly at available prices, while a **limit order** sets a maximum purchase price or minimum sale price. Actual order types and execution rules vary by venue.

The **bid** is a price at which a buyer is willing to buy; the **ask** or offer is a price at which a seller is willing to sell. The **bid–ask spread** is the difference between them under the quoted convention. It can reflect order-processing costs, inventory risk, adverse-selection risk, competition and market conditions.

An **order book** displays eligible resting buy and sell orders under a venue's rules. Visible depth at the best price is not the same as total market depth; hidden orders, other venues, dealer liquidity and changing orders may matter.

A displayed quote is not a guarantee that a large order can execute fully at that price. Market impact and execution cost depend on order size, available depth, speed, volatility and the trader's execution strategy.

## 8. Market price, transaction price and fundamental value

A **quoted price** is a displayed bid, ask, indicative value or other market reference. A **transaction price** is the price at which a trade actually executes. They may differ.

**Market value** is a valuation based on a defined market price or methodology at a stated time. **Fundamental value** is an estimate based on assumptions about future cash flows, risk, discount rates and other relevant factors. It is an analytical estimate, not a directly observable universal fact. **Book value** is an accounting amount determined under applicable recognition and measurement rules.

The terms answer different questions. Market prices reflect the marginal terms of trades or quotes in a market. Fundamental-value estimates depend on models and uncertain assumptions. Book values reflect accounting rules and historical or updated measurements. None should be substituted for another without explaining the purpose.

Market prices can be affected by expected cash flows, discount rates, risk appetite, liquidity, market structure, supply and demand, positioning, news and constraints. A price change alone does not identify which mechanism dominated or prove that the asset became more productive or socially valuable.

## 9. Liquidity: market, funding and asset liquidity

**Market liquidity** concerns the ability to buy or sell an asset in meaningful size, quickly and with limited price impact. Common dimensions include tightness (such as bid–ask spreads), depth, immediacy and resilience after trades or shocks.

**Funding liquidity** concerns the ability of an entity to obtain cash or financing when needed. An institution can hold assets that are valuable over the long term but face difficulty meeting near-term payments if those assets cannot be sold or financed in time.

**Asset liquidity** describes how readily an asset can be converted into cash with limited loss, under specified market conditions. Liquidity is not a permanent binary characteristic: it can deteriorate during stress, for large positions or in markets with concentrated participants.

A market may have high turnover but poor depth at the moment a large order arrives. An asset may usually trade easily but become illiquid during stress. A firm can be solvent on a balance-sheet basis but suffer a cash shortfall. These cases illustrate why market liquidity, funding liquidity and solvency must be assessed separately.

## 10. Turnover, volume, depth and market capitalization

**Trading volume** counts or measures instruments traded over a period according to a defined convention. **Turnover** may mean traded value or a ratio of trading value to an appropriate market stock, depending on the publisher. The exact definition, treatment of double-counting and unit must be checked.

**Market capitalization** commonly equals a share price multiplied by the number of shares included in the defined capitalization measure. It is a market valuation of the equity stock, not a pool of cash available to the company and not the amount investors collectively paid for every outstanding share at current prices.

A relatively small marginal trade can change the quoted price used to mark a much larger stock of shares. Thus a change in market capitalization is not necessarily an equal amount of money entering or leaving the market. It is a change in the valuation of outstanding equity under the chosen methodology.

**Market depth** describes available buy and sell interest at different prices or within a defined range. **Free float** refers to shares considered available for public trading under a particular methodology; it does not mean that those shares will be offered for sale at any moment.

Turnover, volume, depth, spread, market capitalization and new issuance measure different aspects of a market. No one measure alone proves that the market is liquid, fair, efficient or financing productive activity effectively.

## 11. Price discovery and information

**Price discovery** is the process through which market interactions, orders, information and expectations contribute to observed prices. It can aggregate dispersed information, but prices can also be noisy, influenced by constraints or distorted by thin trading, market power, manipulation or unreliable disclosures.

Information may reach participants unevenly. Public disclosures, analyst estimates, private information, macroeconomic conditions, liquidity needs and investor mandates can affect bids and offers. Regulations governing disclosure and insider trading vary by jurisdiction.

A market price is evidence about the terms at which marginal trades occur or quotes are posted, under that market's conditions. It is not a perfect or complete summary of all available information, and it does not reveal every participant's valuation, motive or information.

When studying price changes, examine event timing, trading volume, spreads, market depth, relevant news, broader benchmarks and alternative explanations. Do not infer causation from a price chart alone.

## 12. Clearing and settlement

**Execution** is the matching or agreement of a trade. **Clearing** establishes, calculates or manages the obligations arising from trades, potentially including netting, margin and default-management processes. **Settlement** is the final transfer of the relevant cash and/or securities under the system's rules.

These are separate stages. A trade can be executed but not yet settled. Settlement risk includes the possibility that one party delivers an asset but does not receive the expected counter-asset or payment as intended.

**Delivery versus payment (DvP)** links the transfer of securities to the transfer of payment under a defined arrangement to reduce principal risk. Payment systems, securities settlement systems, clearing houses and custodians may participate at different stages.

Settlement cycles, finality rules, collateral requirements and failure procedures vary by market and instrument. “The trade was done” should not be treated as proof that settlement has completed or that every related obligation has been discharged.

## 13. Central counterparties, netting and counterparty risk

A **central counterparty (CCP)** becomes the buyer to each seller and seller to each buyer for trades it clears, subject to the clearing arrangement. It manages counterparty exposures through rules that may include margin, default funds, position limits and default-management procedures.

**Netting** offsets eligible obligations under defined legal and operational rules, reducing the number or size of payments or deliveries that must be made. Gross obligations and net settlement obligations can differ substantially.

Central clearing can reduce some bilateral exposures and support standardized risk management, but it concentrates risk and operational importance in the CCP. Its resilience depends on financial resources, models, governance, liquidity arrangements, legal enforceability and participants' ability to meet obligations.

Not all instruments are centrally cleared. Bilateral OTC arrangements can have distinct collateral, netting and counterparty-risk structures. Do not assume that clearing eliminates risk; it changes where and how risk is managed.

## 14. Custody, recordkeeping and ownership

**Custody** involves safeguarding or administering financial assets and associated records. Securities may be held directly, through intermediaries or in omnibus arrangements, depending on market rules.

**Legal ownership**, **beneficial ownership**, **custody** and **economic exposure** can differ. The registered holder may be a nominee or intermediary while another person has the beneficial interest. A fund investor may have a claim on the fund rather than direct legal title to each portfolio security. A derivative can create economic exposure without ownership of the referenced asset.

A custody statement is important evidence of a recorded position, but interpretation may require account terms, legal structure, liens, collateral arrangements, nominee records and the applicable law. Similarly, exposure to an asset's price does not prove ownership of that asset.

For footprint tracing, identify the issuer, account holder, registered holder, beneficial owner where established, custodian, intermediary, instrument and legal claim separately. Do not infer ultimate control from one visible account entry.

## 15. Derivatives, hedging and leverage

A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate, index, event or other reference. Examples include futures, forwards, options and swaps. Derivatives can be used to hedge exposures, gain or transfer risk, or express views on future market conditions.

**Hedging** aims to reduce a specified risk, but it may introduce basis risk, liquidity needs, counterparty risk, margin calls or other exposures. A position described as a hedge must be evaluated against the exposure it is intended to offset, including size, timing, currency and instrument mismatch.

**Leverage** creates exposure or financing relative to an entity's own capital or resources. It can magnify gains and losses and create margin or refinancing pressure. Different leverage measures—balance-sheet debt, gross notional derivatives, net exposure, margin exposure and economic leverage—are not interchangeable.

Gross derivative notional is generally a reference amount used to calculate contractual payments, not automatically the amount at risk or the net market value. Exposure depends on contract terms, netting, collateral, market moves, counterparty credit and the measurement purpose.

## 16. Market regulation and infrastructure resilience

Market infrastructure includes trading venues, clearing and settlement systems, payment rails, depositories, custodians, data and reporting systems, and supporting communications and governance. A disruption in one critical node can delay or impair transactions across connected institutions.

Regulation can address disclosure, conduct, market abuse, capital, margin, custody, settlement finality, operational resilience and investor protection. The exact rules and supervisory responsibilities depend on the jurisdiction and instrument.

Operational risks include cyber incidents, outages, erroneous data, failed processes, fraud, third-party concentration and business-continuity failures. Infrastructure can be systemically important because many institutions rely on the same service, not merely because it is large by revenue.

A market's existence or high activity does not prove reliable access, fair treatment or resilience. Evaluate rules, access, concentration, contingency arrangements, transparency and actual performance.

## 17. Market stress and feedback loops

During stress, falling prices can trigger collateral calls or margin requirements, forcing some participants to sell assets. If many participants sell similar assets into a thin market, prices can fall further, increasing losses and funding needs. This is one potential feedback mechanism, not an inevitable outcome of every price decline.

Liquidity providers may reduce risk, funding can become more expensive, bid–ask spreads can widen, and previously reliable hedges can become less effective. Common exposures and concentrated positions can transmit stress between institutions or markets.

The distinction between a price loss and a cash-flow obligation matters: a mark-to-market loss may create collateral or regulatory consequences even before an asset is sold. Conversely, an asset's long-run estimated value does not guarantee that it can be liquidated in time to meet a payment.

Stress analysis should consider position concentration, leverage, maturity mismatch, collateral, market depth, common holdings, settlement dependencies and the timing of cash obligations. Do not infer systemic risk from market volatility alone without evidence about connections and exposures.

## 17.1 Bond yields, duration, convexity and term structure

A bond's **yield** is a return measure calculated from its price and specified cash flows under stated assumptions. It is not necessarily the investor's realized return: realized results depend on purchase and sale prices, coupon reinvestment, default or restructuring, taxes, fees and holding period. Coupon rate, current yield and yield to maturity are distinct measures.

**Duration** summarizes a bond's sensitivity to interest-rate changes under a defined method and set of assumptions. Macaulay duration is a weighted timing measure for cash flows; modified duration approximates percentage price sensitivity to a small yield change under the relevant convention. Effective duration is often used where cash flows may change, such as for callable or mortgage-related instruments. Duration is an approximation, not a guarantee.

**Convexity** describes how the price-yield relationship curves and helps improve a duration-based approximation for larger yield movements, subject to the instrument and method. Embedded options can change the relationship, so a simple positive-convexity intuition does not apply to every bond or market condition.

The **term structure of interest rates** describes yields across maturities for a defined class of instruments or curve-construction method. A yield curve may reflect expectations about future short rates, term premiums, inflation and growth expectations, liquidity, supply/demand and instrument-specific risks. A curve's shape alone does not uniquely reveal which mechanism dominates.

For any comparison, state the instrument, issuer/credit quality, currency, maturity, yield convention, price date, curve source and whether the yield is observed or model-derived. Government-bond yields, swap rates and corporate yields are not interchangeable.

## 17.2 Repo, collateral reuse and securities lending

A **repurchase agreement (repo)** is economically a secured funding transaction commonly structured as a sale of securities with an agreement to repurchase them later at an agreed price. In a reverse repo, the same transaction is described from the other party's perspective. Legal characterization, accounting treatment, title transfer and close-out rights depend on the agreement and jurisdiction.

**Haircuts** reduce the collateral value recognized against a transaction to provide a buffer against price changes, liquidation costs and other risks. A larger haircut generally means the borrower must provide more collateral for the same funding amount. Haircuts can rise during stress, creating additional funding needs even when the borrower has not increased its desired position.

**Securities lending** temporarily transfers securities to a borrower under agreed collateral and return conditions, often to support settlement, short selling or other market activity. **Collateral reuse** or rehypothecation refers to collateral being reused or re-pledged where the contract and rules permit. Reuse can support funding and market activity, but it can also create chains of claims and dependencies across institutions.

A simplified secured-funding chain:

**Securities supplied as collateral → funding obtained → collateral or proceeds reused where permitted → interconnected return and settlement obligations**

In plain language, one institution may obtain cash by pledging securities, while the counterparty may be able to reuse the collateral under the contract. This can make funding more efficient, but it also means stress at one institution or in one collateral market can affect other participants. The actual risk depends on legal rights, collateral quality, haircuts, maturity, concentration, settlement systems and the ability to return securities or cash when due.

Repo volume or securities-lending activity is not by itself proof of excessive leverage or systemic risk. To assess risk, examine the counterparties, collateral, maturity, margining, reuse rights, concentration, legal enforceability and what happens if a participant defaults.

## 18. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Financial asset vs productive asset | A financial claim differs from a produced asset such as equipment or a structure. |
| Primary vs secondary market | Primary issuance raises funds for an issuer; secondary trading transfers existing claims. |
| Trading vs financing | Trading changes holdings/exposures; financing provides funds to a borrower or issuer. |
| Financial investment vs capital formation | Buying securities is not automatically creation of new produced assets. |
| Exchange vs OTC market | Venue-based organized trading differs from bilateral/dealer or network-based arrangements. |
| Broker vs dealer | A broker acts for clients; a dealer may trade as principal, depending on role and rules. |
| Market price vs fundamental value | Observed price differs from a model-based estimate of value. |
| Market value vs book value | Market valuation differs from accounting measurement. |
| Quote vs transaction price | A displayed bid/ask or indicative price is not necessarily an executed trade. |
| Market liquidity vs funding liquidity | Ability to trade assets differs from ability to obtain cash or funding. |
| Volume/turnover vs depth | Amount traded over time differs from available interest at prices. |
| Market capitalization vs cash raised | Equity valuation is not cash held by the company or new financing. |
| Price change vs money inflow/outflow | Repricing of a stock need not equal net funds moving into or out of it. |
| Execution vs clearing vs settlement | Trade agreement, obligation management and final transfer are separate stages. |
| Clearing vs settlement | Calculating/managing obligations differs from final exchange of cash or securities. |
| Custody vs beneficial ownership | Safekeeping or recordkeeping does not alone establish who benefits from or controls a claim. |
| Security ownership vs economic exposure | Derivatives can create price exposure without ownership of the underlying asset. |
| CCP risk reduction vs risk elimination | Central clearing redistributes and manages risk but does not remove all risk. |
| Derivative notional vs exposure | Reference notional is not automatically current market value or potential loss. |
| Hedge vs speculation | Classification depends on the exposure, purpose and resulting risk, not merely the instrument. |
| Leverage vs debt | Leverage can arise through multiple arrangements; debt is a specific obligation category. |

## 19. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before using a financial-market claim, ask:

1. Which instrument, issuer, venue and participant are covered?
2. Is the transaction a new issue, a secondary trade, a loan, a deposit or a derivative?
3. Who actually receives the proceeds, and what evidence shows how they are used?
4. Is the price a quote, a transaction price, a closing price or a model-based valuation?
5. What are the market's trading hours, settlement rules and quote conventions?
6. Is the measure volume, traded value, turnover, depth, spread, capitalization or issuance?
7. Are market capitalization and price changes being mistaken for equivalent cash flows?
8. What size can realistically trade at the quoted price, and what market impact might occur?
9. Are clearing, settlement, custody and ownership roles identified separately?
10. Is the instrument centrally cleared or bilateral, and what netting/collateral rules apply?
11. Is a derivative's notional being confused with market value, net exposure or loss at risk?
12. What do the data omit—off-venue trades, hidden orders, unreported positions or illiquid assets?
13. Are changes driven by transactions, valuation, new issuance, corporate actions or methodological changes?
14. Is the proposed economic impact supported by evidence of financing, use of funds and subsequent outcomes?
15. Are legal definitions and market rules appropriate to the jurisdiction and period?
16. Could market structure, concentrated positions or liquidity conditions explain the observation without proving intent?

- **Workstream A:** connects market organization and trading to financial claims, investment, capital allocation, credit, liquidity, ownership and productive capacity.

## 21. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Primary issuance and secondary trading have different immediate funding effects.
- Market capitalization is a valuation measure, not a pool of cash available to an issuer.
- Market liquidity, funding liquidity and solvency are separate concepts.
- Execution, clearing, settlement, custody and ownership refer to different functions or rights.
- Financial-market transactions do not automatically represent new productive capital formation.
- Derivative notional is not the same as market value or potential loss.
- Market price changes can reflect information, liquidity, positioning and other mechanisms; a price change alone does not prove a specific cause.

### Scope limits
- No current Nigerian market capitalization, turnover, issuance, liquidity or trading values are asserted.
- Legal definitions, trading arrangements and settlement cycles vary by jurisdiction and instrument.
- The note does not claim that secondary markets have no economic value; liquidity and price discovery can support future financing indirectly.
- The economic impact of a financing transaction depends on its use and outcome, which require separate evidence.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which official sources define and publish Nigerian exchange-market indicators and issuance statistics?
- How are turnover, market capitalization, free float, liquidity and bond-market measures defined by each source?
- What are the key trading, clearing, settlement and custody institutions for each Nigerian market segment?
- What data can distinguish primary issuance from secondary-market trading and subsequent use of proceeds?
- How should market liquidity and funding liquidity be monitored separately?
- Which official rules govern beneficial ownership, custody, clearing and settlement for relevant instruments?
- What evidence is available on market access, concentration, settlement failures and resilience?

## 22. Short summary

Financial markets trade and price financial claims, but trading, financing and productive investment are not the same. Primary issuance can raise funds for an issuer; secondary trading normally transfers existing claims between investors. Market capitalization is a valuation, not cash available to a company. Market liquidity differs from funding liquidity and solvency. Execution, clearing, settlement, custody and ownership are separate stages or roles. To understand an economic outcome, trace who received the funds, what happened to them and what evidence shows the result.

# Concept 3 — Economic Value, Usefulness and Exchange Value

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concepts:** [Production](production.md); [Output and Intermediate Inputs](output-and-intermediate-inputs.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain why usefulness, economic value, market price, exchange value, welfare value and value added are related but different concepts.

## 1. The starting question: what does “value” mean?

People use the word *value* in several ways. Someone may say that clean water is valuable, that a phone is worth ₦100,000, that a business has a market value of ₦50 million, or that a service adds value to a customer's work. These statements may all be reasonable, but they do not necessarily mean the same thing.

Before interpreting a claim about value, ask:

1. **Value to whom?** A household, buyer, seller, worker, business, government, or society?
2. **Value of what?** A good, service, asset, activity, relationship, outcome, or future opportunity?
3. **Which meaning of value?** Usefulness, willingness to pay, observed exchange price, accounting value, value added, or a broader welfare effect?
4. **At what time and under what conditions?** Value and prices can change with scarcity, alternatives, quality, information, institutions, location and expectations.
5. **What evidence supports the claim?** An observed transaction, a stated preference, an accounting rule, an estimate, or a hypothesis?

The word *value* should not be used as though it had one universal numerical meaning. The appropriate concept depends on the question being asked.

## 2. Usefulness and human needs

**Usefulness** is a broad, plain-language idea: something helps a person or group meet a need, achieve a goal, avoid harm, save time, or improve an outcome. Usefulness depends on circumstances and on whose perspective is being considered.

Examples:
- Clean water can support health and household activity.
- A road can make travel and trade possible.
- A warning about a dangerous product can prevent harm.
- Accurate market information may help a trader compare selling options.
- A tool may be useful to one person but irrelevant to another.

Usefulness is not automatically expressed in money. It may be described qualitatively, measured through physical outcomes, or evaluated using monetary and non-monetary indicators.

Usefulness is also not the same as a claim that something has positive effects in every respect. A product may help one user while imposing costs on others; a road may improve access while creating noise or environmental damage. Assessment must identify the affected parties, time horizon, benefits, costs and possible external effects.

## 3. Economic value is not one single measurement

In economics, *value* may refer to different concepts depending on the theory and application. This roadmap will distinguish at least the following:

- **Use value / usefulness:** The benefit or function something provides in a given context. The term “use value” has different meanings in different economic traditions, so a note must state its intended usage.
- **Willingness to pay (WTP):** The maximum amount a person would be willing to give up for a good, service or change under specified conditions. This is a preference-based concept, not automatically the price actually paid.
- **Exchange value:** The value at which a good, service, labour or asset is exchanged, or could be exchanged, for money under a stated institutional and market setting. National accounts use exchange-value principles to make transactions and production comparable.
- **Market price / transaction price:** The price observed or agreed in a particular transaction or market. Prices can vary across buyers, sellers, locations, quality grades, timing, and contractual terms.
- **Accounting value:** A value recorded under a specified accounting or statistical rule. It may be based on a transaction price, an estimate, an imputation or another permitted valuation basis.
- **Value added:** In national accounting, output less intermediate consumption under the applicable definitions and valuation rules. It is not a synonym for customer benefit or business profit.
- **Welfare value:** An estimate of how an outcome affects well-being or economic welfare under a stated analytical framework. It may include benefits or costs not reflected in observed transactions, such as consumer surplus or external effects.
- **Asset value:** A valuation of an asset under a specified basis and date. Market value, book value, replacement cost and present value are not interchangeable.

These concepts can be related, but converting one into another requires a method and assumptions. A number labelled simply “value” is incomplete unless the meaning, perspective, unit, date and method are clear.

## 4. Price, willingness to pay and consumer surplus

A price is the amount agreed or observed in an exchange. It is not necessarily the maximum the buyer would have been willing to pay.

Suppose a buyer would have been willing to pay up to ₦5,000 for a bag of food but obtains it for ₦4,000. Under the standard introductory concept, the buyer's consumer surplus is ₦1,000, assuming the willingness-to-pay estimate and transaction conditions are valid.

The ₦4,000 is the observed exchange price. The ₦5,000 is the buyer's stated or inferred willingness to pay. The ₦1,000 difference is not money the buyer received, nor is it part of the seller's revenue. It is a measure of buyer benefit within the specified economic framework.

This example is hypothetical. In real settings, willingness to pay is not always directly observable and can depend on income, information, alternatives, urgency and how the question is asked. Stated preferences may also differ from actual behaviour.

National accounts generally record exchange values rather than adding consumer surplus to recorded transactions. This matters because welfare valuations and national-accounting exchange values answer different questions and cannot simply be mixed in one total. The OECD and the UN's System of National Accounts materials discuss this distinction.

## 5. Market price is evidence, not a complete measure of usefulness

A market price tells us something about an exchange under particular conditions. It does not, by itself, reveal:
- the full benefit received by the buyer;
- the seller's costs or profit;
- the distribution of gains between buyer, seller and intermediaries;
- any external costs imposed on others;
- whether the transaction was affordable to everyone who needed the product;
- the quality or reliability of the product unless these are specified;
- the value of alternatives that were unavailable or not chosen.

Prices are shaped by supply and demand, scarcity, competition, market power, taxes, subsidies, regulations, information, bargaining power, access and transaction costs. The observed price is therefore not a pure measurement of intrinsic usefulness.

A high price does not prove that a product is more useful than a low-priced one. A low price does not prove that a product has little usefulness. Some essential goods may be inexpensive when abundant; some scarce or heavily branded goods may command high prices. The correct interpretation depends on the question and evidence.

## 6. Example: clean water

Clean water can be useful for drinking, cooking, sanitation and health. Its value can be examined from several perspectives:

- **Physical outcome:** Litres of water supplied, reliability of access, and water quality.
- **Market exchange:** The amount paid to a vendor or utility for a specified quantity and quality.
- **Household perspective:** Time saved collecting water, affordability, health implications, and alternative sources.
- **Producer perspective:** Revenue, input costs, operating costs and financial viability of supplying water.
- **Public perspective:** Health effects, access, external effects, and distribution across communities.
- **National accounts:** Output and value added are measured under the relevant rules; these are not a complete measure of every health, time-saving or welfare benefit.
- **Welfare analysis:** An evaluation may estimate benefits and costs beyond the transaction price, using explicit methods and assumptions.

No single figure automatically captures all these perspectives. A useful analysis chooses the measure that matches the decision and keeps other important dimensions visible.

## 7. Example: market information as a service

Imagine a trader can buy maize in one location and sell it in another. A service reports recent prices, verifies their source, estimates transport and market charges, and explains uncertainty.

Its possible usefulness includes:
- reducing the time spent collecting information;
- revealing a price difference the trader had not known;
- helping compare locations after transport and other costs;
- reducing the chance of relying on stale or false information;
- supporting a decision to buy, sell, wait or avoid a transaction.

But several distinct questions remain:
1. Was the information accurate and timely?
2. Did the trader understand it and have the ability to act?
3. Did acting on it improve the decision compared with what the trader would otherwise have done?
4. Did the benefit exceed the fee and any additional costs?
5. Was the result caused by the information or by unrelated market changes?

The provider's fee is a transaction price. The provider's revenue is not automatically profit or value added. The trader's gross trading margin is not automatically the net benefit attributable to the information. To estimate causal benefit, one needs a defensible comparison with a plausible alternative or counterfactual, not just a story in which the service and a good outcome occurred together.

This is a conceptual example, not evidence that a particular market-information service has demand or improves profits.

## 8. Externalities and effects beyond the transaction

An **externality** is an effect of an activity on others that is not fully reflected in the relevant transaction price or private decision. The precise definition and policy treatment depend on the analytical context.

Examples can include pollution affecting nearby households, congestion imposed on other road users, or wider health benefits from disease prevention. These examples require evidence to establish their scale in any real case.

An observed price may not include all external benefits or costs. Therefore, private willingness to pay, market price, producer revenue and broader social benefit may diverge.

This does not mean analysts can freely add any estimated benefit to market values. External effects and welfare values require explicit methods, scope, assumptions and attention to double counting. National accounts and cost-benefit analysis have different purposes and valuation conventions.

## 9. Exchange value versus welfare value in national accounting

The System of National Accounts (SNA) is designed to describe economic activity consistently. Its exchange-value principle uses values at which goods, services, labour or assets are exchanged, or could be exchanged, for money under the relevant conditions. Where no direct market price exists, a statistical framework may require a justified imputation or alternative valuation method.

Welfare analysis asks a different question: how much does a change in circumstances affect people's welfare? It can include consumer surplus and other effects not represented by the exchange value recorded in the accounts.

Important consequences:
- GDP and value added are not complete measures of well-being.
- An activity outside a particular GDP production boundary is not thereby useless.
- An observed transaction price is not the full amount the buyer might have been willing to pay.
- A welfare estimate cannot automatically be added to GDP or national-accounting value added.
- A valuation can be valid for one purpose and unsuitable for another.

The OECD's work on national accounts and the UN's SNA materials emphasize that accounting exchange values and welfare values are different concepts. The appropriate valuation depends on the analytical purpose.

## 10. Valuation, context and comparability

Before comparing two monetary values, verify:
- **Object:** Are both values for the same good, service, asset or outcome?
- **Perspective:** Buyer, seller, owner, government, society or national accounts?
- **Basis:** Transaction price, market value, book value, replacement cost, willingness to pay or welfare estimate?
- **Date:** Are they valued at the same time?
- **Quantity and quality:** Do they cover comparable units and quality?
- **Location and market conditions:** Are taxes, transport, regulation, access and market power comparable?
- **Scope:** Do they include externalities, consumer surplus, fees or other adjustments?
- **Currency and price level:** Are exchange rates and inflation relevant?
- **Method:** Is the value observed, estimated, imputed or hypothetical?
- **Uncertainty:** What assumptions and error margins apply?

Without these checks, apparent differences may reflect definitions or methods rather than meaningful economic change.

## 11. Distinctions to preserve

| Term | Core meaning / question |
|---|---|
| Usefulness | What function or benefit does something provide in a context? |
| Use value | A term with context-dependent meanings; state the theoretical or practical usage |
| Willingness to pay | What is the maximum a person would give up for a specified good or change under specified conditions? |
| Price | What amount is quoted, agreed or observed in exchange? |
| Exchange value | At what value is an item exchanged, or could be exchanged, under the applicable valuation framework? |
| Consumer surplus | Under standard demand analysis, the difference between willingness to pay and the price actually paid for a specified unit or set of units |
| Revenue | What amount is recognized from a business's activities under its accounting rules? |
| Profit | What remains under a specified profit measure after the included costs and expenses |
| Value added | Output less intermediate consumption under the applicable national-accounting rules |
| Welfare value | How is an outcome valued in terms of welfare under a stated analytical framework? |
| Asset value | What value is assigned to an asset under a specified basis and date? |
| Social benefit/cost | The benefit or cost to the relevant social group or society under a stated evaluation framework; not automatically identical to private gain or loss |

## 12. Connections to the next concepts

- **Costs and prices:** Price is not the same as cost, and both can differ from value to the buyer or broader social value.
- **Revenue, income and receipts:** A price multiplied by quantity may help describe sales value, but accounting recognition, other income and cash timing require separate treatment.
- **Value added and profit:** Value added is a production-accounting concept; profit is a specified business result. Neither is a complete measure of welfare.
- **Money, wealth and capital:** A monetary valuation is not the same as money held, wealth owned, or capital committed to production.
- **Workstream B:** Select operational definitions and measures only where the project needs an indicator. This note does not define a new GDP or welfare indicator.
- **Workstream C:** Market prices and non-market indicators can be observations, but interpretation requires their scope, timing, and limitations.
- **Workstream D:** Observed transactions and physical traces can support claims about activity, but they do not independently establish usefulness, consumer benefit or social welfare.
- **Workstream E:** A customer problem may involve time, risk, access, reliability or avoided losses rather than the product's quoted price alone. Any opportunity must still be validated; this concept note is not market-demand evidence.

## 13. Evidence status and limits

### Supported by the cited conceptual sources
- National accounts use exchange-value principles to value transactions and economic activity under a consistent accounting framework.
- Welfare valuation may include consumer surplus and effects not represented by exchange values.
- GDP and national accounts are not designed to provide a complete measure of well-being.
- Market and non-market output may require different valuation approaches under national-accounting rules.

### Illustrative only
- The clean-water and market-information examples explain possible differences in perspectives; they do not estimate actual values or effects.
- The ₦5,000 willingness-to-pay and ₦4,000 transaction example is hypothetical.
- No claim is made about the measured welfare value of any Nigerian product, service or public programme.

### Questions to revisit
- How should Lifeconomist distinguish economic welfare, social welfare, well-being and usefulness in later applications?
- Which valuation techniques are appropriate for which decision types, and what evidence do they require?
- How should distributional effects be represented when a change benefits some groups and harms others?
- When can non-market values be monetized without creating false precision or double counting?
- How should these distinctions be reflected in specific measurement or monitoring products?

## 14. Short summary

“Value” has several meanings. Usefulness concerns what something does for people; willingness to pay concerns what someone would give up for it; price and exchange value concern exchange under specified conditions; value added is an accounting measure of production; and welfare value concerns broader effects on well-being under a stated framework. These measures may inform one another, but none can automatically replace the others. Always state whose value is being considered, what is valued, the valuation basis, and what evidence supports the conclusion.

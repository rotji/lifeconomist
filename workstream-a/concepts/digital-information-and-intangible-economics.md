## 1. Starting point: information can be economically useful without being a physical object

Modern economic activity depends on information, knowledge, software, data, designs, brands, algorithms, digital networks and other resources whose value is not explained by physical material alone. A retailer can use a stock-management system to reduce waste; a logistics company can use route information to improve delivery; a software developer can distribute a product to many customers without manufacturing a separate physical object for every user; and a business can own a valuable brand while owning relatively few physical assets.

These examples do not mean that digital activity is weightless or costless. It depends on electricity, devices, communications infrastructure, data centres, skilled people, financing, maintenance, legal rights, trust and institutional arrangements. The digital layer often changes how physical and human resources are coordinated rather than replacing them.

A reality-first investigation asks:

- What is the underlying resource: raw data, processed information, knowledge, software, a legal right, a network, a service or a combination?
- Who created it, who legally owns it, who controls access, who can use it and who benefits?
- What rights, contracts, licences, permissions or technical controls govern its use?
- What does it cost to create, validate, update, secure, store, deliver and maintain?
- Is the resource reusable, rival, excludable, replicable, transferable or dependent on a particular system?
- What customer, production or decision problem does it actually solve?
- How can its economic contribution be observed without confusing usage, price, valuation, revenue, profit and productivity?

The core principle is that **digital availability, information quality, legal ownership, economic usefulness and realized value are distinct conditions**.

## 2. Data, information, knowledge and intelligence

These terms are related but should not be treated as interchangeable.

- **Data:** Recorded observations, measurements, events, identifiers or symbols. Data may be raw, structured, unstructured, incomplete, erroneous or context-free.
- **Information:** Data organized and interpreted in a way that reduces uncertainty or supports understanding about a question.
- **Knowledge:** Understanding, skills, models or experience that allow a person or organization to interpret information and act in a context.
- **Intelligence:** In an analytical or operational setting, a product created by collecting, validating, contextualizing and interpreting information to answer a decision-relevant question. The word has other meanings in different disciplines, so the intended meaning should be stated.
- **Insight:** A conclusion or relationship that changes understanding of a problem. It may be useful, but its reliability depends on evidence and reasoning.
- **Decision support:** Information, analysis or a tool designed to help a decision-maker compare options. It informs a decision; it does not automatically make the decision or guarantee a good outcome.

A useful conceptual progression is:

**Observation/data → validation and context → information → interpretation and knowledge → decision-relevant intelligence → action → observed result and feedback.**

In plain language, collecting a number is not the same as understanding it. A data point becomes more informative when its source, date, unit, coverage and meaning are known. Analysis may turn it into a conclusion that helps someone choose an action, but the action still depends on incentives, authority, resources and judgement. The outcome must then be observed rather than assumed.

This progression is not a universal linear law. People can act on raw signals, use prior knowledge to decide what data to collect, and revise interpretations repeatedly. It is a checklist for tracing the transformation from an observation to a claimed outcome.

### Information value depends on the question

The same dataset may be valuable for one decision and irrelevant for another. A current wholesale price may help a shop decide whether to replenish stock but may not establish future demand. A national annual statistic may describe a broad trend but be too aggregated for a single shop's purchasing decision.

Ask: **useful to whom, for which decision, at what time, compared with which alternative, and at what cost?**

Information can be accurate yet not useful; useful-looking yet inaccurate; or accurate and useful but delivered too late. These are different failure modes.

## 3. Information quality: accuracy is necessary but not sufficient

Information quality has several dimensions, and the right priorities depend on the use case.

- **Accuracy:** How closely a value or claim corresponds to the relevant reality or reference.
- **Completeness:** Whether the required records, variables, entities or time periods are present.
- **Coverage:** Which population, market, geography, activity or event universe is represented.
- **Timeliness/freshness:** How recently the information was observed or updated relative to the decision.
- **Consistency:** Whether definitions and values agree across records, periods and sources when they should.
- **Validity:** Whether the field or method measures the concept it claims to measure.
- **Granularity:** The level of detail—such as country, region, firm, product, transaction or time interval.
- **Provenance:** Where information came from, how it was collected or transformed, and what chain of custody or version history can be established.
- **Reproducibility:** Whether another analyst can reconstruct the result from documented inputs, transformations and methods.
- **Representativeness:** Whether the observed records support conclusions about the intended population.
- **Independence:** Whether sources offer genuinely separate evidence or repeat the same original claim.
- **Interpretability:** Whether a user can understand what a result means, what assumptions it relies on and what it does not establish.

A source can be authoritative but too slow, timely but poorly verified, detailed but unrepresentative, or accurate for one purpose and invalid for another. A score combining these dimensions must document the dimensions and weights; a single “data quality score” is not self-explanatory.

### Freshness is relative to the decision

A ten-year-old geological map may remain useful for some physical features, while yesterday's retail price may already be stale. An annual population estimate can support broad planning but not minute-by-minute network operations. The relevant question is not simply “How old is the data?” but “How quickly can the underlying condition change, and how sensitive is the decision to that change?”

**Observation date + publication date + update frequency + decision sensitivity → practical freshness.**

In plain language, data age matters in relation to the thing being measured. Record when the underlying event happened, when the source published it, and when the analyst retrieved it. A recently published report may describe old events, while a regularly updated feed may still miss important cases.

## 4. Data products, information products and intelligence services

These are different layers of a possible economic offering.

- **Data product:** A dataset, API, feed, registry, document collection or structured record set delivered under defined access and quality terms.
- **Information product:** An organized view, report, comparison, alert, index or dashboard that gives context to data.
- **Analytical product:** A repeatable method, model or workflow that produces estimates, classifications, forecasts or evaluations.
- **Intelligence service:** An ongoing process that monitors evidence, interprets changes against a user's decision context, explains confidence and uncertainty, and delivers relevant findings.
- **Decision-support system:** A tool or workflow that helps users assess options, constraints or scenarios. It may combine data, rules, models, interfaces and human judgement.
- **Managed service:** A provider undertakes recurring work for a customer rather than only supplying a tool or information artefact.
- **Software as a service (SaaS):** Software delivered for recurring use, usually under a subscription or usage arrangement. SaaS describes a delivery/business model, not proof that the software creates value or fully performs a service.

A product can combine these layers. The important distinction is what the customer receives and what work remains for them.

For example, a price database gives a user records. A price dashboard organizes and visualizes them. An alerting service flags changes against specified rules. An intelligence service might explain whether the change appears unusual, what sources corroborate it, what possible mechanisms could matter, and what remains uncertain. Each additional layer creates potential value but also adds validation, interpretation, liability and delivery costs.

## 5. Intangible assets: what they are and what they are not

An **intangible asset** is generally a non-physical resource or right with economic value, subject to the applicable accounting, legal or analytical definition. Examples can include software, patents, licences, customer relationships, trademarks, copyrighted material, proprietary processes and certain data-related rights.

The category needs careful handling:

- A useful idea is not automatically a legally protected asset.
- A file is not the same as the rights to use, reproduce, distribute or commercialize its contents.
- A company may use software it does not own, under a licence.
- A brand can influence demand without being separable from the business under every valuation method.
- Internally generated knowledge can be economically important even when it is not recognized as an asset on the balance sheet.
- Some intangible spending is expensed rather than capitalized under applicable accounting standards.
- Accounting recognition, legal ownership, economic usefulness and market valuation answer different questions.

Do not infer that something is absent merely because it does not appear as a recognized balance-sheet asset. Equally, do not assume that every internally created dataset, customer list, idea or code repository has a defensible monetary value or can be sold lawfully.

### Economic asset, legal right and accounting asset

These lenses overlap but are not identical:

| Lens | Main question |
|---|---|
| Economic | Does the resource provide expected future benefits or improve decisions, production or exchange? |
| Legal | What enforceable rights, restrictions, licences and obligations apply? |
| Accounting | Does the item meet the relevant recognition and measurement requirements? |
| Commercial | Is there a buyer or user willing and able to pay under realistic terms? |
| Operational | Can the resource be accessed, maintained, transferred and used reliably? |

An intangible resource can be economically useful but difficult to transfer; legally protected but commercially unsuccessful; or commercially promising but not recognized as an accounting asset. The analyst should specify which lens is being used.

## 6. Reproducibility, low marginal cost and hidden costs

Digital goods and software often have a cost structure different from physical goods. Creating the first working version may require substantial design, engineering, research, data collection, testing and legal work. Producing another copy can be cheap, but delivering a reliable service at scale still has costs.

**Initial creation and validation → replication or delivery → continuing operations and maintenance.**

In plain language, the first version of software or a dataset may be expensive to create. Making another copy can cost very little, but the provider may still pay for hosting, bandwidth, support, security, updates, licences, customer acquisition, compliance and correcting errors. “Near-zero marginal cost” for a copy is not the same as “zero total cost” for a reliable business.

Relevant cost categories include:

- Research, design, coding, documentation and testing.
- Data acquisition, collection permissions, cleaning, labelling and verification.
- Compute, storage, networking, power and physical infrastructure.
- Security, privacy controls, backups, incident response and access management.
- Updating, monitoring, customer support and compatibility maintenance.
- Sales, distribution, integration, onboarding and training.
- Licensing, contractual obligations, legal review and compliance.
- Failure costs, downtime, model errors, misleading results and reputational damage.
- Switching, migration, interoperability and eventual retirement.

A digital business can scale quickly, but it can also accumulate maintenance debt, security exposure, dependence on a platform provider, data licensing costs and support obligations. Assess total cost and reliability, not just the cost of making a copy.

## 7. Software, algorithms and AI-enabled production

Software is a set of instructions, data structures and related components used to perform tasks or coordinate systems. Algorithms are procedures for transforming inputs into outputs. Artificial intelligence (AI) systems may infer patterns, generate content, classify cases, recommend actions or automate parts of a workflow.

The presence of software or AI does not itself establish productivity improvement. A system's effect depends on task fit, input quality, error rates, workflow integration, worker skills, adoption, oversight, reliability and the cost of implementation.

Potential channels include:

- Automating repetitive tasks.
- Reducing search, coordination or processing time.
- Improving prediction, classification or quality control.
- Enabling new products and services.
- Extending a service to more users or locations.
- Improving consistency, documentation and auditability.
- Supporting decisions with more timely or better-organized evidence.

Potential costs and risks include:

- Incorrect or fabricated outputs.
- Automation of the wrong process.
- Biased or unrepresentative data.
- Security, privacy and intellectual-property issues.
- Overreliance on recommendations.
- Difficult-to-observe quality deterioration.
- New dependencies on vendors, cloud infrastructure or proprietary models.
- Job displacement, work intensification or unequal distribution of gains.
- Costs of review, exceptions, integration, monitoring and fallback procedures.

A valid evaluation compares the complete workflow before and after introduction, including quality, speed, cost, failure, human review and downstream outcomes. Counting generated outputs, automated tasks or user clicks is not enough.

## 7.1 AI service economics: training, inference, utilization and human review

The cost of an AI-enabled service is not captured by the price of a model call alone. **Training** or fine-tuning costs may be substantial but occur at particular stages or intervals; **inference** costs recur when a model is used. A deployed service may also require retrieval and data preparation, orchestration, storage, network traffic, security, evaluation, monitoring, human review, customer support and recovery from errors. Which costs matter most depends on model choice, task, usage pattern and service quality requirements.

The relevant unit must be defined before calculating unit economics: per request, completed task, accepted result, active customer, document processed or decision supported. Cost per generated response can be misleading if many responses are rejected, corrected or never used. A more decision-relevant measure may be cost per **accepted and useful outcome**, including the human time and downstream costs required to reach it.

Utilization also matters. Fixed infrastructure or reserved capacity spread over few successful tasks can make unit costs high; greater utilization can lower average cost until capacity constraints, latency, quality degradation or additional staffing create new costs. Cheap output is not necessarily economical if error handling, review or failure consequences are expensive.

**Model and infrastructure costs + data and workflow costs + human review and failure costs → total cost per defined useful outcome → comparison with the existing process and realized benefit.**

In plain language, an AI service should be compared with the work it actually completes, not merely the number of answers it generates. If a person must verify every output, repair mistakes and manage exceptions, those costs belong in the comparison. The service creates economic value only if the quality-adjusted benefits—such as time saved, fewer errors or better decisions—justify its total cost and risks.

A defensible evaluation records the task and unit, model/version, workload, utilization, latency, quality and error thresholds, human-review rate, failure severity, data and infrastructure costs, baseline workflow, and the period over which fixed costs are allocated. Report uncertainty and avoid assuming that a benchmark result will transfer unchanged to another workload or user population.

## 8. Network effects, platforms and market power

A **network effect** occurs when the value or usefulness of a product or service to a user changes as the number or composition of other users or participants changes. Some networks become more useful as participation grows, but the strength and direction of the effect are empirical questions.

A platform may connect buyers and sellers, drivers and passengers, developers and users, advertisers and audiences, or data providers and consumers. Its role may include matching, search, payments, identity, reputation, dispute resolution, access control and rule enforcement.

Distinguish:

- **Direct network effects:** More users of the same type may make a network more useful to each other.
- **Indirect or cross-side network effects:** Participation on one side may increase value for another side, such as more sellers attracting buyers.
- **Economies of scale:** Average cost may decline as output or usage increases; this is not the same mechanism as a network effect.
- **Economies of scope:** Shared capabilities reduce the cost of offering related products or services.
- **Switching costs:** Costs or losses users face when moving to another provider.
- **Multi-homing:** Users or businesses participate in more than one platform.
- **Interoperability:** Different systems can exchange data or work together.
- **Lock-in:** Users or counterparties face practical, technical, contractual or social barriers to exit.
- **Market power:** Ability to influence prices, access, terms, quality or competitive conditions; it requires evidence rather than inference from size alone.

**More participation → potentially better matching or more complementary offerings → greater usefulness → potential further participation.**

In plain language, a platform can become more useful as more relevant participants join, which can attract still more users. But the loop is not guaranteed: congestion, fraud, low quality, weak trust, high fees or poor matching can make growth reduce usefulness. Market dominance does not by itself prove abuse, and a large user count does not establish profitable or socially beneficial operation.

Platforms may lower coordination costs and enable small businesses to reach new customers. They may also control access, rank participants, set fees, limit data portability or change terms unilaterally. Investigate both value creation and control over the rules.

## 9. Information asymmetry, verification and trust

**Information asymmetry** exists when parties to a decision or transaction have different relevant information. A seller may know more about product quality than a buyer; a borrower may know more about business risk than a lender; a public agency may publish a statistic whose limitations users do not understand.

Information asymmetry can create adverse selection, moral hazard, mistrust, costly verification or market failure. But asymmetry is not always harmful: specialized knowledge and privacy can be legitimate, and eliminating all information differences may be impossible or undesirable.

Verification can reduce some uncertainty by checking a claim against records, independent observations, signatures, physical traces or alternative sources. Verification has limits:

- A valid signature can show that a key signed a record, not that the record's factual content is true.
- A timestamp can support when a record was created or received under a particular system, not necessarily when the underlying event happened.
- A source's reputation can be relevant but does not eliminate error.
- Multiple reports may repeat one original source rather than independently corroborate it.
- A public record may be authentic but outdated, incomplete or wrongly interpreted.
- A model's confidence score is not automatically a calibrated probability that the claim is true.

A defensible evidence product should preserve source, observation date, publication date, retrieval date, transformations, definitions, corroboration, uncertainty and the distinction between observed trace and inferred conclusion.

## 10. Search costs, coordination costs and decision value

Digital tools can reduce the cost of finding information, comparing options, communicating, matching buyers and sellers, coordinating work and monitoring changes. These savings can create real economic value when they improve an actual decision or process.

Examples include:

- A retailer checks stock and supplier prices before reordering.
- A pharmacy verifies product registration or official safety notices.
- A logistics operator detects a route interruption and compares alternatives.
- A lender obtains more reliable information about an applicant, subject to lawful access and fair-use constraints.
- A public agency links records to detect duplication or service gaps.
- A business monitors changes in regulation, competitors or infrastructure that may affect operations.

But a dashboard or report does not create value merely because it contains many data points. The value depends on whether the information changes a decision, reduces error, avoids cost, enables revenue, reduces uncertainty or improves outcomes compared with the best feasible alternative.

A simple decision-value framework is:

**Expected value with information − expected value without information − total information and implementation cost.**

In plain language, compare the expected outcome when a decision-maker has the information with the expected outcome when they do not, then subtract the cost of obtaining and acting on it. This is a conceptual framework, not a directly measurable formula in every situation. Estimation requires a defined decision, alternatives, probabilities or scenarios, consequences, and the costs of obtaining and using information. Information can have negative net value if it is misleading, arrives too late, distracts the user or creates costs greater than the benefit.

## 11. Data rights, privacy, licensing and governance

The fact that information is technically accessible does not mean every use is lawful, ethical, safe or commercially permitted. Access may be governed by copyright, database rights where applicable, confidentiality, contract, data-protection law, sector-specific regulation, platform terms, trade secrets or other legal rules. The rules differ by jurisdiction and data type and must be checked for the specific case.

Distinguish:

- **Access:** Ability to view or obtain the information.
- **Possession:** Having a copy or holding the medium.
- **Control:** Practical ability to decide how it is accessed or used.
- **Ownership:** A legal or economic relationship whose meaning depends on the object and governing law.
- **Licence:** Permission to perform specified acts subject to terms.
- **Copyright:** A legal framework protecting qualifying expression, not automatically every underlying fact or idea.
- **Personal data:** Information relating to an identified or identifiable person under the applicable legal definition.
- **Confidential information/trade secret:** Information protected under applicable rules and circumstances; not every non-public item automatically qualifies.
- **Consent:** A particular legal basis or permission in some contexts; it is not the only possible basis and must meet applicable requirements.
- **Data governance:** The roles, policies, controls, responsibilities and processes for data quality, access, security, retention, sharing and accountability.

Responsible data use needs a documented purpose, lawful access, minimization, security, retention rules, correction pathways and clarity about who is accountable. Removing direct identifiers does not automatically make a dataset anonymous, particularly where records can be linked to other sources.

A commercially valuable dataset may still be unusable for a proposed product because rights are unclear, consent or legal basis is missing, re-identification risk is high, the source terms prohibit redistribution, or the data are too unreliable. Governance is therefore part of the product's operating model, not an administrative afterthought.

## 12. Intangible value and accounting boundaries

Digital and intangible activity creates measurement challenges because the economic benefit may be distributed over time, shared across products, difficult to separate from organizational capital, or poorly represented by conventional asset records.

Examples:

- Software development may be expensed or capitalized depending on the applicable accounting rules and circumstances.
- Training can improve organizational capability without being recorded as a separable asset.
- A brand can support customer demand, but its value depends on assumptions about future cash flows, comparables or another valuation method.
- A dataset may have reuse value, yet be non-transferable or rapidly lose value as conditions change.
- A free digital service may create user benefits and advertising or transaction revenue without charging each user directly.
- A company can have high market valuation relative to book assets because investors expect future returns; that gap is not a direct measurement of a single intangible asset.

Market capitalization is not an independently verified appraisal of every intangible resource. A valuation reflects expectations, risk, financing conditions, competitive position, market power and other factors, and can change without any corresponding change in physical assets.

National accounts also use defined production and asset boundaries. Not every useful digital activity, free service, data collection activity or internally generated intangible is measured as a separate asset or output in the way a non-specialist might expect. Consult the relevant national-accounting and accounting standards rather than assuming all digital value is omitted or fully captured.

## 13. Measuring digital productivity and economic contribution

Potential measures include:

- Software and data investment, with the relevant accounting and national-accounting definitions.
- ICT capital services and infrastructure availability.
- Digital service use, active usage and service reliability.
- Time to complete a task, cost per completed task and error/rework rates.
- Customer acquisition, retention, conversion, transaction success and support burden.
- Output and value added in ICT-producing industries and in digitally enabled industries.
- Labour productivity and multifactor productivity, using consistent output and input definitions.
- Adoption rates and the distribution of adoption by firm size, sector and region.
- Network coverage, affordability, latency, downtime and power reliability.
- Data freshness, completeness, validation error, correction rate and source diversity.
- Security incidents, privacy failures and the costs of compliance and recovery.
- User or customer outcomes relative to a baseline or comparison group.

Do not treat the following as interchangeable:

- Number of records collected versus useful coverage.
- Number of API calls versus successful decisions.
- User registrations versus active, retained and served users.
- AI-generated content versus accepted, accurate and useful work.
- Software spending versus realized productivity gains.
- Company valuation versus the value of an individually identified asset.
- Data volume versus information quality.
- Network size versus network usefulness or market power.
- Digital access versus effective ability to use a service.

Productivity comparisons require a defined unit of output and input. Digital services may improve quality, variety, convenience, speed or accessibility in ways that simple revenue or volume measures do not capture. At the same time, claims of unmeasured quality improvement should be supported rather than used to dismiss unfavourable results.

## 14. Worked example: a retailer's information service

Consider an illustrative small retailer that has irregular access to supplier prices, stock availability and product safety updates. A proposed information service provides dated supplier quotes, flags large changes, and links official notices to relevant products.

The potential mechanism is:

**Verified and timely records → better comparison and earlier detection → changed ordering or compliance decision → possible reduction in avoidable cost or risk.**

In plain language, the service can help only if the records are accurate enough, arrive before the retailer's decision, cover relevant suppliers and products, and are easy to act on. If the information is stale, does not match the retailer's products, or arrives after purchasing, it may provide little benefit or cause harm.

Before claiming that the service works, define and test:

1. The exact decision and existing workflow.
2. The current cost, delay, error or risk that the service is meant to improve.
3. The source coverage, update schedule and verification method.
4. The retailer's ability to change suppliers or purchasing quantities.
5. The baseline and outcome metrics, such as time spent comparing prices, stockouts, avoidable losses or verified compliance actions.
6. The full cost of subscription, staff time, integration and data correction.
7. Whether observed improvements can reasonably be attributed to the service rather than seasonality, supplier changes or other factors.
8. Whether the customer is willing to pay enough to support continued delivery.

The example is hypothetical. It demonstrates how to test an information service; it is not evidence of demand, efficacy or commercial viability for any real retailer or market.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Data vs information | Data are recorded observations; information is data interpreted in context for a question. |
| Information vs knowledge | Information conveys content; knowledge includes understanding and capability to interpret or use it. |
| Knowledge vs intelligence | Intelligence is usually a decision-oriented analytical product in this context; usage varies by field. |
| Intelligence vs decision | Intelligence informs a decision; it does not automatically make or guarantee it. |
| Accuracy vs freshness | Accuracy concerns correspondence to reality; freshness concerns recency relative to the decision. |
| Completeness vs coverage | Completeness concerns missing required elements; coverage concerns which universe is represented. |
| Provenance vs truth | Provenance describes origin and transformations; it does not alone prove a claim is true. |
| Corroboration vs repetition | Independent corroboration adds separate evidence; repeated copies may all trace to one source. |
| Data access vs data rights | Technical access does not establish permission for every reuse, redistribution or commercial purpose. |
| Possession vs ownership | Holding a copy or device is not the same as owning all rights in its contents. |
| Software vs digital service | Software is an artefact or system; a service includes ongoing delivery, operation and support. |
| Digital product vs intelligence service | A product may deliver data or tools; an intelligence service also interprets evidence for an ongoing decision context. |
| Intangible resource vs recognized accounting asset | Economic usefulness does not guarantee accounting recognition or a separately recorded value. |
| Intellectual property vs information | IP rights apply to defined subject matter under law; not every fact, idea or dataset is protected in the same way. |
| Data volume vs data value | More records do not guarantee relevance, quality or decision usefulness. |
| Network effect vs economies of scale | Network effects depend on participation and connections; scale economies concern cost per unit. |
| Platform growth vs market power | User growth alone does not prove an ability to control prices, access or competitive conditions. |
| Market capitalization vs asset valuation | Market capitalization values equity at the market price; it is not a direct appraisal of each intangible asset. |
| Software spending vs productivity gain | Expenditure is an input; productivity change is an outcome that must be measured. |
| Automation vs augmentation | Automation substitutes for specified tasks; augmentation supports or changes human work. Both effects depend on context. |
| Privacy vs secrecy | Privacy concerns appropriate control and use of information; secrecy concerns whether information is concealed. They overlap but differ. |
| Anonymization vs removing identifiers | Removing names may not prevent re-identification through linkage or distinctive attributes. |
| Open data vs unrestricted use | Public availability does not necessarily remove licence, privacy, security or other legal limits. |
| AI confidence vs calibrated probability | A model score may not correspond to a verified probability of correctness. |
| Low marginal cost vs zero total cost | Copying can be cheap while development, operations, security, support and governance remain costly. |
| Digitalization vs digital transformation | Digitization can convert information to digital form; digitalization changes processes, while transformation implies broader organizational change. |
| Digital activity vs weightless activity | Digital services still depend on physical infrastructure, energy, materials and human work. |
| Usage metric vs outcome metric | Usage records activity; outcome metrics measure what changed for the user or system. |
| Correlation vs causal effect | Observed association does not establish that the information product or technology caused the result. |

## 17. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual points

- Data, information, knowledge, intelligence and decision outcomes are related but distinct.
- Information quality is multidimensional; freshness, accuracy, completeness, coverage and provenance should not be collapsed without justification.
- Digital products rely on physical infrastructure, people, rights and ongoing operations.
- Intangible economic usefulness, legal ownership, accounting recognition and commercial value are different questions.
- Network effects and scale economies are distinct mechanisms.
- Software or AI use does not automatically establish productivity gains.
- Access to data does not automatically grant permission for every use or redistribution.
- An information service's value depends on the decision, timing, alternatives, reliability, user response and total costs.

### Scope limits

- This note does not estimate the size of the digital economy, intangible capital, AI productivity gains or the value of any specific dataset or company.
- It does not provide legal advice or determine data rights for a particular jurisdiction or source.
- It does not claim that any proposed information service has customer demand or commercial viability.
- It does not prescribe one universal data-quality score or intangible valuation method.
- Empirical claims about productivity, platform market power, welfare or causal effects require separate evidence and methods.
- Accounting and national-accounting treatment must be checked against the standards and definitions applicable to the specific question.

### Questions for follow-up

- Which digital and information activities are represented consistently in national accounts, and which require complementary measures?
- How should Lifeconomist record source provenance, freshness, confidence and dependencies across repeated monitoring?
- What evidence is sufficient to distinguish a genuinely independent source from a syndicated copy?
- How can the value of decision support be measured when outcomes are rare, delayed or affected by many other factors?
- Which costs and risks are hidden when an AI tool appears to automate a task cheaply?
- How should rights, lawful access, privacy and redistribution constraints be documented in an intelligence pipeline?
- Which measures can show whether a digital platform creates value for participants as well as capturing value for itself?
- How should changes in digital service quality, convenience and access be compared when prices or output volumes are poor proxies?

## 18. Short summary

Digital and intangible economics studies how data, information, knowledge, software, intellectual property, platforms and digital services contribute to production, exchange and decision-making. It distinguishes raw data from validated information and decision-relevant intelligence; usefulness from legal rights, accounting recognition and commercial value; network effects from scale economies; and software adoption from measured productivity gains. Digital systems still rely on physical infrastructure, energy, people, governance and continuing costs. Lifeconomist should trace each information product from source and quality through interpretation, decision, action and observed result, while preserving provenance, uncertainty, rights and the difference between plausible mechanisms and demonstrated effects.

# Concept 8 — Capital Formation, Depreciation, Maintenance and Replacement

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Productivity and Productive Capacity](productivity-and-productive-capacity.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain how productive assets are created, acquired, maintained, consumed and replaced; distinguish capital formation from financial investment and purchases of existing assets; connect capital accumulation to productive capacity and productivity.

## 1. The central distinction

**Capital formation** concerns adding to or improving the stock of produced assets under a specified accounting framework. It is not synonymous with saving, money, buying shares, borrowing, or every transaction commonly called an investment.

A business may buy a new machine, build a warehouse, purchase another firm's shares, buy a used machine, restock goods for resale, or service existing equipment. These transactions differ economically and in accounting treatment. Some add to produced fixed assets; some are financial transactions; some are current expenses or inventory; and some transfer ownership of an existing asset without creating a new asset for the economy as a whole.

Always identify the question:
- **Business decision:** Does the expenditure improve operations or expected returns?
- **Financial accounting:** Is it an asset, expense, inventory, or financial asset under applicable standards?
- **National accounts:** Is it gross fixed capital formation, a change in inventories, a financial transaction, or something else?
- **Productivity analysis:** Does it add to capital services available for production?

These perspectives overlap but are not identical.

## 2. Different meanings of capital

- **Produced productive assets:** machinery, buildings, equipment, infrastructure and qualifying software used over time.
- **Capital stock:** accumulated assets of a defined entity, industry or economy, measured using a stated valuation and depreciation method.
- **Financial capital or funds:** money and financing available to support activity or acquire assets.
- **Financial assets:** claims such as shares, bonds and deposits. They may provide funding or ownership rights, but are not themselves the same as a machine or building.
- **Human capital:** skills, knowledge, health and capabilities embodied in people. It matters for production but is not generally recorded as an owned produced asset in standard national balance sheets in the same way as machinery.
- **Natural or social capital:** terms used in broader frameworks for natural resources, networks or institutions; definitions and measurement must be stated.

A firm can have finance but lack productive equipment, skilled workers, reliable electricity or demand. It can also own valuable equipment but lack cash for inventory or wages. Do not use capital as a vague substitute for money, wealth, finance or productive capacity.

## 3. Capital formation and gross fixed capital formation

In national accounts, **gross fixed capital formation (GFCF)** measures acquisitions, less disposals, of produced fixed assets during a period, under the applicable System of National Accounts.

Fixed assets are produced assets used repeatedly or continuously in production for more than one year. Examples may include buildings, machinery, transport equipment, infrastructure, and qualifying software or research and development, depending on the framework.

GFCF may include:
- construction of new productive buildings and infrastructure;
- new machinery and equipment;
- qualifying software and intellectual-property products;
- major improvements that increase capacity, performance or useful life;
- own-account production of qualifying fixed assets.

It is not simply all money spent by businesses or government. Ordinary wages, many routine repairs, purchases of financial securities, and transfers of existing assets are not automatically GFCF. Formal classifications depend on the asset and transaction.

**Gross** means consumption of fixed capital has not been deducted. Gross investment can therefore be positive even while existing assets wear out or become obsolete.

## 4. Gross versus net capital formation

A simplified conceptual relationship is:

**Net capital formation = gross capital formation − consumption of fixed capital**

This expresses the distinction, not a complete formula for every national-accounting category.

- **Gross capital formation** measures qualifying additions before deducting consumption of fixed capital.
- **Consumption of fixed capital (CFC)** estimates the decline in the current value of fixed assets used in production during the period, attributable to physical deterioration, normal obsolescence and normal accidental damage under national-accounting assumptions.
- **Net capital formation** deducts CFC from gross capital formation.

If gross additions are smaller than CFC, net fixed capital formation can be negative even while new assets are still acquired. If gross additions exceed CFC, the stock may grow in net terms, subject to disposals, revaluations and the measurement method.

**Illustration:** Suppose qualifying new assets worth ₦10 million are acquired in a year and the relevant estimate of asset consumption is ₦7 million. The ₦3 million difference is only a simplified illustration of additions beyond measured consumption; it is not automatically the exact change in market value or in the economy's capital stock. Disposals, price changes, asset lives and valuation rules matter.

## 5. Business accounting: capitalise or expense?

In financial accounting, an outlay may be recognized as an asset when it meets the relevant recognition requirements; otherwise it may be expensed, included in inventory or treated another way. Applicable standards, materiality and circumstances matter.

- **Capital expenditure (CapEx):** spending to acquire, construct or improve long-lived assets, as defined for the reporting purpose.
- **Operating expenditure (OpEx):** spending associated with ongoing operations; the label is common in management reporting, not a universal rule that determines accounting treatment.
- **Inventory:** goods held for sale or materials used in production. Inventory is generally an asset until sold or consumed.
- **Financial investment:** acquisition of shares, bonds or other claims; it is not the same as creating a new productive fixed asset.
- **Maintenance and repair:** often expensed when they keep an asset operating at its intended level, while qualifying improvements may be capitalized.

An invoice label such as “upgrade,” “repair,” or “investment” does not decide treatment by itself. The nature of the work and applicable rules matter.

## 6. Depreciation versus consumption of fixed capital

**Depreciation** in business accounting allocates an asset's depreciable amount over its estimated useful life using a method consistent with the relevant accounting framework. It is usually a periodic expense, not the cash payment for a replacement asset.

**Consumption of fixed capital (CFC)** in national accounts is related but distinct. It estimates the decline in the current value of fixed assets used in production under national-accounting assumptions. It can differ from company depreciation because valuation bases, useful lives, methods, coverage and assumptions differ.

Assets may lose productive value through physical wear, normal obsolescence, or normal accidental damage under the relevant framework. Book value, market value, replacement cost and productive service potential are not interchangeable.

Depreciation is sometimes described as “money set aside to replace an asset.” The accounting entry itself does not set aside cash. Actual replacement requires a decision, financing, availability and a cash outlay. A business may spend cash elsewhere and still record depreciation.

## 7. Maintenance, repair, replacement and improvement

- **Routine maintenance:** keeps an asset in intended operating condition, such as servicing a generator.
- **Repair:** restores an asset after a fault or damage.
- **Replacement:** retires or removes an asset and obtains another for a similar function.
- **Improvement or upgrade:** changes an asset's capability, capacity, efficiency, quality or expected useful life.
- **Expansion:** adds assets or capacity to serve more production or demand.
- **Replacement investment:** expenditure primarily to renew worn-out, obsolete or inadequate assets rather than expand scale.

Routine maintenance and repairs are often expensed, while qualifying improvements may be capitalized. The treatment depends on the applicable rules. Economically, even a current expense can be essential to preserve productive capacity; conversely, an asset capitalized in accounts is not guaranteed to earn a good return.

Postponed maintenance can reduce immediate outflows but increase downtime, breakdown, safety and future replacement risks. Maintenance itself has costs and may require downtime, so decisions depend on asset condition, expected use, failure risk and consequences.

## 8. New assets versus transfers of existing assets

Buying a newly produced machine can contribute to GFCF. Buying a used machine from another domestic business generally transfers ownership of an existing asset; it does not create a second machine or automatically add the full purchase price to economy-wide new capital formation. National accounts may include ownership-transfer costs and other specific treatments.

The buyer may still record the used machine as an acquired asset in its own accounts. Entity-level accounting and economy-wide capital formation answer different questions.

Similarly:
- Buying shares on an exchange transfers a financial claim; it does not by itself create productive assets.
- A company issuing new shares raises finance; the issue itself differs from how the company later uses the funds.
- A loan may finance equipment, but borrowing is a financial transaction, not capital formation by itself.
- A newly constructed road may contribute to fixed capital formation; routine road maintenance is generally treated differently.
- Buying land is not equivalent to producing a fixed asset, though buildings and qualifying land improvements have specific treatment.

## 9. Inventories and capital formation

**Inventories** are goods held for sale, in production or to be used in production. Changes in inventories can form part of gross capital formation in national accounts, but inventory is different from fixed capital.

For a retailer:
- unsold goods held for resale are inventory;
- a refrigerator used for years may be a fixed asset if it meets relevant criteria;
- electricity to operate the shop is a current input;
- routine servicing is generally maintenance;
- buying a new refrigerator may count as fixed capital formation under the applicable rules.

Inventory can rise because a business intentionally builds stock, because goods are in transit, or because sales unexpectedly fall. An increase is not necessarily evidence of a deliberate investment decision.

## 10. Saving, financial investment and financing

- **Saving** is income not used for current consumption under a specified definition. Household, corporate and national saving have different boundaries.
- **Financial investment** commonly means acquiring financial assets such as shares, bonds or deposits.
- **Productive investment** often means expenditure on assets used to produce goods or services, although the exact term varies by context.
- **Capital formation** is the formal addition to produced assets under a defined accounting framework.
- **Financing** describes how an activity is funded, for example from retained earnings, loans, equity issuance or grants.
- **Capital allocation** concerns directing funds and resources among possible uses.

A household deposit is a financial asset for the household; the deposit itself does not create a machine. A bank may use its funding and balance-sheet capacity to support lending, but whether that leads to new productive investment depends on later decisions and conditions.

Buying an existing share changes ownership of a claim; it does not automatically finance new productive investment. A new share issue can provide funds to a company, but the real-economy effect depends on how the funds are used.

## 11. Capital stock and productive capacity

**Capital stock** is an asset stock at a point in time. **Capital formation** is a flow over a period. A stock and a flow are different quantities.

A simplified intuition is:

**Ending capital stock ≈ starting capital stock + additions − retirements and asset consumption**

This is not an official estimation formula. Capital-stock estimates require consistent asset classifications, service lives, depreciation profiles, prices, retirements and valuation methods; revaluation and other changes can also matter.

New capital can increase capacity, improve quality or lower costs, but only with complementary inputs such as skilled workers, reliable energy, materials, spare parts, logistics, finance, demand, maintenance and compatible organisation.

A machine that sits idle because power or raw materials are unavailable may increase the recorded asset base without materially increasing actual output. Capital contributes through use and interaction with other inputs, not merely because it has been purchased.

## 12. Worked small-retailer example

A retailer considers three purchases:
1. ₦300,000 of additional goods for resale.
2. A ₦250,000 refrigerator expected to be used for several years.
3. ₦25,000 for routine servicing of an existing refrigerator.

The first is likely inventory, the second may qualify as a fixed asset, and the third is routine maintenance often treated as a current expense. Exact treatment depends on the relevant standards, materiality and circumstances.

If the retailer borrows ₦250,000 to buy the refrigerator, the loan provides financing and creates a liability; the refrigerator is the asset acquired. The loan is not the same as the asset purchase. If the retailer instead buys shares worth ₦250,000, that is a financial investment, not a refrigerator and not automatically new productive capital for the economy.

All amounts are hypothetical; this is an illustration, not accounting advice.

## 13. Why capital formation matters—and what it cannot prove

Capital formation can support expanded capacity, better tools and infrastructure, improved reliability, quality or energy efficiency, and lower resource use per unit. It does not guarantee productivity growth or economic development. Assets may be poorly selected, overpriced, underused, badly maintained, mismatched to demand, or dependent on missing infrastructure.

**Capital deepening** means more capital input per unit of labour. It can raise labour productivity even without a rise in multifactor productivity. Multifactor productivity attempts to account for changes in combined measured inputs; its residual is not automatically pure innovation or technology.

A capital-spending announcement is not proof that an asset was funded, purchased, delivered, installed, operating or productive. These are distinct stages that require different evidence.

## 14. Interpretation checklist

Before interpreting a capital-formation or investment figure, ask:

1. Is the framework business accounting, national accounts, financial markets or a management budget?
2. Is the number a flow over a period or an asset stock at a point in time?
3. Are the items newly produced assets, existing assets, financial claims, inventories, land or another category?
4. Is the measure gross or net of consumption of fixed capital?
5. Which asset types are included, and how are software, R&D and major improvements treated?
6. Is valuation based on historical cost, current prices, replacement cost or another basis?
7. How are depreciation, useful life, retirements and obsolescence handled?
8. Does the figure include maintenance, repairs, ownership-transfer costs, taxes or installation?
9. How is the asset financed, and are financing and the asset acquired kept distinct?
10. Is the asset operating, and are complementary inputs available?
11. Is the claim about acquisition, capacity, productivity, profitability or welfare? Each requires different evidence.
12. Are definitions consistent across firms, sectors, countries and time?

## 15. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Money, wealth and capital:** money can finance assets; wealth is a broader net-worth concept; capital has productive, financial and other meanings.
- **Production and output:** an asset is an input to production, but its purchase price is not automatically output from the buyer's ordinary production.
- **Costs and prices:** capital expenditure, maintenance, depreciation and unit costs differ in meaning and timing.
- **Revenue and receipts:** borrowing and asset purchases affect cash and balance sheets but are not automatically revenue or expenses in the same amount and period.
- **Productivity:** capital services affect labour productivity; capital deepening is not identical to multifactor productivity growth.
- **Balance sheets:** fixed assets, inventory, cash, financial assets and liabilities must be distinguished by category and valuation.
- **Workstream B:** a formal GFCF indicator requires a defined source, asset coverage, price basis, revision policy and national-accounting methodology.
- **Workstream C:** announced investment, committed funding, expenditure, delivery, installation and productive operation are distinct stages.
- **Workstream D:** procurement records, construction progress, asset registers and operating evidence can corroborate different stages; no single record proves full productive use.
- **Workstream E:** an asset shortage may suggest a constraint, but does not alone establish demand for a paid service or prove equipment purchase is the best solution.

## 16. Evidence status and unresolved questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Capital formation differs from financial investment, saving, borrowing and all spending called investment.
- Gross measures do not deduct consumption of fixed capital; net measures do under the defined framework.
- Business depreciation and national-accounts CFC are related but not interchangeable.
- Transfers of existing assets can change ownership without creating an equivalent amount of new productive assets economy-wide.
- Maintenance, replacement and expansion have different purposes; accounting treatment depends on applicable rules.
- Asset acquisition does not guarantee productive use, productivity growth, profit or wider welfare improvements.

### Illustrative only
- Currency amounts and business examples are hypothetical.
- No Nigerian capital-formation figure, capital stock, depreciation rate or investment trend is estimated.
- The simplified stock-flow relationship is a conceptual aid, not an official estimation formula.

### Questions to revisit
- Which Nigerian NBS publications define GFCF and its asset composition?
- How are capital formation and CFC measured in the national-accounting framework used by Nigeria?
- What capital-stock proxies are defensible when asset-register data are unavailable?
- How should small retailers distinguish inventory, equipment, maintenance and improvements in lightweight records?
- What evidence distinguishes an announced investment, committed funding, expenditure, delivery, installation and productive operation?

## 17. Short summary

Capital formation adds to produced assets under a defined framework; financial investment, saving, borrowing and transfers of existing assets are different concepts. Gross capital formation is measured before deducting consumption of fixed capital; net capital formation deducts it. Business depreciation is not a cash reserve, and national-accounts CFC is not automatically equal to company depreciation. Maintenance preserves assets, replacement renews them, and expansion adds capacity. Assets improve production only when suitable and used with complementary inputs.

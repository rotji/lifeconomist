# Concept 13 — National Income, GDP and Economic Accounting

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal statistical specifications remain in Workstream B  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 12 — Markets, Prices and Exchange](markets-prices-and-exchange.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain what GDP and national accounts measure, how the production, expenditure and income approaches relate, and why GDP, national income, wealth, welfare and financial transactions must not be treated as interchangeable.

## 1. Why national accounting exists

An economy contains many producers, workers, households, firms, governments and cross-border transactions. National accounting provides a consistent framework for organizing selected economic activities into comparable aggregates. It is not a complete inventory of everything valuable in society and it is not simply a tally of cash moving through banks.

The System of National Accounts (SNA) defines concepts, boundaries, classifications and accounting relationships for measuring production, income, consumption, saving, investment and financial positions. Published statistics depend on the framework version, source data, compilation methods, reference period and revisions.

National accounts are designed to be internally coherent. That coherence does not make every estimate exact: data can be incomplete, delayed, revised or modelled. Interpret an aggregate together with its definition, coverage, units, timing and uncertainty.

## 2. Gross domestic product (GDP)

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** measures the value of final goods and services produced within an economy's domestic production boundary during a specified period, under the applicable national-accounting framework. “Domestic” refers to the production territory defined by the accounts, not simply to the nationality of the owner. “Gross” means consumption of fixed capital has not been deducted from the aggregate.

GDP is a flow over a period—such as a quarter or year—not a stock of all assets owned at a date. It is not the same as:
- the total money circulating in the economy;
- the total value of bank deposits or financial assets;
- government revenue or government spending;
- the market capitalization of listed companies;
- national wealth or the value of all land and natural resources;
- the total amount of money exchanged in every transaction;
- a direct measure of happiness, equality or living standards.

GDP includes qualifying production by resident and non-resident-owned entities operating within the domestic production boundary. Income received by domestic residents from abroad and income paid to foreign owners are handled through other national-accounting aggregates and accounts.

## 3. Production boundary and final output

GDP does not include every activity that is useful or every transfer of money. It measures production as defined by the statistical framework. Market production is central, and specified non-market production and imputed items are also included under established rules.

Examples of activities that may be within the production boundary include producing food for sale, retail services, transport, professional services, public education services, construction and qualifying software production. Government non-market output is often valued using production costs because there may be no market price.

Unpaid household services such as ordinary cooking, cleaning and care for one's own household are generally outside the core GDP production boundary, although some related goods and specified services are included. This means GDP is not a measure of all socially useful work. Boundaries are defined for comparability and practicality, not as a declaration that excluded activity has no value.

Illegal or informal activity is not automatically outside the conceptual production boundary, but actual statistical coverage varies with definitions, source data and national compilation practices. Do not assume that unrecorded activity is either fully counted or entirely absent.

## 4. Final goods, intermediate inputs and double counting

A **final good or service** is acquired for final use rather than further use in producing other goods and services within the relevant accounting context. An **intermediate input** is used up or transformed in producing output during the period. The classification depends on use, not merely on the physical item.

Consider a simplified bakery:
- A farmer sells wheat to a mill.
- The mill sells flour to a bakery.
- The bakery sells bread to a household.

Adding the full sales values at all three stages would count the value embodied in wheat and flour more than once. National accounting avoids this by counting final expenditure on bread, or by summing the **value added** at each production stage, with consistent boundaries and accounting treatment.

An item can be an intermediate input for one buyer and a final good for another. A computer bought by a household for personal use is final consumption; a computer bought by a business for use over multiple years may qualify as fixed capital; a computer purchased for resale is inventory until sold. Classification requires knowing the purpose and treatment.

Double counting can also arise when analysts add aggregates that already contain one another. GDP, sectoral value added, final expenditure and financial transactions should not be summed indiscriminately.

## 5. Value added and the production approach

**Gross value added (GVA)** for a producer or industry is broadly output less intermediate consumption under national-accounting definitions. Summing GVA across industries and making the applicable adjustments for taxes less subsidies on products yields GDP under the production approach.

This links national accounting to the production and value-added concepts already documented in Workstream A. A firm's revenue is not its value added: revenue can include the value of purchased inputs embodied in the goods sold. Nor is value added the same as accounting profit: it is distributed among labour compensation, taxes on production (net of relevant subsidies), operating surplus or mixed income, subject to accounting definitions.

GDP is “gross” because consumption of fixed capital is not deducted. Net domestic product (NDP) deducts consumption of fixed capital from GDP under the relevant framework. Gross and net aggregates answer different questions.

## 6. The three approaches to GDP

National accounts present three conceptually connected approaches.

### Production approach
Measures value added generated by producers and adjusts it for relevant product taxes and subsidies to derive GDP.

### Expenditure approach
Measures final expenditure on domestically produced output. A familiar identity is:

GDP = household final consumption + government final consumption + gross capital formation + exports − imports.

In shorthand, this is often written (Y = C + G + I + (X-M)), but the symbols must be interpreted according to the national-accounting definitions. “Investment” in this identity refers to capital formation, not every purchase of shares, bonds or other existing financial claims. Imports are subtracted to remove imported production already included in expenditure components; it does not mean imports are inherently harmful.

### Income approach
Measures income generated by production, using the national-accounting components and adjustments specified by the framework. Depending on presentation, components include compensation of employees, gross operating surplus, gross mixed income and taxes less subsidies on production and imports.

These approaches should converge conceptually on the same GDP aggregate, but independently compiled source data may not match perfectly. Statistical discrepancies, balancing procedures, revisions and estimation methods may be involved. Do not treat the three approaches as three different economies or assume a published discrepancy automatically indicates wrongdoing.

## 7. GDP, gross national income and national income

GDP is based on production within the domestic economic territory. **Gross national income (GNI)** adjusts GDP for the balance of primary income receivable from and payable to the rest of the world. It therefore reflects income attributable to resident institutional units more directly than GDP alone, under national-accounting definitions.

**Net national income (NNI)** deducts consumption of fixed capital from GNI. “National income” can have more specific historical or statistical meanings depending on the framework and source. When using the term, name the exact aggregate and definition rather than assuming that all publications mean the same thing.

For example, production inside a country can contribute to its GDP even when some profits or other primary income are payable to foreign owners. Conversely, residents may receive primary income from assets or work connected to production abroad. GDP and GNI can therefore differ.

These aggregates do not equal household disposable income. National income is distributed across sectors and may be affected by taxes, transfers, retained earnings and other flows before a household's disposable income is determined.

## 8. Nominal GDP, real GDP and the GDP deflator

**Nominal GDP** values output at prices of the current period under the chosen accounting method. It can increase because quantities produced increased, prices increased, or both.

**Real GDP** is a volume measure designed to separate changes in quantities and composition of output from changes in prices. The exact construction—such as chain-linking, reference-year presentation and revisions—depends on the statistical system. Real GDP is not simply nominal GDP divided by any convenient price index.

The **GDP deflator** is a price measure derived from the relationship between nominal GDP and the corresponding volume measure, with details depending on the statistical methodology. It reflects prices of domestically produced final output covered by GDP, whereas a consumer-price index tracks a defined basket of goods and services acquired by households. The two indices differ in coverage, weights, purpose and behaviour.

If nominal GDP rises 12% and a consistent volume measure rises 5%, the difference cannot be described as exactly 7 percentage points of inflation without considering the index-number method and exact growth relationship. The figures are illustrative. Use official published growth rates and definitions rather than approximating across incompatible series.

## 8.1 GDP deflator versus consumer-price index

The **GDP deflator** relates the value of domestically produced output at current prices to a corresponding volume measure under the national-accounts method. A **consumer-price index (CPI)** tracks price changes for a defined basket of goods and services acquired by the covered household population, using its own scope, weights and compilation methods. The two measures answer different questions.

The GDP deflator's coverage follows domestic production included in GDP and can reflect changes in the composition of that output. CPI coverage follows the selected consumer basket and can include imported consumer goods while excluding many investment goods, exports and government-produced services. Neither index is a universal measure of every price relevant to every household or business.

Consequently, GDP-deflator inflation and CPI inflation can differ without either series necessarily being wrong. Before comparing them, check population and product coverage, weights, index construction, reference period, frequency, revision policy and whether the question concerns domestic production prices or household consumption prices. For a Nigeria-specific application, use the current National Bureau of Statistics metadata rather than infer the official method from these general definitions.

## 8.2 National disposable income and the boundary between production and income available to spend

**Gross national income (GNI)** adjusts GDP for the relevant net primary income flows between residents and the rest of the world. **National disposable income** further accounts for net current transfers with the rest of the world under national-accounting definitions. It therefore addresses income available to resident institutional sectors for final consumption or saving more directly than GDP alone.

These aggregates are still not identical to the disposable income of a typical household. National totals include multiple sectors and income recipients; taxes, social contributions, benefits, transfers, retained earnings and the distribution of income shape what households can actually spend. A rise in GDP does not imply that every household's disposable income rose by the same proportion—or rose at all.

## 9. GDP level, GDP growth and per-capita GDP

**GDP level** is the measured size of production over a period in the chosen unit and price basis. **GDP growth** is the percentage change between periods, normally using real/volume GDP when discussing growth in production. Nominal GDP growth can reflect both volume and price changes.

**GDP per capita** divides GDP by population. It is a broad average, not the income received by each person and not a description of the median household. It can rise while many households experience stagnant or falling real incomes if gains are unevenly distributed or if other costs change.

A country's total GDP can grow because its population grows, because output per person rises, or both. Per-capita growth is still not a full productivity measure; it can change with employment rates, hours worked, demographics, terms of trade and other factors.

Comparisons between countries require consistent periods, definitions, currency conversions or purchasing-power adjustments as appropriate. Exchange-rate conversion and purchasing-power-parity comparisons answer different questions.

## 10. GDP versus wealth, money and financial assets

GDP is a flow of production during a period. **Wealth** is a stock of assets less liabilities measured at a date under a defined boundary. A country can have high annual GDP and relatively low net wealth, or substantial natural and produced assets with lower annual production. Changes in asset prices can change measured wealth without changing current GDP.

Money and bank deposits are financial assets for holders and liabilities for issuers under the relevant balance sheets. A transfer of an existing deposit, a purchase of an existing share or a rise in a share price does not directly add that transaction's full value to GDP. Financial services produced during the period may contribute to GDP, and financial activity can finance production, but the accounting treatment must be specific.

GDP is not a measure of how much money is available for investment. Nor does a high stock-market valuation prove high current production, broad prosperity or a proportional increase in productive capacity.

## 11. Consumption, capital formation, government and trade

The expenditure identity groups final expenditure into broad categories. These categories have specific statistical meanings:

- **Household final consumption expenditure:** qualifying goods and services acquired for household consumption under the accounts.
- **Government final consumption expenditure:** government-provided goods and services treated as final consumption under national-accounting rules; it is not the same as all government spending.
- **Gross capital formation:** includes gross fixed capital formation, changes in inventories and relevant acquisitions less disposals of valuables, as defined by the framework. It is not synonymous with financial-market investment.
- **Exports:** goods and services supplied to non-residents under the relevant trade and national-accounting definitions.
- **Imports:** goods and services acquired from non-residents. They are subtracted in the GDP expenditure identity because expenditure components may include imported content.

Government transfers, such as many pension or benefit payments, are not themselves purchases of current output and are not added to GDP as government final consumption. The later spending by recipients may contribute to final expenditure when qualifying goods and services are purchased. Likewise, borrowing is a financing transaction, not itself production.

## 12. GDP and welfare: what it does and does not tell us

GDP is useful for measuring production, comparing output over time and analysing the structure of an economy. It is not a comprehensive welfare index.

GDP alone does not show:
- how income and wealth are distributed;
- whether gains reach poorer households;
- unpaid care and much household work;
- health, safety, leisure, social connection or subjective well-being;
- environmental depletion and many external costs in a complete way;
- whether growth is sustainable or resilient;
- whether output is accessible or affordable to all groups;
- whether public services achieve good outcomes.

Some defensive or remedial expenditures can add to measured production even if they respond to harmful events. Some environmental damage may not be fully reflected in GDP. Complementary measures—such as poverty, employment, real household income, health, education, wealth, inequality and environmental indicators—are needed for broader assessment.

This does not make GDP useless. It means the statistic should be used for the question it was designed to answer and combined with other evidence when the question concerns welfare or distribution.

## 13. Sector shares, sector growth and contributions to growth

Three statements that sound similar can mean different things:

- **Sector share of GDP:** the sector's measured contribution to total GDP at a specified valuation and period.
- **Sector growth rate:** the percentage change in that sector's real output between periods.
- **Contribution to overall GDP growth:** how much a sector's change contributes to aggregate growth under the applicable decomposition.

A large sector can grow slowly and still matter to aggregate output. A small sector can grow rapidly but contribute little to total growth. A sector's nominal share can change because of relative prices as well as volume. Under chain-linked volume measures, sector components may not be simply additive in every published presentation.

When making a sector comparison, state whether the measure is nominal or real, the period, the denominator, the aggregation method and whether the statistic is a share, growth rate or contribution.

## 14. GDP compilation, revisions and uncertainty

GDP is compiled from multiple data sources, potentially including business surveys, administrative records, government accounts, household surveys, trade statistics, price data and estimation models. Coverage, timeliness and reliability vary by component and sector.

Early estimates may be revised as better data arrive or methods change. Base-year updates, classification changes, rebasing, changes in coverage and statistical-framework updates can alter measured levels and sector shares. A revision does not automatically mean the earlier estimate was fabricated; it may reflect improved information or methodology. But users should track versions and breaks in series.

For any published statistic, record:
- source and release date;
- reference period;
- current-price or volume measure;
- units and seasonal adjustment, if relevant;
- framework, base/reference year or chain-linking method when supplied;
- provisional/final status and revision history;
- coverage, limitations and known breaks.

Workstream B will decide how to operationalize specific indicators. This conceptual note does not choose a Nigerian GDP series or provide a formal calculation protocol.

## 15. Worked conceptual example

Suppose a domestic farm sells ₦100 of produce to a mill, the mill sells flour worth ₦160 to a bakery, and the bakery sells bread worth ₦300 to households. Assume for illustration that these are the only relevant transactions and ignore taxes, imports, inventory changes, capital consumption and other complexities.

Adding gross sales at each stage gives ₦560, which double counts intermediate goods. The final bread sale is ₦300. Value added across stages is:
- farm: ₦100, assuming no intermediate inputs;
- mill: ₦160 − ₦100 = ₦60;
- bakery: ₦300 − ₦160 = ₦140.

Total value added is ₦300, matching the final sale in this simplified example. Real national accounts are more complex and include many categories, valuation adjustments, imports, taxes, government non-market output, inventory changes and other treatments. The example demonstrates the logic, not a national-accounting estimate.

## 16. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| GDP vs money supply | GDP is a production flow over a period; money supply measures defined monetary stocks. |
| GDP vs national wealth | GDP is a flow; wealth is a stock of assets less liabilities at a date. |
| GDP vs GNI | GDP measures domestic production; GNI adjusts for net primary income from abroad. |
| GDP vs household income | GDP is an aggregate production measure, not what each household receives. |
| Gross vs net domestic product | Net domestic product deducts consumption of fixed capital from GDP. |
| Final vs intermediate good | Classification depends on use; intermediate inputs are not added again to final output. |
| Revenue vs value added | Revenue may include purchased intermediate inputs; value added subtracts intermediate consumption. |
| Value added vs profit | Value added is distributed among labour, taxes and operating surplus/mixed income; it is not simply company profit. |
| Nominal vs real GDP | Nominal GDP includes current-price changes; real GDP is a volume measure under a defined method. |
| GDP deflator vs CPI | They cover different economic baskets and use different constructions and weights. |
| GDP growth vs inflation | Nominal growth reflects price and volume changes; real growth aims to measure volume change. |
| GDP per capita vs typical income | GDP per person is an average output measure, not median disposable income. |
| Investment in GDP vs financial investment | GDP capital formation is not every purchase of shares, bonds or existing assets. |
| Government expenditure vs government final consumption | Transfers, interest payments and other outlays are not all purchases of current output. |
| Sector share vs sector growth | Share is a portion of the total; growth is a rate of change; contribution measures impact on aggregate growth. |
| Statistical revision vs error or manipulation | Revisions can reflect new data and methods; evaluate the evidence rather than assume motive. |

## 17. Measurement and evidence checklist

When interpreting GDP or national-accounting claims, ask:

1. Which aggregate is being reported: GDP, GVA, GNI, NNI, disposable income, wealth or another measure?
2. Is it a flow over a period or a stock at a date?
3. Is the figure at current prices or a real/volume measure?
4. Which statistical framework, source, reference period and release vintage apply?
5. Are values expressed in local currency, another currency, per capita, or as a share of an aggregate?
6. Are final and intermediate uses distinguished to avoid double counting?
7. Does “investment” mean capital formation or financial asset acquisition?
8. Are imports, product taxes, subsidies and valuation adjustments treated consistently?
9. Is a reported growth rate nominal or real, and which series was used?
10. Is the claim about a sector's share, its own growth rate or its contribution to aggregate growth?
11. Are revisions, rebasing and coverage limitations disclosed?
12. If the claim concerns welfare, inequality, sustainability or household experience, which complementary indicators are needed?
13. Are comparisons between countries or periods methodologically consistent?
14. Does the evidence support a measured relationship, a plausible explanation or a causal conclusion?

## 19. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- GDP measures production within a defined domestic production boundary over a period.
- GDP can be expressed through conceptually related production, expenditure and income approaches.
- Intermediate inputs are handled to avoid double counting.
- GDP, GNI, household income, money stocks and national wealth are different aggregates.
- Nominal GDP can change because of prices and volumes; real GDP aims to measure volume change under a defined method.
- GDP is informative about production but incomplete as a measure of welfare, distribution or sustainability.
- Published estimates can be revised as data and methods change.

### Scope limits
- Formal treatments depend on the applicable SNA version and national statistical methods.
- This note does not determine the official Nigerian series, validate current figures, or specify a complete GDP growth calculation.
- The worked example is simplified and should not be read as an empirical estimate.
- No current GDP level, growth rate, sector share or national-income statistic is asserted.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which current national-accounting framework and rebasing methodology does Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics use for each relevant series?
- How are Nigerian GDP estimates compiled by production, expenditure and income approaches, and which components are directly measured versus estimated?
- What release calendar, revision policy, deflators and sector classifications apply?
- How should nominal and real GDP series be compared across rebasing or classification changes?
- Which complementary indicators best answer questions about household purchasing power, poverty, inequality, employment and national wealth?

## 20. Short summary

GDP is a measure of final production within a defined domestic boundary over a period, not a stock of national wealth or a measure of all money and assets. It can be approached through production/value added, expenditure and income. Intermediate inputs must not be double counted. Nominal GDP reflects prices and quantities, while real GDP aims to measure volume changes. GDP differs from GNI, household income, money supply and welfare. Its interpretation depends on statistical definitions, sources, revisions and complementary evidence.

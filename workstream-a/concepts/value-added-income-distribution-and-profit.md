# Concept 6 — Value Added, Income Distribution and Profit

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concepts:** [Production](production.md); [Output and Intermediate Inputs](output-and-intermediate-inputs.md); [Economic Value, Usefulness and Exchange Value](economic-value-usefulness-and-exchange-value.md); [Costs and Prices](costs-and-prices.md); [Revenue, Income and Receipts](revenue-income-and-receipts.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain what value added measures, how it differs from revenue and profit, and how the value generated through production is distributed among workers, government, lenders, owners and other claimants.

## 1. The central distinction

**Value added** measures the value a producer contributes through production after deducting the value of intermediate goods and services used up in producing its output, under a specified accounting framework and valuation basis. **Profit** is a business result calculated after subtracting expenses and other items included in a defined profit measure. They answer different questions.

Value added asks: *What value did this producer add beyond the intermediate inputs it used?* Profit asks: *What remains under a particular accounting calculation after the costs and other items included in that calculation?*

Revenue, value added, profit and cash receipts are related but distinct. Revenue is recognized under the relevant accounting rules; cash receipts describe money actually received; value added concerns production; profit is calculated under a specified income-statement or accounting definition.

## 2. The basic production identity

At a simplified level:

**Gross value added (GVA) = output − intermediate consumption**

This national-accounting identity must be applied using the relevant definitions and valuation conventions. Output and intermediate consumption must refer to compatible periods and valuation bases.

- **Output:** goods and services produced under the accounting framework; not always equal to sales or cash received in the period.
- **Intermediate consumption:** goods and services used up as inputs in production during the period; not simply all cash paid for purchases during that period.
- **Gross value added:** “gross” because consumption of fixed capital has not been deducted.
- **Net value added (NVA):** GVA less consumption of fixed capital under the relevant national-accounting definitions.

Consumption of fixed capital measures the decline in the current value of fixed assets used in production due to physical deterioration, normal obsolescence or normal accidental damage. It is related to, but not necessarily identical with, depreciation recorded in company accounts.

## 3. A simple production example

Suppose a bakery buys flour and other intermediate inputs valued at ₦100,000 and produces bread with output valued at ₦180,000 during a stated period.

- Output: ₦180,000.
- Intermediate consumption: ₦100,000.
- GVA: ₦80,000.

The ₦80,000 is not the bakery's profit. The bakery may still need to account for employee compensation, taxes on production less subsidies, consumption of fixed capital, financing costs and other relevant items. The precise bridge from GVA to a particular profit measure depends on the accounting framework and classification of items.

The figures are hypothetical and illustrate the distinction, not actual bakery economics.

## 4. Why value added is not revenue

A retailer may sell goods worth ₦500,000 in a period. That sales figure alone does not tell us the retailer's value added under national-accounting rules. The resale value includes the value of goods produced elsewhere. The retailer contributes services such as making goods available, displaying and sorting inventory, holding stock, and facilitating transactions.

In national accounts, trade output is generally measured using trade margins rather than treating the full resale price as newly produced output by the retailer. The exact treatment depends on national-accounting rules and the transaction.

This prevents the same product's full value from being counted as new output at every stage. Do not assume that the difference between a retailer's sales and purchase cost is a complete measure of value added in every setting: inventory timing, other intermediate inputs, trade-margin conventions and valuation rules matter.

## 5. How value added is distributed

Value added generated by production is associated with income accruing to different participants and with taxes/subsidies under the accounting framework. A simplified view of gross value added includes:

- **Compensation of employees:** wages, salaries and relevant employer social contributions.
- **Taxes less subsidies on production:** specified taxes on production less relevant subsidies.
- **Gross operating surplus:** the residual associated with production after relevant preceding items are accounted for.
- **Gross mixed income:** the corresponding residual for unincorporated household enterprises where the labour contribution of the owner or family cannot be cleanly separated from the return on capital.

These are national-accounting concepts, not a universal template for every company's income statement. Exact identities and item coverage depend on institutional sector, valuation basis and presentation. National-accounting operating surplus is not automatically identical to a company's reported operating profit.

This distribution view matters because value added is not simply an amount “kept by the owner.” It is connected to compensation, production taxes and subsidies, and operating surplus or mixed income under the accounting framework.

## 6. From operating surplus to business profit

A business's reported profit depends on its accounting framework, financial structure and the profit measure selected. Depending on the statement and measure, relevant items can include cost of sales, employee compensation, depreciation or impairment, taxes and subsidies, interest and other financing costs, gains or losses on asset sales, income taxes, and non-operating items.

Do not mechanically subtract every item in that list from GVA in a universal order. Some items are already reflected in output or intermediate consumption; others belong to later parts of the income account or business income statement. A valid reconciliation needs a consistent framework and a defined measure.

A business may have positive value added but low or negative net profit because employee costs, fixed-capital consumption, financing costs or other expenses are high. Profit may also be affected by items that do not represent value generated by current production alone, such as asset-sale gains or financial income.

## 7. Gross versus net

In the relevant framework, “gross” generally means consumption of fixed capital has not been deducted; “net” means it has. These labels must be interpreted within the specific accounting system.

For national accounts:
- GVA = output less intermediate consumption.
- NVA = GVA less consumption of fixed capital.

For a company, **gross profit**, **operating profit** and **net profit** are different financial-reporting measures; none is a synonym for GVA or NVA. Gross profit commonly refers to revenue less cost of sales under a company's accounting presentation, which is not the national-accounting GVA formula.

Company depreciation may use cost-based estimates and accounting policies, while national-accounting consumption of fixed capital aims to measure the decline in the current value of fixed assets used in production. The two can differ.

## 8. Value added across a supply chain

Consider a simplified chain: a farmer produces grain; a miller turns grain into flour; a bakery turns flour into bread; a retailer sells bread to households. Each stage uses outputs from previous stages as intermediate inputs, alongside labour, capital services and other resources.

Measuring value added at each stage in a consistent accounting system helps avoid counting the full value of the same intermediate product repeatedly as new production. Real calculations require consistent valuation and treatment of inventories, imports, taxes, trade/transport margins and production boundaries. This example does not imply that every stage can be traced using public data.

## 9. Value added and GDP

At the level of an economy, GDP can be measured through production, expenditure or income approaches that are reconciled under the national-accounts framework. Under the production approach, gross value added across industries is combined with relevant taxes less subsidies on products to obtain GDP at market prices, subject to the framework's valuation conventions.

GDP is not obtained by simply summing all company revenues: revenue may include intermediate goods already counted at earlier production stages. GDP measures domestic production during a period; it is not a balance sheet of national wealth, a direct measure of household welfare, or the amount of cash available to a government or country.

## 10. Distribution is not the same as fairness or welfare

Describing how value added is distributed is a measurement task. Judging whether that distribution is fair, efficient or desirable is a separate normative and analytical task.

Distribution analysis may examine shares accruing to labour and operating surplus, differences among sectors, taxes and subsidies, income concentration, disposable income, and changes over time. A distribution statistic does not by itself establish why the distribution occurred, whether market power was involved, or whether a policy caused the observed change. Such claims need additional evidence and an appropriate analytical design.

Value added does not fully measure all benefits and harms. Unpriced external effects and unpaid household activity may require separate treatment depending on the question. Do not add such estimates to GDP or value-added measures without a defensible framework that avoids overlap and double-counting.

## 11. Comparison table

| Concept | What it measures | What it does not automatically tell us |
|---|---|---|
| Sales/revenue | Sales or revenue recognized under a stated convention | Value added, profit, cash received or social benefit |
| Gross value added | Output less intermediate consumption under national-accounting rules | Net profit, owner income or full social welfare |
| Net value added | GVA less consumption of fixed capital | Company net profit or cash flow |
| Gross profit | Company measure, commonly revenue less cost of sales | National-accounting GVA |
| Operating surplus | National-accounting income component associated with production | Company operating profit without reconciliation |
| Operating profit | Business measure under stated accounting policy | National-accounting operating surplus by default |
| Net profit | Specified bottom-line business measure | Cash available or value added |
| Cash flow | Cash movements during a period | Profit or value added |
| GDP | Domestic production under national-accounting rules | National wealth, cash balance or complete welfare |

## 12. Worked comparison

Suppose a business reports these hypothetical figures for one period:
- Output: ₦1,000,000.
- Intermediate consumption: ₦400,000.
- Employee compensation: ₦250,000.
- Taxes on production less subsidies: ₦20,000.
- Consumption of fixed capital: ₦50,000.

Then:
- GVA = ₦1,000,000 − ₦400,000 = ₦600,000.
- NVA = ₦600,000 − ₦50,000 = ₦550,000.

In this simplified illustration, the amount remaining after employee compensation and production taxes less subsidies is ₦330,000 before the appropriate operating-surplus/mixed-income treatment and other national-accounting details. It should not casually be labelled the company's net profit. A full reconciliation needs the enterprise's institutional status, accounting scope and relevant items.

## 13. Common errors to avoid

1. Treating revenue as value added.
2. Treating value added as profit or money retained by owners.
3. Treating gross profit as equivalent to GVA.
4. Treating national-accounting operating surplus as identical to company operating profit.
5. Subtracting all purchases as intermediate consumption without considering inventory and fixed assets.
6. Treating all purchases in a period as inputs used up in production during that same period.
7. Treating gross and net measures as interchangeable.
8. Summing company revenues and calling the total GDP.
9. Assuming an income-distribution measure establishes fairness, market power or causation.
10. Adding external-effect estimates to national accounts without a consistent valuation method.

## 14. Practical checklist

Before interpreting a value-added or profit figure, establish:
1. **Framework:** national accounts, company financial statements, management accounting, or another model?
2. **Entity and boundary:** which producer, institutional unit, sector or economy?
3. **Period:** which production or reporting period?
4. **Output basis:** how is output valued and how are inventories treated?
5. **Intermediate consumption:** which goods and services used up in production are included?
6. **Fixed capital:** is the measure gross or net, and how is consumption of fixed capital treated?
7. **Distribution items:** are compensation, production taxes/subsidies, operating surplus or mixed income being measured?
8. **Profit definition:** gross, operating, pre-tax, net or adjusted profit?
9. **Comparability:** are units, prices, policies and coverage comparable?
10. **Evidence:** is the result observed, officially published, estimated or illustrative?

## 15. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Production/output:** GVA depends on the output and intermediate-consumption boundary.
- **Economic value:** Market price and measured GVA do not capture every dimension of usefulness or welfare.
- **Costs/prices:** Intermediate consumption is not identical to all cash purchases or all business costs.
- **Revenue/income/receipts:** Revenue recognition, cash collection and profit require distinct definitions; GVA is another distinct measure.
- **Capital/depreciation:** Gross/net distinctions depend on treatment of consumption of fixed capital.
- **Workstream B:** Operational value-added or GDP indicators require authoritative definitions, source series, valuation basis and reconciliation.
- **Workstream C:** Changes in sales, wages or profits can be signals, but cannot serve as direct proxies for GVA without a justified relationship.
- **Workstream D:** Invoices and transaction records may support parts of a value-chain investigation but do not automatically establish total value added or income distribution.
- **Workstream E:** Value-chain and margin analysis may inform an opportunity hypothesis, but these conceptual distinctions do not prove a market opportunity.

## 16. Evidence status and limitations

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Under national-accounting rules, GVA is output less intermediate consumption.
- NVA deducts consumption of fixed capital from GVA.
- Revenue, GVA, gross profit, operating surplus, operating profit, net profit and cash flow are different measures.
- GDP is not obtained by summing unadjusted company revenues.
- Measuring income distribution and judging fairness are distinct tasks.

### Illustrative only
- All numeric examples are hypothetical and not estimates of Nigerian firms or sectors.
- The simplified distribution bridge is not a complete financial statement or full national-accounts reconciliation.
- No conclusion about a real firm's profitability, productivity, market power or social contribution is drawn.

### Questions to revisit
- Which valuation conventions apply to particular Nigerian industries and institutional sectors?
- How should small-business records be mapped cautiously to national-accounting concepts when full data are unavailable?
- Which distribution indicators are useful, and what do they omit?
- How do depreciation differences affect cross-firm comparisons?
- How can value-chain analysis connect to evidence about ownership, bargaining power or welfare without overclaiming causation?

## 17. Short summary

Value added measures production's contribution after deducting intermediate consumption; profit is a business result under a specified accounting calculation. GVA and NVA differ by treatment of consumption of fixed capital. Under national-accounting rules, value added is associated with compensation, production taxes less subsidies, operating surplus and mixed income. It is not simply revenue, gross profit, net profit or cash retained by owners. Interpret every figure within its framework, boundary, period and valuation method.

# Concept 2 — Output and Intermediate Inputs

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 1 — Production](production.md)  
**Purpose:** Clarify what is produced, what is used in producing it, and why output, sales, purchases, expenses, cash receipts, and intermediate consumption must not be treated as synonyms.

## 1. Plain-language starting point

A production process uses resources and activities to produce goods or services. **Output** refers to the goods or services produced by that process, or to their measured value under a stated accounting convention. **Inputs** are the resources and items used to produce output.

The word *input* is broad. It can refer to labour, land, equipment, energy, materials, software, purchased services, information, or other resources. In national accounting, however, **intermediate consumption** has a narrower meaning: the value of goods and services used up or transformed in the production process during the accounting period. Fixed assets used over several periods are generally not recorded as intermediate consumption; their use is accounted for through consumption of fixed capital (depreciation in simplified business language).

The central question is therefore not simply, “What did the business spend money on?” It is: **What was produced during the period, and which goods and services were used up in producing it under the relevant definition?**

The System of National Accounts is the internationally agreed framework for national-accounting concepts and rules. Its terms are designed for consistent measurement, not to make every national-accounting category identical to everyday business language. See the [United Nations Statistics Division's System of National Accounts overview](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/econstats/national-accounts/Sna/).

## 2. Output is not automatically sales or cash received

In everyday conversation, a business may say “output” when it means sales, units produced, revenue, or completed work. These ideas often overlap, but they answer different questions.

- **Physical output:** How many units or how much quantity was produced, such as 500 loaves of bread or 100 bags of maize.
- **Service output:** What service was provided, such as 40 transport journeys or a completed repair service. Suitable units depend on the service.
- **Output value:** The monetary value assigned to the goods and services produced under the relevant valuation rules.
- **Sales:** Goods or services sold during a period. Sales can include goods produced earlier, and goods produced during a period may remain unsold.
- **Cash receipts:** Money actually received during a period. Receipts can occur before or after production or sale, depending on deposits, credit terms, prepayments, and arrears.
- **Revenue:** An accounting measure recognized under a specified accounting basis and policy. It often relates to sales or services supplied, but its timing and scope should not be assumed identical to output value in national accounts.

### Example: a bakery

Suppose a bakery produces 500 loaves during a day. It sells 420 loaves that day, keeps 70 for sale the next day, and gives 10 away as samples.

These figures allow us to distinguish production from sales. They do not by themselves tell us the correct accounting value of output, whether every loaf meets the bakery's normal sale standard, what happened to any unsold loaves later, or when customers paid.

If some customers buy on credit, the bakery's sales and cash receipts can also differ. A sale on credit does not necessarily mean cash has already been received. Conversely, a customer paying in advance does not necessarily mean the bakery has already produced the item.

**Lesson:** Count or describe what was produced first; then separately ask what was sold, what was recognized as revenue, and what cash moved.

## 3. Inputs are broader than intermediate consumption

A production process may rely on several categories of resources.

| Resource or item | Everyday production role | National-accounting distinction to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Seeds, flour, packaging, feed or raw materials | Material used to make the output | Usually intermediate inputs when used up in the production period |
| Electricity, fuel or purchased transport | Supports the production process | Often intermediate consumption, subject to the applicable rules |
| Purchased cleaning, accounting or repair services | Services used by the producer | May be intermediate consumption when used in production |
| Workers' time and effort | Labour input | Labour is not purchased intermediate consumption; compensation of employees is part of the distribution of value added |
| A machine used for several years | Provides productive capacity over time | A fixed asset; its purchase is generally capital formation, while its consumption over time is recorded as consumption of fixed capital |
| Land or a building used in operations | Provides a place or resource for production | Treatment depends on the asset and transaction; not simply intermediate consumption |
| Inventory purchased for resale by a retailer | Goods to be sold to customers | National accounts treat trade output using the trade margin rather than treating the full resale price as newly produced output by the retailer |
| Owner's time in an unincorporated business | Labour and management contribution | May be reflected in mixed income rather than a separately observed wage |

This table is an introductory map, not a complete classification manual. The detailed treatment depends on the transaction, asset type, producer, period, and applicable accounting standards.

## 4. Intermediate consumption: the narrower concept

In national accounts, intermediate consumption consists of the value of goods and services consumed as inputs in production during the accounting period. They may be transformed, incorporated into the output, or used up in the process. It excludes fixed assets whose consumption is recorded separately as consumption of fixed capital.

The [United Nations Statistics Division glossary](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/nationalaccount/glossresults.asp?gID=286) describes intermediate consumption in these terms. Eurostat's [glossary of intermediate consumption](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/SEPDF/cache/1619.pdf) also emphasizes that it is a national-accounts concept and excludes fixed assets.

This means intermediate consumption is not necessarily the same as:
- all cash paid out during the period;
- every line called an expense in a company's books;
- the total cost of buying machinery or other long-lived assets;
- wages and salaries;
- loan principal repayments;
- dividends or owner withdrawals;
- the value of all goods purchased, regardless of whether they were used, held in inventory, or acquired for resale.

The exact treatment of particular items must be checked against the relevant national-accounting or financial-accounting rules. The categories overlap in real-world business activity, but they are not identical systems of classification.

## 5. Worked example: a small bakery

Assume the following simplified figures for one production period. They are invented solely to explain the relationships.

| Item | Illustrative amount |
|---|---:|
| Value of bread produced during the period | ₦300,000 |
| Flour, yeast, packaging and other goods/services used up in production | ₦110,000 |
| Intermediate consumption under the simplified assumptions | ₦110,000 |
| Gross value added under the simplified calculation | ₦190,000 |

Under these assumptions:

**Gross value added = output value − intermediate consumption**

₦300,000 − ₦110,000 = ₦190,000.

This is not the bakery's profit. The gross value added is the amount available, in the production-accounting framework, to cover the relevant components of primary income and consumption of fixed capital, subject to the applicable valuation conventions. It is not simply the amount the owner keeps.

The bakery may also have:
- wages and employer contributions;
- rent;
- taxes on production;
- interest expenses;
- depreciation/consumption of fixed capital;
- insurance and other costs;
- financing and inventory timing differences.

Those items do not all belong to intermediate consumption in national accounts in the same way. The accounting category matters.

The example also assumes the output and input values are measured on compatible bases. In real national accounts, output and intermediate consumption have specified valuation rules; taxes, subsidies and other adjustments can affect the bridge from value added to GDP.

The OECD's [value-added explanation](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/value-added-by-activity.html) describes value added as output minus intermediate consumption. The UN's national accounts statistics similarly distinguish gross value added from net value added, which deducts consumption of fixed capital.

## 6. Why machinery is different from flour

Suppose the bakery buys a commercial oven that is expected to be used for five years.

The oven helps produce bread, but its entire purchase price is generally not treated as intermediate consumption in the period of purchase under national accounting. The oven is a produced fixed asset; its acquisition is normally treated as investment/capital formation, and the use of the oven over time is reflected through consumption of fixed capital.

By contrast, flour is used up or transformed into bread in the production process. It is normally an intermediate input.

The distinction is about the **economic use and accounting treatment of the item**, not simply whether money was spent. A major repair, a routine repair, a replacement component, a lease, and a new fixed asset may have different treatment depending on the facts and rules.

This distinction will matter later when Workstream A studies capital, investment, depreciation, and productive capacity. It also explains why “business spending” cannot be treated as one undifferentiated category.

## 7. Timing: production, inventory, sale and payment

Economic activity has a time dimension. A producer can create output before it is sold, sell output produced earlier, buy materials before using them, or receive payment after a sale.

Consider a maize farmer:
1. The farmer harvests maize in October.
2. Some maize is stored and sold in November.
3. The farmer may have bought seed and fertilizer months before harvest.
4. A buyer may pay immediately or later.
5. Some harvested maize may be retained for the farmer's own use or lost.

Production, purchases, sales, cash receipts and inventory changes therefore describe different events. An accounting system must specify the period and treatment of inventories to avoid treating every purchase, sale or cash movement as if it occurred at the same time as production.

This is one reason a business cashbook alone cannot automatically reproduce a national-accounts measure of output. Cash records remain useful evidence, but they answer a different question unless supplemented and classified appropriately.

## 8. The retailer example: resale value versus trade output

A retailer buys a product from a wholesaler for ₦8,000 and sells it to a customer for ₦10,000. The ₦2,000 difference is the gross trade margin before the retailer's operating costs, losses, and other relevant adjustments.

It would be misleading to say the retailer produced ₦10,000 of new goods. The product was already produced by an upstream producer. The retailer provides distribution and selling services; in national accounting, trade output is generally measured through the trade margin under the relevant rules, not simply the full resale value of the product as new output by the retailer.

The [Nigeria National Bureau of Statistics GDP methodology](https://www.nigerianstat.gov.ng/page/gdp-methodology) explicitly describes output for the trade sector as the trade margin—the difference between revenue from goods sold and the value of goods purchased for resale—and identifies operating goods and services as intermediate consumption.

This example also demonstrates why a price difference is not automatically profit. The retailer may still have costs for staff, rent, electricity, packaging, transport, payment fees, spoilage, theft, and financing.

## 9. Output, inputs and value added across a production chain

Imagine maize moving through a chain:

1. **Farmer:** Produces maize.
2. **Transporter:** Produces transport service.
3. **Mill:** Uses maize, electricity, packaging and other inputs to produce flour.
4. **Wholesaler/retailer:** Provides distribution and selling services.
5. **Bakery:** Uses flour and other inputs to produce bread.
6. **Customer:** Purchases the final good for consumption.

Each producer may purchase goods or services from another producer. If the full sales value of every intermediate transaction were simply added together as though each were final output, the same underlying value could be counted repeatedly.

Value-added accounting addresses this problem by measuring each producer's output less its intermediate consumption, then aggregating value added across producers, with the applicable national-accounting adjustments. This is a conceptual explanation; the exact production boundary and valuation rules are set by the national-accounts framework.

The chain is also a reminder that the real production system includes physical transformations, transport, storage, market services, energy and information. The value added at each stage is not automatically equal to profit, and the price paid by the final customer does not reveal how all income is distributed across the chain.

## 10. Key distinctions

| Term | Main question |
|---|---|
| Production | What productive activity takes place? |
| Input | What resources are used to support production? |
| Output | What goods or services are produced, or what value is assigned to them under a specified convention? |
| Sales | What goods or services were sold during the period? |
| Revenue | What amount is recognized as revenue under the applicable accounting basis and policy? |
| Cash receipts | What money was actually received during the period? |
| Intermediate consumption | What goods and services were used up or transformed in production during the period under national-accounting rules? |
| Fixed asset | What produced asset supports production over more than one period? |
| Consumption of fixed capital | What value of fixed capital is consumed through use, ageing or obsolescence during the period under national accounts? |
| Gross value added | Output less intermediate consumption |
| Net value added | Output less intermediate consumption and consumption of fixed capital |
| Profit | A specified business result after the costs and accounting items included in the chosen profit measure |
| Trade margin | The difference between the resale value of goods and the value of goods bought for resale, as defined for the relevant trade-accounting context |

## 11. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Production:** This note extends Concept 1 by clarifying the produced result and the inputs used to create it.
- **Costs, prices, revenue and income:** These concepts follow next because the monetary values of output and inputs depend on prices, valuation and timing.
- **Value added and profit:** The note introduces value added; a later note can examine its relationship to wages, operating surplus, mixed income, depreciation and profit measures in greater depth.
- **Capital and investment:** Fixed assets and consumption of fixed capital will connect to capital formation, productive capacity and depreciation.
- **Workstream B:** Official measurement rules, sector-specific methods, data series and formulas belong in measurement specifications. This note does not determine the final Lifeconomist indicator formulas.
- **Workstream C:** Observed sales, production, input costs and inventory changes can be evidence, but their timing and coverage must be understood before interpreting them as output.
- **Workstream D:** Physical traces such as raw-material deliveries, machinery, stock levels and transport activity may help investigate a production chain, but individual traces do not prove total output or value added.
- **Workstream E:** A service that tracks output, inventory or input-cost pressure might be worth investigating in a real business setting, but no willingness to pay or commercial viability is established by these conceptual examples.

## 12. Evidence status and limitations

### Supported by authoritative national-accounting references

- The System of National Accounts is an internationally agreed framework of concepts, definitions and accounting rules.
- Output less intermediate consumption is the basis of gross value added.
- Intermediate consumption covers goods and services used up or transformed in production and excludes fixed assets whose use is recorded through consumption of fixed capital.
- Nigeria's NBS methodology describes trade-sector output in terms of the trade margin and identifies examples of intermediate consumption in several sectors.

### Illustrative only

- All bakery and retailer figures are hypothetical.
- The maize-to-bread chain is a simplified conceptual illustration, not a measured Nigerian supply chain.
- The examples do not establish any particular business's profitability, productivity, or national-accounting value added.

### Questions to revisit

- How do different accounting valuation bases affect output and intermediate consumption?
- How are changes in work-in-progress and finished-goods inventories treated in different sectors?
- How do national accounts distinguish routine maintenance from improvements or acquisition of fixed assets?
- How do informal businesses record production, own-use output, inventory and cash-based transactions in practice?
- How should this conceptual framework be adapted for specific sectors without confusing business accounting with national accounts?

## 13. Short summary

Output describes what a production process produces; inputs describe what it uses. Intermediate consumption is a narrower national-accounting category for goods and services used up or transformed in production during a period, not a synonym for every business expense. Production, output, sales, revenue, cash receipts and purchases may occur at different times. Fixed assets are distinguished from intermediate inputs, and gross value added is output minus intermediate consumption. These distinctions help explain production without double-counting the same value across a chain, but they do not by themselves determine profit or welfare.

# Concept 9 — Money, Wealth, Capital and Financial Resources

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational concept note; definitions depend on context  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concept:** [Capital Formation, Depreciation, Maintenance and Replacement](capital-formation-depreciation-maintenance-and-replacement.md)  
**Purpose:** Establish precise distinctions among money, currency, deposits, assets, liabilities, wealth, income, saving, productive capital, financial capital, funding and liquidity. Explain how these ideas interact without collapsing them into one measure.

## 1. Why these terms must be separated

In everyday speech, people may use “money,” “wealth,” “capital,” “funds” and “resources” as if they mean the same thing. They do not.

A household may own land and a house, receive monthly income, keep some cash, hold a bank deposit, owe a loan, and have limited money immediately available for an emergency. A retailer may own refrigerators and shelves, hold inventory, owe suppliers, have money in its bank account, and still lack enough cash to pay a bill due today. A bank may have deposits as liabilities, loans as assets, and regulatory requirements affecting how much liquidity and capital it must hold.

These are different positions:
- **Money** supports payment and settlement under the relevant monetary system.
- **Wealth** concerns the value of assets net of liabilities under a stated boundary and valuation.
- **Income** is a flow over a period.
- **Capital** has several meanings, including productive assets, financial resources and loss-absorbing funds.
- **Funding** describes where financing comes from or how an activity is financed.
- **Liquidity** concerns the ability to make payments or convert assets to means of payment in time, at acceptable cost and without excessive loss.

The correct meaning depends on whether the discussion concerns household finances, a business, a bank, a financial market, economic theory or national accounts.

## 2. Money: a functional concept

Money is commonly described by the functions it performs:
1. **Medium of exchange:** used to buy and sell goods and services.
2. **Unit of account:** provides a common unit in which prices, debts and accounts are expressed.
3. **Store of value:** allows purchasing power to be carried through time, although inflation, risk and other changes can erode its value.

No single physical form is required for money to perform these functions. In a modern monetary economy, money-like instruments include notes and coins and certain bank deposits. The exact monetary aggregate counted as “money” depends on the statistical definition being used.

Money should not be defined only as physical cash. Nor should every valuable asset be called money. A house, company share or machine may store value, but is not generally a means of payment accepted directly for everyday transactions.

The functions can vary in strength. High inflation may weaken money's store-of-value function; some assets may be used as a unit of account in a narrow setting without being broadly accepted for payment. Definitions should be tied to the actual institutional and statistical context.

## 3. Currency, cash, deposits and payment instruments

These terms overlap but are not identical.

- **Currency:** generally notes and coins issued by the monetary authority or other authorized issuer, depending on the jurisdiction and definition.
- **Cash:** in ordinary use, physical notes and coins. In some business contexts, “cash” may also include cash equivalents, but accounting definitions set specific criteria.
- **Bank deposit:** a claim the depositor has on a bank. For the customer it is an asset; for the bank it is a liability.
- **Electronic money or stored value:** a digitally recorded monetary value issued under a particular legal and institutional arrangement. It is not automatically identical to a bank deposit.
- **Payment instrument or payment channel:** a means or method for initiating or making a payment, such as a card, transfer instruction, cheque or mobile interface. The instrument is not necessarily itself money.
- **Reserves:** balances that eligible financial institutions hold at the central bank. They are central-bank liabilities and play a role in interbank settlement and monetary implementation; households do not ordinarily hold central-bank reserve accounts.

A bank transfer normally changes the deposit claims recorded for the payer and recipient and can require settlement between banks. It does not mean physical banknotes travelled from one person to another. The exact settlement mechanics depend on the payment system.

**Example:** If a customer pays a retailer ₦20,000 by bank transfer, the customer's deposit claim is reduced and the retailer's deposit claim is increased, subject to the payment and settlement process. The transfer is a payment, not necessarily new income for the economy: the retailer may be receiving payment for goods already sold, repayment of a loan, a gift or another transaction.

## 3.1 Monetary aggregates: why the boundary matters

A **monetary aggregate** is a statistical measure that groups selected monetary liabilities or instruments according to a published definition. It is not a synonym for all financial wealth, all liquid assets, or all central-bank liabilities.

Different aggregates can include different combinations of currency held outside banks, transferable deposits, other deposits and selected near-money instruments. The exact components, sector coverage, consolidation rules and treatment of foreign-currency instruments depend on the monetary authority's published methodology and the reference date. Names such as *narrow money* or *broad money* are useful labels, but the label alone is not enough to reproduce a series.

Three boundaries must be kept separate:

- **Currency held by the public:** physical notes and coins held outside the issuing monetary authority and, depending on the series, outside depository institutions.
- **Bank deposits included in a monetary aggregate:** deposit liabilities included under that aggregate's definition. Not every account, financial claim or digital payment balance automatically qualifies.
- **Central-bank reserves:** eligible institutions' balances at the central bank. Reserves support settlement and monetary implementation but are not generally counted as money held by households in the same way as their bank deposits.

A simplified relationship is:

**Monetary aggregate = selected eligible monetary instruments, counted under a specified statistical definition.**

In plain language, an aggregate is a constructed category, not a pile of every asset that looks like money. To compare two reported values, check which instruments and sectors are included, whether the series is consolidated, the date or period, and whether the definition has changed. A broader aggregate is not necessarily a better measure for every question.

A deposit is a claim on a bank; a reserve balance is a claim on the central bank held by an eligible institution. Banks use reserves and other settlement arrangements to complete interbank payments. The fact that reserves support the payment system does not mean that an additional unit of reserves mechanically creates an equal amount of lending or broad money in every circumstance. Lending decisions also depend on demand, creditworthiness, capital and liquidity constraints, profitability, regulation, risk appetite and the operating framework.

**Evidence rule:** For a country-specific monetary aggregate, use the central bank's own statistical release and metadata. Record the exact series name, component definition, units, frequency, seasonal adjustment (if any), valuation basis, institutional coverage and revision status. Do not infer the current Nigerian definition from this conceptual note; verify the current Central Bank of Nigeria methodology when building a Workstream B indicator.

## 4. Money is not wealth

**Wealth** is usually a stock of economic value owned or controlled by a person, household, institution or economy at a point in time, after specifying the boundary and valuation. **Net worth** is assets minus liabilities under the selected accounting framework.

Wealth can include:
- cash and deposits;
- shares, bonds and other financial assets;
- homes, land and other real property;
- productive equipment and other non-financial assets;
- ownership interests in businesses;
- pension entitlements or other claims where the chosen framework includes them.

Wealth can also include assets that are difficult to sell quickly. A house may be valuable but cannot normally be used to pay a shopkeeper directly. A machine may be productive but difficult to sell promptly at its recorded value.

A simple balance-sheet identity is:

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

This is meaningful only if assets and liabilities are defined consistently and valued on a compatible basis. Market values can change; some assets are difficult to value; some claims may be excluded from a particular balance-sheet boundary; and human capabilities or environmental assets may not be fully captured in conventional financial accounts.

Money is one possible component of wealth, not a synonym for wealth. A person can be wealthy but cash-poor, or hold substantial cash temporarily while having large debts.

## 5. Assets and liabilities

An **asset** is an economic resource or claim recognized within a specified framework. A **liability** is an obligation or claim against the entity that is expected to require a transfer of economic resources or is otherwise recognized under the applicable framework.

Examples:
- A bank deposit is an asset for the depositor and a liability for the bank.
- A loan receivable is an asset for the lender and a liability for the borrower.
- A company share is a financial asset for its owner and represents an ownership claim on the issuing company; its accounting classification depends on the framework.
- A refrigerator owned by a retailer may be a non-financial asset for the retailer.
- Inventory held for resale is generally an asset before it is sold or consumed.
- An unpaid supplier invoice may be a liability for the buyer and a receivable for the supplier.

A financial asset often corresponds to a liability or equity claim elsewhere in the system, although classifications and exceptions depend on the instrument and accounting boundary. A non-financial asset such as a machine is not simply a financial claim on another entity.

This matters when aggregating wealth. Adding the value of a bank deposit to the bank's corresponding deposit liability as though both were independent net wealth would double-count a claim and its counterpart. Consolidated sector and national balance sheets treat these relationships according to defined rules.

## 6. Income, saving and wealth are different dimensions

- **Income** is a flow measured over a period. Depending on the context, it may refer to wages, business income, interest, rent, transfers or national-accounting income.
- **Consumption** is expenditure or use of goods and services for current needs, defined according to the relevant framework.
- **Saving** is the part of income not used for current consumption under a specified definition.
- **Wealth/net worth** is a stock measured at a point in time.
- **Capital gain or loss** is a change in the value of an asset, realized or unrealized depending on the definition. It is not automatically current income under every accounting or national-accounting framework.
- **Cash flow** records cash inflows and outflows over a period; it can differ from recognized income or profit.

A person can have high income and little wealth if income is spent, debts are large or assets have not accumulated. Another person can have substantial wealth but low current income. Someone can receive a large loan and see cash in their account increase, but the loan also creates a liability; the borrowing is not automatically income or an increase in net worth.

Saving can add to wealth if it leads to asset accumulation and is not offset by losses, debt changes or consumption of assets. Wealth can rise without current saving when asset prices rise, and it can fall despite saving when prices decline or liabilities increase.

## 7. Capital: several meanings that must be named

The word **capital** is especially context-sensitive.

### 7.1 Productive or real capital
Produced assets used in production over time, such as equipment, buildings, infrastructure and qualifying software. This connects to the previous concept on capital formation.

### 7.2 Financial capital
Funds or financial resources available to finance activity. In everyday business discussion, “raising capital” often means raising money through equity, debt or other financing arrangements. It does not mean that cash raised has already become productive equipment.

### 7.3 Accounting or regulatory capital
For a bank or other regulated institution, capital may refer to equity or a defined set of eligible instruments that absorbs losses and is measured under specific rules. Regulatory capital is not simply the cash sitting in a vault or bank account. It is a framework-defined measure with eligibility, deductions and risk-weighting rules.

### 7.4 Human capital
Skills, knowledge, experience, health and capabilities that affect a person's productive potential. It is an economic concept, not a claim that people are owned assets.

### 7.5 Social and natural capital
Terms used in broader frameworks for relationships, institutions, ecosystems or natural resources. Because frameworks differ, a writer should state the definition rather than assume one universal accounting measure.

When a source says “capital increased,” ask: which capital, for whom, measured how, over what period, and under which framework?

## 8. Funding, financing, investment and capital allocation

These terms describe related but distinct things.

- **Funding:** the source or pool of resources used to support an activity; everyday and institutional usage varies.
- **Financing:** the arrangement through which spending is funded, such as retained earnings, borrowing, equity issuance, grants or supplier credit.
- **Investment:** may mean acquisition of financial assets, expenditure on productive assets, or a broader commitment of resources. The context must be stated.
- **Capital allocation:** the decision process for directing resources among projects, assets, firms, sectors or uses.
- **Commitment:** an announced or contractually agreed intention or obligation to provide funds, depending on the evidence and legal status.
- **Disbursement:** transfer of funds to a recipient under an arrangement.
- **Expenditure:** funds spent or costs incurred, depending on the reporting context.
- **Deployment:** use of funds or assets for a stated purpose; this term can be vague unless the action is specified.

A funding announcement does not establish that money has been transferred, spent, or converted into a productive asset. Each stage needs separate evidence. The sequence may be commitment → approval → disbursement → expenditure → delivery → installation → operation, but real arrangements do not always follow this exact order.

**Example:** A business receives a ₦5 million loan. Its cash or deposit balance may rise by ₦5 million and its liabilities rise by the same principal amount at origination, before fees and other effects. The business has obtained financing, but has not necessarily increased its net worth or productive capital. If it later buys a qualifying machine, that is a separate asset transaction.

## 9. Liquidity and solvency

**Liquidity** concerns the ability to meet payments when due, or to convert assets into means of payment quickly and at acceptable cost without excessive loss. It can refer to:
- an entity's short-term payment capacity;
- how readily an asset can be sold;
- how easily a market supports transactions without large price changes;
- liquidity in banking or funding systems.

These meanings are related but not interchangeable.

**Solvency** concerns the ability to meet obligations over a longer horizon or whether assets and resources are adequate relative to liabilities, under the definition being used. Accounting solvency ratios, legal insolvency tests and economic assessments may differ.

A business can be solvent on a balance-sheet valuation but illiquid because money is not available when a bill falls due. It can also have cash today while being insolvent because liabilities greatly exceed realizable assets or future resources. Liquidity and solvency must therefore be assessed separately.

A valuable asset does not guarantee liquidity. A marketable asset may become less liquid in stress; selling quickly can require a discount. A bank may hold assets that are valuable over time but cannot be converted into settlement money quickly enough to meet immediate outflows without borrowing, selling assets or receiving support.

## 10. Household, SME, bank and national-economy examples

### Household
A household owns a home worth ₦30 million, has deposits of ₦1 million, and owes a mortgage of ₦12 million. Its simplified net worth is ₦19 million if the home and deposit are the only assets, the mortgage is the only liability, and the stated values are consistent. Its immediately available deposit balance is ₦1 million, not ₦19 million. This excludes transaction costs, other assets and liabilities, and any uncertainty in the home's valuation.

### Small retailer
A retailer has ₦150,000 in its bank account, inventory worth ₦500,000 at the chosen accounting valuation, equipment worth ₦300,000, and supplier debt of ₦250,000. The business has more assets than cash alone, but not all assets can be used immediately to pay a supplier. Inventory must be sold, possibly at a discount or after a delay; equipment may be difficult to sell quickly without disrupting operations. Liquidity analysis asks when obligations fall due and how reliably assets can be converted to payment.

### Bank
A bank's customer deposits are liabilities to customers. Loans to borrowers are assets for the bank, subject to credit risk and accounting valuation. The bank also has capital measured under accounting and/or regulatory rules and must manage liquidity. It would be wrong to describe customer deposits as the bank's own net wealth or to equate regulatory capital with cash reserves.

### National economy
A country's national wealth is not the same as its money supply, GDP, government budget, foreign-exchange reserves or stock-market capitalization. These are different measures with different boundaries. National balance sheets may cover produced assets, natural resources and financial assets/liabilities according to a specified framework. Many financial claims cancel when sectors are consolidated, while non-financial assets and external positions require separate treatment. A single “total money and wealth” figure cannot be obtained by simply adding all these measures.

All numbers above are hypothetical and demonstrate concepts rather than describe any actual household, business, bank or country.

## 11. Commonly confused pairs

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Money vs wealth | Money is a means of payment/unit of account/store of value; wealth is a broader stock of assets net of liabilities. |
| Wealth vs income | Wealth is a stock at a point in time; income is a flow over a period. |
| Income vs cash receipts | Income recognition and cash collection can occur at different times. |
| Saving vs wealth | Saving is a flow; wealth is a stock affected by saving, valuation changes, debt and other changes. |
| Money vs bank deposits | Physical currency is one form; deposits are claims on banks and are included in some monetary aggregates. |
| Productive capital vs financial capital | Productive capital refers to assets used in production; financial capital refers to funds or financial claims, depending on context. |
| Capital vs capital formation | Capital may be a stock or a defined resource category; capital formation is a flow of qualifying additions. |
| Funding vs financing | Funding names the source or pool; financing describes the arrangement. Usage varies, so define the intended meaning. |
| Loan proceeds vs income | Borrowing creates a liability and is not ordinarily income. |
| Share purchase vs productive investment | A secondary-market purchase transfers an existing claim; it does not by itself create new productive assets. |
| Liquidity vs solvency | Payment capacity at the required time differs from longer-term ability to meet obligations or assets relative to liabilities. |
| Asset value vs spendable cash | An asset may be valuable but difficult or costly to convert into payment immediately. |
| Bank deposits vs bank capital | Deposits are liabilities to customers; capital is a distinct accounting/regulatory loss-absorbing measure. |
| Gross wealth vs net worth | Gross assets omit liabilities; net worth deducts liabilities under the stated boundary. |
| Market value vs book value | Market value reflects a market-based valuation; book value follows accounting recognition and measurement rules. |

## 12. Measurement and interpretation checklist

Whenever a report refers to money, wealth, capital or resources, ask:

1. What exact concept is being measured, and in what institutional context?
2. Is it a stock at a date or a flow over a period?
3. Whose balance sheet is being described: household, company, bank, government, financial sector or whole economy?
4. Are the amounts assets, liabilities, net worth, income, cash flow, funds, or transactions?
5. Which financial instruments and non-financial assets are included or excluded?
6. Are claims and their counterpart liabilities counted once, netted, or consolidated?
7. What valuation basis is used: face value, market value, book value, replacement cost or another basis?
8. Does “money” refer to currency, deposits, a specific monetary aggregate, reserves, or broader liquidity?
9. Does “capital” mean productive assets, financial funding, accounting equity or regulatory capital?
10. Is a funding amount announced, committed, approved, disbursed, spent, or actually deployed?
11. Is the issue liquidity, solvency, profitability, net worth, or productive capacity?
12. Are definitions and time periods consistent enough for comparison?

## 14. Evidence status, limits and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Money, wealth, income and capital are not interchangeable.
- Deposits are assets for depositors and liabilities for banks.
- Assets and liabilities must be analyzed from a defined balance-sheet perspective.
- Wealth/net worth is a stock; income and saving are flows.
- Loans and equity financing can fund activity but are not themselves proof of productive-asset creation.
- Liquidity and solvency answer different questions.
- National wealth, money supply, GDP, government debt and foreign-exchange reserves are different measures.

### Simplifications in this note
- Examples use simplified balance sheets and omit taxes, fees, valuation uncertainty and many possible assets/liabilities.
- Everyday and formal meanings of terms such as “capital,” “funding,” “cash” and “investment” vary. Formal analysis must state the chosen definition.
- Not every economic resource is recognized as an asset in every accounting framework; national and institutional boundaries matter.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which monetary aggregates does the Central Bank of Nigeria publish, and how are their components defined?
- How do Nigeria's national balance-sheet or wealth statistics define assets, liabilities and sector boundaries, where such statistics are available?
- How should the project compare household, business, bank, government and national measures without double-counting claims?
- Which measures best represent liquidity available for a specific obligation, as distinct from wealth or solvency?
- How should “committed,” “disbursed,” “spent,” “deployed” and “operational” be operationalized in the project's evidence register?

## 15. Short summary

Money is not wealth; wealth is not income; capital is not always productive equipment; and financing is not the same as investment or capital formation. Assets and liabilities must be viewed from a defined entity's balance sheet, while financial claims can have counterpart liabilities elsewhere. Liquidity concerns timely payment capacity, whereas solvency concerns broader ability to meet obligations. Always identify the definition, boundary, time dimension and valuation basis before interpreting or combining a financial number.

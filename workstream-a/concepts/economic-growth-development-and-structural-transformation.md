## 1. The starting question: what exactly is growing, and what is improving?

A country can report faster GDP growth while many households see little improvement in living standards. A company can expand sales by raising prices without producing more units. A sector can grow quickly while its share of the economy falls because other sectors grow faster. A country can increase output by using more workers and machinery without improving output per unit of input. A development programme can improve health or school attendance even when its effect on measured GDP is small or delayed.

These are not contradictions once the objects being measured are separated.

When a claim says that an economy is growing or developing, ask:

1. **What is changing?** Total output, output per person, productivity, capital stock, employment, income, health, education, poverty, economic complexity or another outcome?
2. **In what units?** Current-price money, inflation-adjusted volume, physical quantities, ratios, rates or non-monetary indicators?
3. **For whom?** The aggregate economy, a sector, firms, workers, households, regions or a particular group?
4. **Over what period?** One quarter, a year, a decade or a longer structural transition?
5. **What is the baseline?** Growth from a low starting point, recovery after a shock, trend growth or a change relative to comparable economies?
6. **What drives the change?** More inputs, better use of inputs, technology, reallocation, terms of trade, prices, demographics, policy or external conditions?
7. **How are gains distributed?** Across wages, profits, rents, taxes, regions and household groups?
8. **What evidence distinguishes a plausible mechanism from a demonstrated cause?**

The central rule is that **economic growth, productivity growth, structural transformation and human development are related but different outcomes**.

## 2. Economic growth: a change in measured output

Economic growth usually refers to an increase in the volume of economic output over time, often measured by the growth rate of real GDP. “Real” means adjusted using the relevant national-accounting methods to separate changes in quantities and composition from changes in prices. The exact index and reference period matter.

A simplified growth-rate expression is:

`Growth\ rate = ((Real\ GDP_t / Real\ GDP_{t-1}) - 1) \times 100\%`

This formula is a compact description, not a complete national-accounting method. The official real GDP series may be chain-linked, rebased or revised, and the comparison must use a consistent series and frequency.

### Total growth versus per-person growth

Total real GDP can rise because there are more people producing and consuming, because output per person increases, or both. Real GDP per capita divides real GDP by population and gives a rough aggregate measure of output per person. It is not the same as household disposable income, median income or a direct measure of welfare.

A country can have positive total growth but little or negative growth per person if population grows at a similar or faster rate. Conversely, GDP per capita can rise even while the total economy contracts if population falls faster, though that would not by itself demonstrate improved welfare.

For moderate growth rates, the following is a useful approximation:

**Growth in real GDP per person ≈ growth in real GDP − population growth.**

In plain language, if the total amount produced grows by about the same percentage as the population, average output per person changes little. The approximation is not an exact identity for finite growth rates; precise calculations use the ratios or logarithmic growth rates consistently.

### Nominal growth versus real growth

Nominal GDP can rise because prices rise, quantities rise or the composition of output changes. Real GDP aims to measure changes in volume using national-accounting price methods. A large increase in money-denominated GDP during high inflation must not automatically be interpreted as a comparable increase in the volume of goods and services produced.

GDP is also a production measure within a defined accounting boundary, not a complete measure of wealth, unpaid activity, distribution, environmental condition or quality of life. Concept 13 addresses those accounting boundaries in detail; this note focuses on how growth connects to development.

## 3. Economic development: broader changes in capabilities and living conditions

Economic development is a broader and more contested concept than output growth. It often concerns durable improvements in people's capabilities and living conditions, including health, education, nutrition, safety, access to essential services, economic opportunity, institutional effectiveness and the ability to withstand shocks.

Development is not a single universally sufficient statistic. Different frameworks select different dimensions and weights. A composite index can help organize evidence, but its ranking depends on definitions, normalization, weights, coverage and data quality.

Possible dimensions include:

- **Material living standards:** real household consumption or income, housing quality, access to essential goods and ability to meet basic needs.
- **Health:** life expectancy, preventable mortality, nutrition, disease burden and access to effective care.
- **Education and skills:** learning outcomes, literacy, school completion, relevant skills and access to learning throughout life.
- **Economic security:** stable livelihoods, social protection, manageable vulnerability and the capacity to recover from shocks.
- **Opportunity and inclusion:** whether groups can access productive work, finance, markets, infrastructure and public services.
- **Agency and institutional capability:** ability to participate in decisions, obtain legal remedies, use public services and rely on rules being implemented.
- **Environmental sustainability:** whether improvements depend on resource depletion or impose costs that undermine future welfare.

These dimensions can reinforce one another, but progress may be uneven. Better health can increase the ability to learn and work; higher incomes can make healthcare more affordable; stronger public institutions can improve service delivery. None of these relationships means every increase in GDP automatically improves every dimension.

## 4. Growth and development are connected, not interchangeable

Growth can support development by expanding the resources available to households, firms and governments. But the pathway depends on how income is generated, who receives it, what is taxed, how public funds are used, whether essential goods are accessible, and whether growth creates durable capabilities.

**Higher real output → potential for higher incomes and public resources → possible improvements in capabilities and services.**

In plain language, producing more can create room for wages, profits, tax receipts and investment to rise. Those resources can help fund better services and living conditions. But the benefits depend on distribution, public and private decisions, prices, service quality, institutional capacity and environmental costs. Growth creates possibilities; it does not guarantee that they will be realized.

The reverse direction also matters. Health, education, infrastructure, reliable energy, security and effective institutions can improve the conditions under which people and firms produce. These relationships are two-way and may involve long lags, complementarities and feedback.

## 5. Sources of growth: inputs, productivity and reallocation

Output can rise through several broad channels.

### More inputs

An economy may employ more labour hours, use more land or natural resources, or operate a larger stock of machinery, buildings and infrastructure. Increasing inputs can raise output even when the efficiency of each input does not change.

### Higher productivity

Productivity concerns output relative to one or more inputs. Labour productivity, for example, measures output per unit of labour input under a defined method. Multifactor or total-factor productivity estimates relate output to a combination of inputs and depend on assumptions, data quality and the production framework.

Concept 7 treats productivity and productive capacity in detail. This concept connects those measures to aggregate growth and development without repeating the full productivity framework.

### Reallocation across firms and sectors

Aggregate productivity can change when resources move from lower-productivity activities to higher-productivity ones, even if productivity within each firm or sector changes little. The reverse can also occur: resources may move toward activities with lower measured productivity, or high-productivity activities may remain too small to change the aggregate result.

Reallocation is not automatically beneficial. Workers may face displacement, skills may not transfer, market power may increase, environmental costs may rise, and measured productivity may fail to capture quality or informal activity. Distribution and transition costs must be examined.

### Terms of trade, prices and external demand

A country that exports commodities may experience higher export prices and stronger nominal income when global prices rise. The volume of domestic production may not increase proportionately. Gains in purchasing power depend on import prices, production costs, exchange rates, ownership of the resource rents, fiscal arrangements and the use of the additional income.

### Recovery and capacity utilization

Output can rise when previously idle factories, workers or infrastructure return to use. This recovery can be substantial without requiring a comparable increase in the economy's long-run productive capacity. Distinguish a cyclical rebound from sustained growth in potential output.

## 6. Growth accounting: contributions are not always causes

Growth accounting decomposes observed output growth into contributions associated with input growth and a residual often interpreted as productivity growth under the chosen framework. A simplified production relationship is:

`Y = A \cdot F(K, L, H, R, ...)`

where (Y) is output, (K) represents produced capital, (L) labour input, (H) may represent human capital or skills, (R) may represent land or natural resources, and (A) summarizes productivity or other factors not separately represented. The exact model depends on the economy and data.

A growth-accounting exercise may estimate how much of growth is associated with more labour, more capital and a residual. It is an accounting decomposition under assumptions; it does not automatically establish the causal effect of a policy or identify a single underlying mechanism. Measurement error, changing input quality, capacity utilization, informal activity and model misspecification can all affect the residual.

**Observed output growth = measured input contributions + estimated residual**, under the chosen model and assumptions.

In plain language, researchers can break the change in output into parts associated with measured inputs and a remaining component. That is useful for organizing explanations, but the remaining component is not a directly observed substance called “technology.” It can include technical progress, organization, resource allocation, omitted inputs and measurement error. Causal claims require additional evidence.

## 7. Capital deepening, skills, technology and organizational capability

**Capital deepening** means increasing the amount of capital available per worker or per unit of labour input, under a defined measure. It can raise labour productivity when workers have better equipment, infrastructure and tools. But capital quantity alone is insufficient: equipment may be unsuitable, poorly maintained, underused or constrained by electricity, skills, logistics, demand or management.

**Human capital** is a broad term for productive capabilities associated with knowledge, skills, health and experience. It should not be treated as a claim that people are merely financial assets. Education spending or years of schooling do not automatically equal learning or job-relevant capability; outcomes and context matter.

**Technology** includes tools, methods, processes, knowledge and technical systems. Adoption is different from invention. An economy may benefit from importing or adapting existing technologies without inventing them locally, but adoption requires skills, complementary infrastructure, financing, maintenance and organizational change.

**Organizational capability** includes the routines and systems through which firms and institutions coordinate people, information, equipment, quality, finance and delivery. Two firms with similar machinery may achieve different outcomes because of differences in reliability, process design, training, management, supplier relationships and learning.

These factors are complementary. New machinery may have little effect without electricity and trained operators; training may have limited returns when firms cannot access equipment or customers; reliable infrastructure may not raise output if finance or market access remains binding.

## 8. Structural transformation: changes in what the economy does

Structural transformation concerns changes in the composition and organization of economic activity. It may include shifts in employment and output across agriculture, industry and services; changes in the types of products produced; movement from low- to higher-productivity activities; changes in firm size and formality; or the development of new domestic capabilities.

It overlaps with Concept 23, which explains economic systems, institutions and structural change. The focus here is the relationship between structural transformation, growth and development.

Important distinctions:

- **Sectoral shift:** the shares of output or employment across sectors change.
- **Diversification:** dependence on a narrow set of products, sectors or revenue sources declines.
- **Upgrading:** firms or workers move toward more capable, higher-quality or higher-value activities within or across sectors.
- **Industrialization:** industrial activities and capabilities expand; the term's exact scope depends on the definition.
- **Formalization:** activities or relationships become registered or covered by specified formal rules; formal status does not guarantee productivity or worker protection in practice.
- **Urbanization:** the share or number of people living in urban areas rises; it is not automatically equivalent to industrialization.
- **Economic complexity:** an approach to describing the capabilities reflected in the diversity and sophistication of products an economy can make; results depend on data and method.
- **Resilience:** the ability to absorb shocks, adapt and recover; diversification can help, but correlations and common dependencies still matter.

A falling agricultural share of GDP does not necessarily mean agricultural output is falling. Its share may decline because other sectors grow faster. Similarly, a service-heavy economy is not automatically more or less developed: services range from high-productivity professional activities to low-productivity, insecure work, and national classifications may obscure this variety.

## 8.1 Employment absorption, job quality and productivity

Output growth and employment growth can move at different rates. **Employment absorption** asks how much additional employment an economy creates as output expands; the answer depends on sector mix, technology, hours worked, labor-force participation, firm entry and exit, and the skills demanded by expanding activities. **Jobless growth** is a descriptive term for output growth accompanied by weak employment growth; it does not by itself explain why the pattern occurred.

Labour productivity is output relative to a defined measure of labour input, such as workers or hours. Output per worker can rise because each worker produces more, because average hours change, or because the composition of workers and sectors changes. Output per hour and output per employed person therefore answer different questions. Higher measured productivity can support higher wages and living standards, but the gains are not automatically shared and may coexist with displacement or weak job creation.

A useful employment assessment combines real output and value added with employment, hours worked, wages, job security, informality, underemployment and labor-force participation. Disaggregate by sector, region, gender and relevant worker groups where reliable data permit. A single jobs-created total can conceal low hours, poor job quality, displacement elsewhere or changes in measurement coverage.

**Output growth → changes in sector demand and production methods → labor demand, hours and skills requirements → employment and wage outcomes.**

In plain language, a growing sector may use more workers, automate tasks, raise output per worker or rely on a different skill mix. Whether growth improves livelihoods depends not only on how much is produced, but also on how many people can access the new work, the quality of that work and how the resulting income is distributed.

## 9. Why sector shares, employment shares and productivity need separate analysis

Suppose a fictional economy has three sectors. Manufacturing output grows, but services grow faster; manufacturing's share of GDP can fall despite increasing manufacturing output. If workers leave agriculture for services, average measured productivity may rise or fall depending on the productivity of the destination jobs and changes within each sector.

To investigate transformation, track at least:

- Real output levels and growth by sector.
- Sector shares of total value added, using consistent prices and aggregation.
- Employment and hours by sector, occupation and employment status.
- Labour productivity by sector, with clear output and labour-input definitions.
- Firm entry, exit, size distribution and survival where data allow.
- Export products, destinations and domestic value added, rather than gross export totals alone.
- Capital formation, infrastructure, energy reliability, skills and access to finance.
- Wages, profits, informality, job quality and distributional outcomes.
- Regional differences and the movement of people, firms and resources.
- Environmental pressures, resource depletion and resilience to shocks.

Do not infer a structural change from one indicator alone. A shift in nominal sector shares may reflect relative price changes; a change in employment may reflect survey redesign; and a rise in exports may reflect imported inputs or commodity prices rather than an equivalent increase in domestic capability.

## 10. Convergence, divergence and catch-up

**Convergence** refers to a tendency for lower-income economies or regions to catch up with higher-income comparators under specified conditions and measures. **Divergence** means gaps widen. Neither is a universal law: results depend on the sample, period, income measure, population, institutions, technology access, human capital, investment, geography, global conditions and other factors.

Different forms of convergence should be distinguished:

- **Absolute (unconditional) convergence:** poorer units tend to grow faster regardless of structural differences; this is a strong empirical proposition and often does not hold across all economies.
- **Conditional convergence:** units approach different long-run levels depending on their structural conditions and parameters.
- **Income-level convergence:** gaps in income per person narrow under a stated measure.
- **Productivity convergence:** productivity gaps narrow.
- **Within-country convergence:** poorer regions catch up with richer regions; this may differ from convergence between countries.
- **Distributional convergence:** gaps between household or worker groups narrow; aggregate convergence does not establish this.

A catch-up story needs evidence about the comparison group, starting point, population and real income measure. High percentage growth from a small base does not necessarily mean the absolute income gap is closing. A country can grow quickly for several years without converging over the full period.

## 11. Growth quality, distribution and well-being

Growth quality is a broad analytical phrase, not one universally standardized metric. It may refer to whether growth is sustained, productive, inclusive, employment-generating, resilient, environmentally compatible or associated with improved living conditions.

Distribution matters because the same increase in aggregate output can produce different household outcomes depending on wages, profits, rents, taxes, transfers, inflation, access to services and asset ownership. Average income can rise while median income stagnates, or poverty can fall while inequality increases. These outcomes can coexist because they measure different parts of the distribution.

Questions to ask include:

- Are real median earnings and household disposable income increasing?
- Which groups, regions and occupations receive the gains?
- Are more jobs being created, and are they productive, safe and sufficiently stable?
- Do prices of essentials rise faster than aggregate inflation?
- Are public services improving in access, quality and reliability?
- Is growth based on new productive capability or a temporary windfall?
- Are natural assets being depleted or environmental costs shifted to other groups or future periods?
- Are households and firms becoming more resilient or more exposed to shocks?

No single indicator answers all these questions. A dashboard should keep distinct dimensions visible instead of compressing them into an unexplained score.

## 12. Potential output, cyclical growth and sustainability

**Actual output** is output produced over a period. **Potential output** is an estimate of the level of output an economy can sustain without generating persistent inflationary or other imbalances under a particular analytical framework. It is not directly observed; estimates vary with model, data and assumptions.

An economy can grow rapidly while recovering from a downturn because idle capacity returns to use. Potential output may rise more slowly. Conversely, actual output can temporarily exceed an estimated sustainable path, though the size and interpretation of any output gap are uncertain.

**Long-run growth** concerns sustained changes in productive capacity and the ability to produce over time. It depends on capital accumulation, labour and demographics, skills, technology, institutions, infrastructure, resource conditions and productivity. It should not be inferred from a single quarter or a temporary price boom.

**Sustainable development** concerns whether current improvements can be maintained without undermining future productive, human or environmental conditions. It is not synonymous with any one “green growth” measure. Resource depletion, pollution, climate exposure and loss of ecosystem services can be economically material even when conventional GDP records current production.

## 13. Worked example: output rises, but did development improve?

Assume a fictional economy records 5% growth in real GDP, while population grows by 3%. The approximate growth in real GDP per person is 2%; the exact result is `(1.05 / 1.03) - 1 \approx 1.94\%`. These are illustrative figures, not a claim about a real country.

What can be concluded?

- Aggregate measured output rose.
- Real output per person rose modestly under this approximation.
- It is not yet known whether median household income rose, whether poverty fell, or whether gains were broadly shared.
- It is not yet known whether the growth came from more inputs, higher productivity, improved terms of trade, a recovery in utilization or another combination.
- It is not yet known whether health, learning, public-service access or environmental conditions improved.
- A causal claim about a policy would require a credible method for distinguishing its effects from other changes.

To evaluate development, add household distribution, labour-market, health, education, public-service and environmental evidence. To explain the growth mechanism, examine sector contributions, input use, productivity, capital formation, external conditions and the timing of changes. Do not make the evidence answer a question it was not designed to answer.

## 14. Measurement and evidence safeguards

Before interpreting a growth or development claim, establish:

1. The exact indicator, formula, unit, frequency and reference period.
2. Whether the series is nominal or real, total or per capita, seasonally adjusted or not, and how it is revised.
3. The national-accounting production boundary, price basis and treatment of informal or non-market activity.
4. Whether sector values are comparable in volume terms or are affected by relative price changes.
5. The population estimate and demographic assumptions used for per-capita calculations.
6. Whether the claim concerns a level, growth rate, contribution, share, index or forecast.
7. Whether growth accounting results are decompositions under assumptions rather than identified causal effects.
8. How labour input, capital services, skills and natural resources are measured, including quality and utilization.
9. Whether sectoral productivity comparisons use consistent output and input definitions.
10. Whether household income, consumption or welfare evidence is available alongside aggregate GDP.
11. Which groups, regions, firms and workers are represented or omitted.
12. Whether changes in survey design, base years, classification or methodology affect comparisons.
13. Whether gains reflect domestic production, changes in terms of trade, asset revaluation or transfers.
14. Whether development outcomes are measured directly or inferred from economic aggregates.
15. What time lags may separate investment, education or health improvements from output effects.
16. Which counterfactual or comparison group supports any causal claim.
17. Whether resource depletion, pollution and other external costs are included or omitted.
18. What uncertainties, revisions, missing data and alternative explanations remain.

Workstream B should formalize indicator-specific choices, sources, transformations and validation. This concept note is not itself a Nigerian GDP measurement protocol or an empirical country assessment.

## 15. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Economic growth vs economic development | Growth concerns output over time; development concerns broader capabilities and living conditions. |
| GDP growth vs GDP per capita growth | Total output growth includes population effects; per-capita growth adjusts for population. |
| Nominal vs real growth | Nominal values reflect price and quantity changes; real measures aim to isolate volume changes. |
| Growth vs level | A growth rate is a change over time; the level is the measured amount at a point or period. |
| Output growth vs productivity growth | Output can rise from more inputs; productivity concerns output relative to input. |
| Labour productivity vs worker effort | Productivity is a measured output/input relationship, not a moral judgement about workers. |
| Productivity vs productive capacity | Productivity is a ratio; capacity is the ability to produce under specified conditions. |
| Capital deepening vs total-factor productivity | More capital per unit of labour is distinct from the residual change not explained by measured input contributions. |
| Growth accounting vs causal explanation | Accounting decomposes growth under assumptions; causal inference asks what would have happened otherwise. |
| Sector share vs sector output | A sector's share can fall while its real output rises if the rest of the economy grows faster. |
| Structural transformation vs growth | Transformation changes economic composition and capabilities; growth changes aggregate output. |
| Diversification vs upgrading | Diversification broadens activities or exposures; upgrading increases capability or position within a value chain. |
| Industrialization vs urbanization | Industrial capability growth is not the same as a larger urban population. |
| Formalization vs productivity | Registration or formal status does not guarantee better output per input or worker welfare. |
| Human capital vs schooling years | School attendance is an input or proxy; learning, skills, health and their use matter for capability. |
| Innovation vs technology adoption | Creating a new technology differs from diffusing or adapting existing technologies. |
| Convergence vs high growth | Catch-up requires narrowing a defined gap, not merely a high growth rate. |
| Average income vs median income | The mean can rise while the typical household's measured income stagnates. |
| Growth vs inclusive growth | Aggregate expansion does not establish who benefits or how broadly gains are shared. |
| Actual vs potential output | Actual output is observed under the accounting system; potential output is model-estimated. |
| Recovery vs long-run growth | Reusing idle capacity differs from a sustained increase in productive capacity. |
| Resource rents vs productive capability | A resource windfall can raise income without proving diversified capabilities or durable development. |
| GDP vs wealth | GDP measures production over a period; wealth is a stock of assets net of liabilities under a defined boundary. |
| Correlation vs causal mechanism | Two variables moving together does not establish that one caused the other. |
| Composite development index vs complete welfare | An index reflects selected dimensions, weights and data; it cannot capture every relevant outcome. |

## 17. Evidence status, limitations and open questions

### Established conceptual points

- Growth in real output is not the same as growth in output per person, productivity or broader development.
- More inputs, improved productivity, reallocation, utilization changes and external price conditions can all contribute to observed growth.
- Growth-accounting decompositions depend on data and assumptions and do not alone establish causal effects.
- Structural transformation involves composition and capability changes, not only a rise in aggregate output.
- Aggregate gains can be distributed unevenly, and development dimensions can move at different speeds.
- Potential output is estimated rather than directly observed.
- Long-run development depends on interactions among economic, institutional, human and environmental conditions.

### Scope limits

- This note does not estimate current growth, productivity, poverty, development or convergence for Nigeria or any other country.
- It does not prescribe a universal development index or claim that a single growth model explains all economies.
- Causal mechanisms are discussed conceptually; no policy effect is claimed without empirical analysis.
- Indicator definitions, data sources, revisions and official calculation rules belong in Workstream B's measurement specifications.
- Cross-country comparisons require attention to purchasing-power concepts, price methods, population, coverage, institutional differences and data quality.

### Questions for follow-up

- Which combination of output, productivity, household and human-development indicators is appropriate for each Lifeconomist question?
- How should sectoral growth contributions be decomposed without confusing volume changes and relative prices?
- Which Nigerian data can support consistent estimates of productivity and structural transformation over time?
- How can growth from commodity price windfalls be distinguished from sustained growth in productive capabilities?
- What evidence is needed to identify whether a specific policy caused a development outcome?
- How should informal activity, unpaid work, environmental costs and regional disparities be represented?
- Which leading indicators can signal changing productive capacity without being mistaken for realized output?

## 18. Short summary

Economic growth describes change in measured output; development concerns broader improvements in capabilities and living conditions. Growth may result from more inputs, productivity improvements, reallocation, recovery or external price conditions. Structural transformation changes the composition and capabilities of an economy, while distribution determines how aggregate gains reach households and groups. Growth-accounting decompositions are useful but do not automatically establish causes, and potential output is estimated rather than directly observed. Lifeconomist should keep output, output per person, productivity, structure, distribution and human-development outcomes separate, then investigate the mechanisms connecting them with source-backed evidence.

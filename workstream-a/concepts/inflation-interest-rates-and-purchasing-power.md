# Concept 16 — Inflation, Interest Rates and Purchasing Power

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; official indicator definitions and Nigerian data validation remain in Workstream B  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 15 — Government Finance, Taxation and Public Institutions](government-finance-taxation-and-public-institutions.md)

## 1. Purpose

Prices, inflation, interest rates and purchasing power affect household budgets, business costs, government finance and financial markets. They are connected but not interchangeable. A price is the amount paid for a particular good or service; a price index summarizes a defined basket; inflation is a rate of change in an index; an interest rate is the price or return associated with credit; and purchasing power concerns what money or income can buy.

This note establishes concepts and measurement safeguards. It does not state current Nigerian inflation, interest-rate or cost-of-living figures. Workstream B remains responsible for selecting official series and validating definitions and calculations.

## 2. Price, price level and price index

A **price** is the amount paid or quoted for a unit of a good, service or asset at a specified place and time. It can vary by quality, location, seller, quantity, contract and payment terms.

A **price level** summarizes prices across a defined set of items. A **price index** combines price observations using specified coverage, weights, reference period and index formula. An index number of 120 on a base of 100 generally means the index is 20% above its reference level; it does not mean goods cost 120 currency units. Rebasing an index is a scaling operation, not by itself a change in underlying price movements.

Indexes serve different purposes: consumer prices, producer prices, construction costs, import/export prices and asset prices describe different domains. A consumer price index is not a universal index of every price in an economy.

## 3. Inflation rate versus price level

**Inflation** is a rise in a broad price index over a period, usually stated as a percentage change. The scope of the index and the period must be specified. A price increase for one item is not enough by itself to establish broad, persistent inflation.

If an index rises from 100 to 110, it has increased 10%. If it then rises from 110 to 115.5, it has increased 5% in the second period. Prices are still rising, but more slowly: inflation has fallen while the index remains above its earlier level. These figures are illustrative.

Common measures include:
- **Year-on-year:** compares an index with the same period a year earlier.
- **Month-on-month:** compares it with the preceding month.
- **Annual average:** compares average index levels across years under the published method.
- **Seasonally adjusted change:** attempts to remove recurring seasonal patterns when an appropriate official series exists.

These measures can differ because they compare different periods and respond differently to seasonal patterns and base effects. Always name the measure being used.

## 4. Inflation, disinflation and deflation

- **Inflation:** the measured broad price index rises.
- **Disinflation:** the inflation rate declines but remains positive; prices continue rising more slowly.
- **Deflation:** the measured broad price index falls.
- **Price stability:** does not mean every individual price is unchanged; relative prices may move while the aggregate remains broadly stable.
- **Stagflation:** commonly describes high inflation alongside weak activity and labour-market conditions; it is not a synonym for inflation alone.

A declining inflation rate does not normally reverse past price increases. Deflation can have different causes and effects depending on whether it reflects productivity gains, weak demand, debt burdens or other forces.

## 5. Consumer price indexes and cost of living

A **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** tracks price changes for a defined basket associated with household consumption. Its basket, weights, geographic coverage, population, collection methods and formula depend on the statistical agency.

CPI is an aggregate indicator, not a record of every household's spending. Households differ by income, location, family size, housing, transport, health needs and consumption patterns. If an essential item occupies a larger share of a household's spending than its weight in the index, that household's cost pressure may differ from headline CPI.

**Headline inflation** usually refers to the broad published index. **Core inflation** generally excludes or adjusts for specified components considered volatile or less useful for measuring underlying trends. There is no single universal core definition; always identify the method.

**Cost of living** concerns the resources needed to maintain a specified living standard. CPI is a useful proxy for price changes but is not identical to a full cost-of-living index. Substitution, quality changes, new products, informal transactions, housing treatment and household-specific needs complicate the comparison.

## 6. Weights, substitution and measurement limits

Price indexes weight categories according to estimated expenditure patterns for a defined population and period. Weight updates matter because household spending changes over time.

Households may substitute toward cheaper items when relative prices change. Some index methods account for substitution differently from others. Substitution can help households manage spending, but does not guarantee unchanged welfare if alternatives are less desirable, lower quality or less convenient.

Quality adjustment tries to separate a price change from a change in product characteristics. New and disappearing products, informal-market prices, unavailable items and hard-to-observe transactions create further measurement challenges. Cross-country comparisons require compatible coverage, weights, formulas, housing treatment, reference periods and revision practices.

## 7. Nominal income, real income and purchasing power

**Nominal income** is measured in current currency units. **Real income** adjusts nominal income using a relevant price index to approximate changes in purchasing power.

A simplified index relationship is:

`Real income index = (Nominal income index / Relevant price index) × 100`

The index bases must be consistent. If nominal income rises 8% while the relevant price index rises 12%, real income changes by approximately 1.08/1.12 − 1 = −3.6%. The common subtraction shortcut gives about −4%; the ratio is more precise. This is an illustrative calculation, not a claim about actual wages.

**Purchasing power** is the quantity of goods and services a unit of money or given income can buy. It depends on the relevant prices, location, time and basket. A broad CPI-adjusted measure is useful but may not reflect an individual household's experience.

A nominal salary increase does not guarantee higher real income. A flat nominal income during falling prices may buy more on average, but taxes, employment, debt, household needs and quality of available goods also affect welfare.

## 8. Nominal and real interest rates

A **nominal interest rate** is the stated rate before adjusting for inflation. Comparisons require compatible periods, compounding conventions, fees and contract terms.

A **real interest rate** adjusts the nominal rate for price changes. For an **ex-ante** question about an expected return or borrowing cost, use expected inflation over the same horizon. For an **ex-post** question about what happened to purchasing power, use realized inflation over a matching period.

A common approximation is:

`Real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate`

A more precise relationship is:

`1 + real rate = (1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation rate)`

For example, a nominal rate of 15% and inflation of 10% imply a real rate of about 4.5%, rather than exactly 5%, assuming compatible periods. A positive nominal rate can correspond to a negative real rate if inflation is higher. Negative real interest does not mean there is no nominal interest payment.

Actual borrower and saver outcomes also depend on fees, taxes, default risk, liquidity, contract terms and the inflation experienced by the relevant household.

## 9. Expected and realized inflation

**Expected inflation** is a forecast or belief about future price changes. It can come from surveys, market prices or models, each with limitations. Market-based measures may include risk and liquidity premia as well as expectations.

**Realized inflation** is calculated from observed price-index data after the period. It is not known with certainty in advance.

Wage negotiations, loans, savings, bond yields and investment decisions may depend on expected inflation; retrospective real returns use realized inflation. Unexpected inflation can reduce the real value of fixed nominal claims and obligations, all else equal. Actual effects depend on indexation, refinancing, income adjustment, taxes and broader economic responses. Forecasts are not facts: record their date, horizon, source and assumptions.

## 10. Policy rates and market rates

A **central-bank policy rate** is an instrument or target in a monetary-policy operating framework. **Market rates** include interbank rates, government-bond yields, corporate yields, business and consumer lending rates, and deposit rates. They do not necessarily move one-for-one with the policy rate.

Market rates reflect expected future policy, inflation expectations, credit/default risk, maturity, liquidity, collateral, bank funding costs, competition, regulation, capital constraints and borrower characteristics. Lending rates may incorporate funding costs, operating costs, credit risk and required return. Deposit rates reflect funding demand, competition and market structure.

A **yield curve** compares yields across maturities for a defined class of securities. Its shape can reflect expected future short rates, term premia, supply and demand, and liquidity. Its slope alone does not mechanically prove a particular future outcome.

Policy changes can influence borrowing, saving, exchange rates, asset prices and demand through several channels and with lags; they do not directly set every market rate.

## 11. Credit contracts and total borrowing cost

When comparing rates, identify whether the rate is fixed or variable, the period and compounding convention, fees, repayment schedule, currency, collateral, maturity and default risk. An advertised rate is not necessarily the total cost of borrowing. Annual percentage rate or similar all-in measures can include specified fees depending on the jurisdiction and product, so the applicable definition matters.

Fixed-rate nominal borrowers and variable-rate borrowers face different inflation and refinancing risks. Inflation may reduce the real value of fixed nominal debt, but it may also increase input costs, weaken real income, raise refinancing rates or create repayment stress. Interest expense is not principal repayment; a lender's interest income is not the same as all cash received on a loan.

## 12. Potential inflation mechanisms

Inflation is an observed outcome; identifying its cause requires evidence. Potential mechanisms include:
- demand growing faster than productive capacity;
- higher production, energy or distribution costs;
- exchange-rate depreciation increasing local-currency import and input costs;
- supply disruptions, weather shocks or transport constraints;
- changes in taxes, subsidies or administered prices;
- monetary and credit conditions affecting nominal demand over time;
- expectations and wage/price-setting behaviour;
- changes in market structure or markups in particular sectors.

These mechanisms can interact. Exchange-rate pass-through, for example, depends on import dependence, inventories, competition, pricing practices and contract timing. A supply shock may affect prices directly and then influence expectations, wages, demand and policy responses.

Do not attribute an inflation episode to one factor merely because it changed at the same time. Correlation, timing and a plausible mechanism are clues, not sufficient causal proof. Different episodes can have different mixes of drivers, and a relative-price change is not automatically persistent broad inflation.

## 13. Household, labour and distributional effects

Inflation affects households differently depending on consumption baskets, income sources, location, ability to substitute, access to credit and whether wages, pensions or benefits adjust. Nominal wage growth, real wage growth, employment, hours, job security, taxes, transfers and debt service should be considered together.

A national average cannot establish what happened to every worker or household. Essential goods may account for different shares of spending across income groups, but the size and direction of distributional effects should be measured rather than assumed.

Inflation can also affect public budgets through nominal tax bases, thresholds, wages, benefits, procurement costs and debt contracts. Unexpected inflation can reduce the real value of some fixed nominal debt while increasing refinancing costs, foreign-currency debt burdens in local currency or expenditure pressure. Maturity, currency, indexation and market response matter.

## 14. Financial-market transmission

Expected inflation can influence bond yields, equity valuations, exchange rates, commodity prices and risk premia. For a bond with fixed nominal cash flows, a rise in required yield generally lowers its market price, all else equal; sensitivity depends on duration, convexity, cash flows, credit risk and liquidity.

Equities are not a guaranteed inflation hedge. Firms may face higher input costs, higher discount rates, weaker demand or changes in pricing power. Outcomes differ by sector, leverage, cost structure and competition.

Inflation, policy rates, government borrowing and market liquidity interact, but no single indicator establishes the full causal story.

## 15. Useful calculation relationships

For a monthly index, year-on-year inflation for month `t` is:

`(Index_t / Index_(t−12) − 1) × 100%`

Real income growth, using compatible nominal growth and inflation rates, can be calculated as:

`((1 + nominal income growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1) × 100%`

The exact real interest rate relationship is:

`real rate = (1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation) − 1`

For ex-ante real rates use expected inflation over the relevant horizon; for ex-post rates use realized inflation over a matching period. Retain source definitions, units, index base, periods and rounding rules. Never mix monthly inflation with an annual interest rate without a clear conversion method.

## 16. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Price vs price level | One quoted price differs from an aggregate measure. |
| Price level vs inflation rate | The level is the index value; inflation is its rate of change. |
| Inflation vs disinflation | Disinflation is a declining positive rate while prices still rise. |
| Disinflation vs deflation | Deflation is a fall in the measured broad price index. |
| Relative-price change vs broad inflation | One category's price movement does not establish broad persistent inflation. |
| CPI vs cost of living | CPI measures a defined basket; cost of living is a broader concept. |
| Headline vs core inflation | Core depends on a specified exclusion or adjustment method. |
| Nominal vs real income | Real income adjusts for prices to approximate purchasing power. |
| Nominal vs real interest rate | Real rates adjust nominal rates for expected or realized inflation. |
| Ex-ante vs ex-post real rate | Ex-ante uses expected inflation; ex-post uses realized inflation. |
| Expected vs realized inflation | Forecasts differ from observed index changes. |
| Policy rate vs market rate | The policy instrument does not mechanically set every market rate. |
| Lending rate vs total borrowing cost | Fees, compounding and repayment terms can change effective cost. |
| Inflation vs interest rate | Inflation measures price-index change; interest is the price/return associated with credit. |
| Index rebasing vs inflation | Rebasing alone does not change underlying price movements. |
| Nominal wage vs real wage | Pay growth may not keep pace with relevant prices. |
| Slower inflation vs falling prices | A lower positive rate does not reverse previous price increases. |
| Average inflation vs household experience | Baskets, needs and locations differ. |
| Correlation vs inflation cause | Simultaneous movement does not establish causation. |
| Bond yield vs bond price | For fixed cash flows, higher required yields generally lower prices, all else equal. |

## 17. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before using an inflation or interest-rate claim, ask:
1. Which index, rate or financial instrument is being discussed?
2. What are the source, population, geography, frequency and period?
3. Is inflation year-on-year, month-on-month, annual average or another measure?
4. Is the index level being confused with the rate of change?
5. Are index weights, base, seasonal adjustment and methodology documented?
6. Does the basket represent the household or population in question?
7. Is the claim about headline, core or sector-specific prices?
8. For real income, is the deflator appropriate and are periods aligned?
9. For a real rate, is inflation expected or realized, and does its horizon match?
10. Is the rate a policy rate, market rate, lending rate, deposit rate or bond yield?
11. Are compounding, fees, taxes, currency and repayment terms comparable?
12. What evidence supports a proposed inflation driver, and what alternatives exist?
13. Could base effects, seasonality, revisions or administered-price changes explain the movement?
14. Are distributional claims supported by household or wage data rather than averages alone?
15. Are nominal and real measures labelled with units and calculation methods?

## 19. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Inflation is the rate of change in a defined price index, not the index level.
- Disinflation is a declining positive rate; deflation is a falling measured index.
- Nominal income and rates differ from real measures adjusted for prices.
- Ex-ante and ex-post real rates use expected and realized inflation respectively.
- CPI is an aggregate index and cannot describe every household's cost of living.
- Policy rates influence but do not mechanically determine every market rate.
- Causal attribution for inflation requires evidence beyond a coincident price movement.

### Scope limits
- No current Nigerian inflation, CPI, policy-rate, lending-rate or wage figures are asserted.
- Index methods and rate definitions depend on the publishing institution and instrument.
- Illustrative calculations do not establish actual household experience or causal explanations.
- Official series selection, calculation and validation remain in Workstream B.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- How are Nigeria's CPI basket, weights, coverage and inflation measures defined by the official statistical agency?
- Which official monthly, year-on-year and annual-average inflation series are available?
- Which policy and market-rate series fit different research questions?
- What household or expenditure data can compare personal cost pressure with aggregate CPI?
- How do exchange rates, food and energy prices, transport, administered prices and other mechanisms contribute to specific inflation episodes?
- How should real wage, lending-rate and deposit-rate indicators be specified without mismatched horizons or unsuitable deflators?

## 20. Short summary

Inflation is the rate at which a defined price index changes, not the price level itself. Disinflation means price increases are slowing; deflation means the index is falling. Nominal income and interest rates do not directly show purchasing power or real return: the relevant inflation measure and matching period matter. CPI summarizes a defined basket, while households experience different price changes according to their needs and spending. Policy rates influence but do not mechanically set market rates, and identifying inflation's causes requires evidence about mechanisms and alternatives.

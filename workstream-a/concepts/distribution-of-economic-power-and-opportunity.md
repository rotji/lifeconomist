## 1. The central question

Economic outcomes depend not only on how much a society produces, but also on who can access resources, who owns assets, who makes decisions, who sets terms, who can refuse an offer, who captures returns, and who absorbs losses.

The central question is: **How is the practical ability to access, control, influence and benefit from economic activity distributed, and through what mechanisms does that distribution arise or change?**

This is broader than measuring inequality. Income and wealth statistics describe outcomes, but do not by themselves explain bargaining positions, institutional rules, geographic barriers, dependencies or historical processes that produced them.

Key dimensions:
- **Income:** a flow received over a defined period.
- **Wealth:** accumulated assets less liabilities at a point in time.
- **Ownership:** legal or beneficial rights over an asset or enterprise.
- **Control:** practical ability to direct decisions or use an asset.
- **Influence:** ability to shape another actor's choices or system rules.
- **Opportunity:** realistic options available to a person, firm or community.
- **Access:** ability to reach, enter, use or obtain a resource, market or institution.
- **Returns:** income, profit, fees, rents, appreciation or other benefits.
- **Risk and responsibility:** who is exposed to loss, must perform obligations, or can transfer burdens.

These dimensions overlap but are not interchangeable. A person can have high income but little wealth, own an asset without controlling its use, have formal market access but lack working capital, or bear substantial risk while receiving little of the upside.

## 2. Distribution is not one statistic

A distribution describes how a quantity or capability is spread across people, households, firms, sectors, regions or generations. Examples include income by household, wealth by person, ownership by beneficial owner, market share by supplier, credit access by firm, infrastructure access by region, and exposure to price shocks by consumption basket or debt structure.

A national average can improve while some regions or groups deteriorate. A sector can grow while a small number of firms capture most new value. Many market participants may exist while essential infrastructure or distribution channels remain controlled by a few gatekeepers.

A distribution analysis should specify:
1. What is distributed: income, wealth, access, decision rights, risk, market share or something else.
2. Across whom or what: the unit and relevant groups.
3. When and where: reference period and geographic or market boundary.
4. How measured: definition, source, coverage and uncertainty.
5. Whether the task is description or explanation of a proposed mechanism.

## 3. Income distribution versus wealth distribution

Income is generally a flow over a period; wealth is a stock measured at a point in time. Income may build wealth through saving and asset acquisition, but consumption, taxes, transfers, borrowing, debt repayment, asset-price changes, inheritance, business ownership and losses also matter.

**Income over time → consumption, taxes, transfers, saving and debt service → changes in assets and liabilities → future wealth and income capacity.**

In plain language, two households with equal current income can have different financial positions. One may own a productive business and have little debt; another may pay high rent, support relatives or have no buffer. Their ability to withstand a shock and build future wealth can therefore differ.

Distinguish market income from income after taxes and transfers; gross from disposable income; income level from reliability; wealth from liquidity; average from median; individual from household income; and annual flows from lifetime resources. Wealth is not necessarily spendable: land or a business may have high estimated value but no clear title, reliable buyer, cash flow or access to credit.

## 4. Ownership, control, influence, benefit and risk

- **Ownership** concerns rights or claims. Legal title, beneficial ownership and economic exposure may differ.
- **Control** concerns the ability to direct use, management or key decisions.
- **Influence** concerns shaping choices or rules without necessarily exercising direct control.
- **Benefit** concerns who receives economic gains or services.
- **Risk-bearing** concerns who absorbs adverse outcomes.
- **Liability** concerns who is obligated to perform, pay or compensate.

A business may be owned by one person, financed by another, operated by a manager, dependent on a dominant buyer, and partly protected by a public guarantee.

**Owner ≠ controller ≠ funder ≠ operator ≠ beneficiary ≠ risk bearer.**

In plain language, a name on a registration document does not answer who makes decisions, supplies money, can stop operations, receives profits, is protected against losses or is liable. Do not infer one role from another without evidence. A role map should record the role, supporting evidence, relevant dates and unresolved uncertainty.

## 5. Bargaining power

**Bargaining power** is the relative ability to influence terms of an exchange. It depends on alternatives, urgency, information, switching costs, coordination, enforceability and the consequences of walking away—not just size or wealth.

A supplier may have weak bargaining power if it has one major buyer, perishable output and urgent cash needs. Alternative buyers, storage, working capital, differentiated products or collective purchasing can improve its options.

**Fewer alternatives + urgent need + high switching costs → weaker ability to refuse terms → greater influence over price, timing or risk allocation.**

In plain language, a party may have less negotiating room when it urgently needs a deal and has nowhere else to go. This is a hypothesis to test, not a universal rule: reputation, regulation, contracts, quality and the other party's dependence can change the result.

Investigate realistic alternatives, concentration, time pressure, information asymmetry, search and compliance costs, contract enforcement, access to credit and dependence on a platform, licence, route or input. A low price does not automatically prove exploitation, and a high margin does not automatically prove market power; costs, risks, quality and alternatives matter.

## 6. Market, institutional and network power

**Market power** is the ability to influence prices, output, quality, terms or competitive conditions without losing enough business to make that influence ineffective. It may arise from concentration, barriers to entry, control of essential inputs, network effects, scale, lock-in or limited substitutes.

**Institutional power** is the ability to shape or apply rules through legislation, regulation, licensing, enforcement, procurement, standards or public services.

**Network position** concerns an actor's position in relationships and flows. A distributor, payment provider, logistics operator, broker or platform may influence access without owning the goods or final product.

Potential evidence includes market shares, concentration, switching rates, entry and exit, contract terms, access conditions, enforcement records, dependencies and substitutes. No single measure proves power in every context. Concentration may reflect scale economies or a small market; low concentration does not guarantee effective competition if participants depend on a shared gatekeeper.

## 7. Access, opportunity, inclusion and exclusion

**Access** means being able to reach or use a resource or service. **Opportunity** concerns the realistic possibility of achieving an outcome through that access, given actual constraints.

A business may qualify for a loan but be unable to provide collateral, afford fees, meet documentation requirements or wait for approval. A farmer may have market access in principle but face transport costs, spoilage, uncertain weights or delayed payment that make participation unviable.

Assess whether the resource exists; whether a person is eligible; whether it can be reached; whether documentation and costs are manageable; whether it is available at the required time and quality; whether terms are enforceable; what risks accompany access; and who is excluded at each stage.

**Inclusion** means participation on specified terms. **Exclusion** means inability to participate or obtain access. **Segmentation** means participants face systematically different products, terms, prices or constraints. Inclusion is not automatically beneficial: participation may bring high fees, unstable earnings or risks. Exclusion is not always deliberate or unlawful. Identify mechanisms and effects rather than assuming motives.

Distinguish formal eligibility from uptake, uptake from sustained participation, participation from benefit, access from outcomes, unequal outcomes from proven discrimination, and voluntary non-participation from constrained exclusion.

## 8. Economic rents and value capture

An **economic rent** is a return above the minimum needed to keep a resource in its current use under a specified framework. It is not identical to accounting profit, a high price or a morally objectionable payment. Rents may arise from scarce land, innovation, exclusive rights, natural resources, market power or regulatory privilege.

**Rent extraction** refers to obtaining returns through an economic or institutional position in ways that may transfer value without a corresponding contribution or impose avoidable costs. The meaning depends on the framework and evidence.

Distinguish productive returns, scarcity rents, innovation returns, market-power rents, regulatory rents, intermediation fees and transfers that do not correspond to new output or a mutually valued service. These categories can overlap. A distributor may provide valuable logistics while also possess leverage over terms. A high margin may compensate for spoilage, working capital, transport, bad debt or volatility—or reflect weak competition. Cost, service, risk and market evidence is required.

## 9. Who pays, benefits and bears risk?

An arrangement distributes more than money: it allocates benefits, costs, obligations, uncertainty, downside exposure and decision rights. A lender may receive a contractual return while a guarantor absorbs some default loss. A platform may earn a commission while sellers bear inventory risk and customers bear switching costs. A subsidy may lower a buyer's price while its fiscal cost falls elsewhere.

Map direct and indirect beneficiaries, formal payers and ultimate economic burden where identifiable, contingent liabilities, operational and financial risks, who can transfer or insure risk, who has voice in decisions, and who receives short-term gains versus long-term consequences.

The party legally paying a tax or fee is not necessarily the party ultimately bearing its economic burden: prices, wages, quantities, profits and behaviour may adjust. Likewise, the immediate recipient may not be the final beneficiary. Incidence must be investigated rather than inferred from legal form.

## 10. Geography, mobility and intergenerational transfer

Opportunity depends partly on location. Transport, electricity, broadband, water, storage, finance, education, security, administrative capacity and proximity to customers influence participation costs. Compare actual access, reliability, travel time and affordability, not just infrastructure presence.

Spatial correlation does not establish cause. A region may attract infrastructure because income is high; infrastructure may support higher income; or both may reflect earlier advantages. Sector mix, population, prices, history and institutions may also matter.

**Unequal initial assets and networks → different access to education, credit and markets → different returns and shock absorption → persistent differences in future assets and opportunity.**

In plain language, people starting with different resources may face different choices and risks. Someone with collateral and support may finance training or survive a failed business; another may need immediate income and be unable to take the same risk. This is a mechanism to investigate, not proof that starting conditions determine outcomes.

Mobility may mean absolute improvement, relative rank change, movement between income or wealth categories, occupational mobility or business formation. Distinguish within-generation from intergenerational mobility, income from wealth mobility, and opportunity equality from outcome equality. Inherited assets, networks, education, location, health, obligations, institutions and chance can all matter.

## 11. Agency, collective action and dependence

**Agency** is the capacity to make choices and act on them. It depends on available options, information, resources, time, safety, rights and the consequences of refusal.

A small business may be unable to change a distributor's terms alone but may gain options through alternative sourcing, collective purchasing, a cooperative, better information or shared logistics. Collective action can improve bargaining power but also create coordination costs, governance problems, internal inequality or a new gatekeeper. Assess who can join, who sets rules, how costs and benefits are allocated, whether participants can leave or challenge decisions, and whether genuine alternatives are created.

Dependence matters when an actor cannot quickly replace a supplier, buyer, lender, platform, route or licence.

**Critical dependency + weak substitute + costly switching → exposure to the counterparty or bottleneck.**

In plain language, dependence can make an actor vulnerable to interruptions or unfavourable terms. Severity depends on inventory, contracts, switching time, financial buffers, alternatives and the counterparty's own dependence. Concentration is a clue; realistic substitutability and switching time are more specific questions.

## 12. A layered investigation of unequal outcomes

When a group or region has worse outcomes, do not jump from disparity directly to cause. Investigate:
1. **Observed outcome:** What differs, for whom, where and when?
2. **Measurement:** Are definitions, units, coverage and composition comparable?
3. **Exposure:** Do groups face different prices, shocks, infrastructure or risks?
4. **Access:** Do rules, costs, distance, documentation or information limit participation?
5. **Decision rights:** Who sets terms, and what alternatives exist?
6. **Assets:** How do collateral, skills, cash buffers and networks differ?
7. **Institutions:** Which rules, practices or enforcement patterns matter?
8. **History:** Did earlier investment, policy, inheritance or market change shape current options?
9. **Alternatives:** Could sector mix, age, firm maturity, location or selection explain part of the pattern?
10. **Causal test:** What evidence would support or weaken the proposed mechanism?
11. **Distribution of effects:** Who benefits, pays, bears risks or may be excluded by a proposed change?
12. **Uncertainty and feedback:** What remains unknown, and could an intervention create new incentives or gatekeepers?

This separates the existence of a disparity from a claim about why it exists.

## 13. Evidence and measurement

| Question | Potential evidence | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| How is income distributed? | Household surveys, tax, wage and transfer data | Informal income, underreporting and sample coverage may distort estimates |
| How is wealth distributed? | Balance-sheet surveys, property and business records | Valuation, informal ownership, debt coverage and illiquid assets create uncertainty |
| Who owns or controls an entity? | Registries, filings, voting rights, contracts and verified ownership data | Nominees, indirect ownership, stale records and informal control can obscure reality |
| Who has bargaining power? | Contract terms, alternatives, switching costs, payment delays and interviews | Stated terms may differ from actual practice |
| Is a market concentrated? | Market shares, entry/exit, buyer and supplier data | Market definition matters; concentration alone does not prove abuse |
| Who has credit access? | Applications, approvals, pricing, collateral and rejections | Applicants are selected; differences may reflect constraints and risk profiles |
| Who can access infrastructure? | Location, reliability, travel time, prices and service quality | Presence does not guarantee affordability or usable capacity |
| Who benefits from a programme? | Eligibility, uptake, delivery, pass-through and outcomes | Announcements and budgets do not prove delivery or final incidence |
| Who bears a tax or price shock? | Price, wage, quantity, profit, expenditure and behaviour | Legal liability does not determine economic incidence |
| Is mobility changing? | Longitudinal income, wealth, education, occupation or firm data | Repeated cross-sections may not identify individual movement |
| Does a disparity have a causal explanation? | Longitudinal data, credible comparisons, institutional records and process tracing | Confounding, selection and measurement error can limit causal claims |

Evidence discipline:
- Separate formal rules from implementation and consequences.
- Distinguish direct evidence from inference about hidden ownership or influence.
- Do not infer intent from unequal outcomes alone.
- Do not treat registry entries as complete proof of effective control.
- Do not treat concentration as sufficient proof of harmful market power.
- Do not assume intended beneficiaries received a programme's benefits.
- Distinguish aggregate change from changes for particular groups.
- Document population, unit, reference period, source and uncertainty for quantitative comparisons.

## 14. Illustrative case: a small retailer and a distributor

Suppose a small shop buys most stock from one distributor. The distributor sets minimum order sizes, payment terms and delivery schedules. The shop has limited working capital and cannot easily buy directly from manufacturers.

Low retail margins alone do not establish exploitation or excessive power. Investigate comparable wholesale and retail prices, delivery and storage costs, spoilage, financing, payment delays, other distributors, minimum order requirements, product availability, substitutes and whether the distributor bears inventory or credit risk. Test whether collective purchasing lowers total costs after coordination and transport.

The distributor may provide valuable logistics while also having leverage over payment terms. Alternatively, the margin may mostly compensate for genuine costs and risks. Both are plausible before evidence is collected. This is a hypothetical example, not a claim about a particular retailer.

## 15. Practical applications for Lifeconomist

- **Market intelligence:** Map suppliers, buyers, distributors, licences, infrastructure and critical dependencies; distinguish ownership from control and benefit.
- **SME intelligence:** Investigate supplier concentration, payment terms, price volatility, market access, transport and external credit conditions without requiring private books for every question.
- **Programme assessment:** Trace policy and budget commitments through implementation to actual recipients and outcomes.
- **Geographic opportunity analysis:** Compare usable access to customers, inputs, transport, power, finance and services—not just population or infrastructure presence.
- **Ownership and capital-flow investigation:** Separate legal and beneficial ownership, operational control, funding, contractual claims, beneficiaries and risk bearers.
- **Opportunity design:** Identify a verified constraint and test whether an intervention expands realistic choices or adds another fee or gatekeeper.

## 16. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Income vs wealth | Flow over a period versus stock of assets less liabilities |
| Wealth vs liquidity | Net asset position versus ability to meet obligations when due |
| Ownership vs control | Rights or claims versus practical decision authority |
| Control vs influence | Directing decisions versus shaping choices or rules |
| Access vs opportunity | Ability to reach/use something versus realistic chance of achieving an outcome |
| Eligibility vs uptake | Meeting formal criteria versus participating |
| Participation vs benefit | Taking part versus receiving a positive net outcome |
| Inequality vs poverty | Relative distribution versus a defined deprivation or resource threshold |
| Absolute vs relative mobility | Improvement in real position versus change in rank relative to others |
| Bargaining vs market power | Influence over a negotiation versus ability to affect market-wide conditions |
| Concentration vs harmful dominance | Few participants versus demonstrated ability to restrict competition or exploit terms |
| Economic rent vs accounting profit | Return above a defined opportunity-cost benchmark versus profit under accounting rules |
| Rent vs rent extraction | Category of return versus obtaining advantage in a way that may impose avoidable costs |
| Intermediation vs extraction | A role that may provide a service versus a claim about how value is obtained |
| Legal payer vs economic bearer | Formal liability versus who ultimately experiences the burden |
| Recipient vs beneficiary | Immediate recipient versus ultimate beneficiary |
| Risk exposure vs realized loss | Possibility of loss versus a loss that occurs |
| Formal vs effective inclusion | Official eligibility or availability versus practical participation |
| Unequal outcome vs discrimination | A disparity versus a demonstrated mechanism meeting a specified definition |
| Budget allocation vs delivery | Budget provision versus services or funds actually reaching users |
| Bargaining advantage vs wrongdoing | Greater negotiating leverage versus conduct shown to violate a rule or defined standard |

## 18. Established principles, limits and open questions

### Established conceptual principles
- Production and aggregate growth do not reveal how gains, costs, control or risk are distributed.
- Income and wealth require different measures and time bases.
- Ownership, control, influence, funding, operation, benefit and risk-bearing can belong to different actors.
- Bargaining power depends on alternatives, urgency, switching costs, information and enforceability, not just size.
- Formal access does not guarantee practical access or beneficial outcomes.
- Concentration is a reason to investigate, not proof of harmful market power.
- Economic rents are not automatically evidence of wrongdoing; source and effects matter.
- The formal payer and ultimate economic bearer may differ.
- Disparities are observations to explain, not proof of a specific cause or motive.
- Geography, history and intergenerational transfers can influence feasible choices.
- Collective action can improve alternatives but can also create new gatekeepers.
- Distributional assessment should consider benefits, costs, risks, voice and alternatives.

### Limits
- This note does not estimate current Nigerian or global inequality.
- It does not assert that a named company, institution or programme exercises improper power.
- It does not infer beneficial ownership or hidden influence without case-specific evidence.
- It does not prescribe one preferred distribution or assume all inequality has the same cause.
- It does not replace legal analysis of whether conduct violates a law or regulation.

### Open questions
- Which dimensions of economic power should be mapped separately rather than compressed into one score?
- What minimum evidence is needed to identify a critical dependency or gatekeeper?
- How can practical access be measured where official data are sparse and informal activity substantial?
- How can concentration be distinguished from harmful conduct in data-poor markets?
- How can the incidence of taxes, subsidies, guarantees and price changes be assessed without overstating causality?
- Which longitudinal sources support mobility analysis in settings with large informal sectors?
- How should geographic comparisons account for reliability, travel time, security and affordability?
- What safeguards are needed when investigating ownership and influence using incomplete records?
- How should interventions be tested for effects on existing participants, excluded groups and new gatekeepers?
- Which distributional questions need formal Workstream B specifications?

## 19. Short summary

The distribution of economic power and opportunity concerns who owns resources, controls decisions, influences terms, accesses markets and institutions, captures benefits, and bears costs and risks. Income, wealth, ownership, control, bargaining power, market power, institutional influence and practical access are related but distinct. Differences in alternatives, liquidity, information, infrastructure, rules, networks and history can create unequal feasible choices and persistent advantages. Yet disparities alone do not prove discrimination, abuse or a specific causal mechanism. A reliable investigation defines the dimension and population, measures the distribution, maps relationships and constraints, tests alternative explanations, and traces who benefits or bears the burden. For Lifeconomist, this concept helps explain the structure beneath observed economic outcomes while preserving evidence discipline.

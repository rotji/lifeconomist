## 1. Purpose: why a comparison register is necessary

Lifeconomist investigates interconnected economic reality. In that reality, words that sound similar may describe different things, different stages of a process, different accounting boundaries, different time horizons or different kinds of evidence. If those distinctions collapse, a valid observation can become a misleading conclusion.

For example, a government can announce a financing commitment without disbursing funds; a company can report accounting profit while experiencing weak cash flow; a bank can hold reserves while a business struggles to obtain credit; and a country can record real GDP growth while household purchasing power falls. These statements can all be true because the terms describe different quantities or processes.

This note is a **navigation and comparison layer** across Workstream A. It identifies the central distinction, explains why it matters, and points to the detailed concept notes. It does not attempt to replace those notes, provide a complete accounting or legal glossary, or settle context-specific definitions where the relevant standard matters.

## 2. A general method for distinguishing terms

When two terms are being used as if they were interchangeable, ask:

1. **What is the object?** Money, an asset, a liability, a flow, a stock, a price, a right, an institution, an action or an outcome?
2. **What is the unit?** Currency amount, physical quantity, percentage, rate, person, enterprise, transaction, sector or country?
3. **What is the time basis?** A point in time, a period, a forecast, a commitment date, a payment date or a settlement date?
4. **What is the boundary?** Individual, household, company, consolidated group, bank, government, sector or whole economy?
5. **What stage of the process?** Proposed, approved, contracted, paid, delivered, deployed, used or producing an outcome?
6. **What valuation basis?** Nominal or real, gross or net, market or book value, before or after tax, accrued or cash basis?
7. **What evidence supports it?** An announcement, a contract, a ledger, a transaction record, a statistical estimate, an observed outcome or a causal study?
8. **What conclusion does it permit?** Does the observation describe a level, a change, a relationship, a mechanism or a demonstrated causal effect?

**Term → definition and boundary → unit and time basis → evidence → permissible conclusion.**

In plain language, first establish exactly what the term refers to and how it is measured. Then check the time period, the entity being discussed and the evidence. Only after those checks should the observation be used to explain a broader economic outcome.

## 3. Money, wealth, capital and financial resources

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Money vs wealth | Money is a means of payment and unit of account in a monetary system; wealth is a broader stock of assets less liabilities. | A wealthy asset owner may have little immediately spendable cash; holding cash does not itself establish high net worth. |
| Wealth vs income | Wealth is a stock at a point in time; income is a flow over a period. | A high-income household may have little accumulated wealth, while a retired household may hold wealth with modest current income. |
| Money vs capital | Money can purchase goods or assets; capital may refer to productive assets, funds committed to production, or financial claims, depending on context. | A cash balance is not automatically productive capacity. |
| Capital vs investment | Capital is a resource or asset stock (or a context-specific financial concept); investment is an action or flow that may add to or change assets. | Buying an existing share is a financial investment for the buyer but does not, by itself, create new productive capital for the company. |
| Savings vs investment | Saving is income not consumed in the relevant period; investment can mean financial-asset acquisition or capital formation, depending on context. | Household saving, financial investment and national-accounts gross capital formation are not interchangeable measures. |
| Liquidity vs solvency | Liquidity is the ability to meet obligations when due; solvency concerns the longer-run ability to meet liabilities or the relationship between assets and liabilities under a specified definition. | An entity can own valuable assets yet lack cash to pay an immediate bill, or have cash today but an unsustainable balance sheet. |
| Funding vs financing | Funding describes the resources made available for an activity; financing describes arrangements and instruments through which resources are provided, borrowed, repaid or claimed. Usage varies by context. | A financing agreement does not prove cash has been received, and cash received does not prove it was spent on the stated purpose. |

## 4. Revenue, income, profit, cash flow and value

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue vs receipts | Revenue is recognized under an accounting or statistical rule; receipts are amounts actually received. | A sale on credit may generate revenue before cash arrives. |
| Revenue vs income | Revenue commonly describes proceeds from ordinary business activity; income can refer more broadly to earnings, transfers or accounting income, depending on context. | The same word can mean household income, national-accounting income or a company's accounting result. State the context. |
| Revenue vs profit | Revenue is before deducting relevant expenses; profit is a residual after specified costs under a defined accounting measure. | Large sales do not establish profitability. |
| Profit vs cash flow | Profit is an accounting result under recognition and matching rules; cash flow tracks cash inflows and outflows over a period. | Credit sales, inventory, depreciation, capital expenditure, debt repayment and timing can separate profit from cash movement. |
| Accounting profit vs cash generated | Profit includes non-cash items and accruals; cash generated depends on actual cash movements and the chosen cash-flow definition. | A profitable firm can face a liquidity crisis, while positive cash flow can result from borrowing or selling assets rather than profitable operations. |
| Price vs value vs cost | Price is the amount quoted or exchanged; value depends on the valuation perspective and purpose; cost is the resource sacrifice or expenditure associated with an activity under a defined boundary. | Price, customer usefulness, production cost and social cost may differ. |
| Output vs sales | Output is production during a period; sales are transactions with customers and may include goods produced earlier. | Inventory changes mean output and sales need not match in the same period. |
| Economic usefulness vs GDP inclusion | Something can be useful without falling inside the production boundary or valuation rules of GDP. | GDP is a defined accounting measure, not a complete score of social usefulness or welfare. |
| Economic value to a customer vs value added | Customer value is a benefit judged by the customer; national-accounting value added is output value less intermediate consumption under defined rules. | A product can be highly useful yet have low measured value added, and a price paid is not identical to GDP contribution. |

## 5. Output, productivity, growth and national accounting

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Output vs productivity | Output is the amount produced; productivity relates output to one or more inputs. | Output can rise because more labour or capital is used even if productivity does not improve. |
| Output growth vs productivity growth | Output growth measures a change in production; productivity growth measures a change in output per input or a multifactor measure. | They have different drivers and policy implications. |
| GDP vs national wealth | GDP is a production flow over a period; national wealth is a stock concept involving assets and liabilities under a specified boundary. | A large annual GDP does not directly measure accumulated national assets or net worth. |
| Nominal growth vs real growth | Nominal change includes price changes; real growth attempts to isolate volume changes using a specified price method. | Rising current-price GDP may reflect inflation rather than more real output. |
| GDP share vs sector growth vs contribution to GDP growth | Share is a sector's fraction of total GDP; sector growth is its own rate of change; contribution to aggregate growth depends on weights and accounting conventions. | A large sector is not necessarily the fastest-growing sector or the largest contributor to growth in a particular period. |
| Gross vs net | Gross measures before a specified deduction; net measures after it. The deduction must be named. | Gross investment and net investment differ after consumption of fixed capital; gross income and net income can use different deductions. |
| Level vs rate of change | A level is the amount at a time; a rate describes change relative to time or a base. | A high inflation rate and a high price level are different conditions. |
| Stock vs flow | A stock is measured at a point in time; a flow is measured over a period. | Debt is commonly a stock; borrowing, repayments and debt service are flows. |

## 6. Public finance, debt and fiscal operations

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Debt vs deficit | Debt is an outstanding liability stock; deficit is a period's excess of defined expenditure over defined revenue. | A deficit can add to debt, but valuation, financing operations and other adjustments also affect the debt stock. |
| Debt stock vs debt-service flow | Debt stock is the outstanding amount at a date; debt service is principal and/or interest due or paid over a period, as specified. | Two governments with similar debt stocks can face different annual payment burdens. |
| Public revenue vs financing | Revenue is income received under a defined fiscal classification; financing includes borrowing, asset transactions and other means of covering a funding need. | Borrowing is not ordinarily treated as tax revenue, though classifications depend on the accounting framework. |
| Budget allocation vs approval vs disbursement vs expenditure | Allocation is a budget provision; approval authorizes or accepts a step; disbursement releases funds; expenditure records use or recognition under the applicable system. | Announced or approved amounts do not prove funds reached a recipient or delivered a service. |
| Tax liability vs tax incidence | Liability is the legal obligation to remit a tax; incidence is who bears the economic burden after adjustments in prices, wages, quantities or returns. | The party legally paying a tax may not bear its full economic cost. |
| Public spending vs service outcome | Spending is a financial input; a service outcome concerns what was delivered and what changed. | Higher expenditure alone does not prove better service quality or social impact. |
| Direct debt vs contingent liability | Direct debt is an existing recognized obligation under the relevant definition; a contingent liability may crystallize if a specified event occurs. | Guarantees and public-private arrangements can create fiscal exposure beyond reported direct debt. |

**Related notes:** [Government Finance, Taxation and Public Institutions](government-finance-taxation-and-public-institutions.md); [Credit, Debt, Banking and Financial Intermediation](credit-debt-banking-and-financial-intermediation.md).

## 7. Financial markets, banking and investment

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Financial-asset purchase vs productive investment | Buying a financial claim changes ownership of that claim; productive investment may create or improve productive assets. | Secondary-market trading does not automatically add new capital to the issuer or economy. |
| Primary vs secondary market | Primary issuance creates or sells a newly issued security; secondary trading transfers an existing security between holders. | Trading volume is not the same as new financing raised by an issuer. |
| Market liquidity vs funding liquidity | Market liquidity is the ability to trade an asset with limited price impact; funding liquidity is the ability to obtain cash or financing to meet obligations. | A firm may hold tradable assets but be unable to borrow against them at the needed time or price. |
| Central-bank reserves vs commercial-bank deposits | Reserves are balances eligible institutions hold at the central bank; deposits are liabilities banks owe to customers. | They occupy different balance-sheet positions and do not move one-for-one in every transaction. |
| Credit vs money | Credit is a claim or arrangement that permits resources to be obtained now against repayment or another obligation; money serves monetary functions. Some bank lending creates deposits, but credit and money are not synonyms. | A loan agreement, a deposit balance and central-bank reserves are distinct objects. |
| Assets under management vs assets owned | AUM is the value of assets managed on behalf of clients or funds under a defined method; assets owned are assets held for the entity's own account. | A manager's AUM is not necessarily its own balance-sheet wealth. |
| Commitment vs approval vs disbursement vs deployment | A commitment is a stated or contracted intention/obligation under its terms; approval is authorization; disbursement is release of funds; deployment is use in the stated activity. | Reported commitments or approvals can substantially exceed funds actually delivered or put to work. |
| Market value vs book value | Market value estimates an exchange value under specified conditions; book value is recorded under accounting policies and measurement rules. | Neither is universally the correct value for every question. |
| Ownership vs custody vs settlement | Ownership concerns rights; custody is safekeeping/administration; settlement completes transfer of cash and/or securities under market rules. | A custodian or payment intermediary is not automatically the beneficial owner. |

**Related notes:** [Financial Markets and Market Infrastructure](financial-markets-and-market-infrastructure.md); [Capital Markets, Institutional Investors and Investment Funds](capital-markets-institutional-investors-and-investment-funds.md); [Investment, Saving, Financial Markets and Capital Allocation](investment-saving-financial-markets-and-capital-allocation.md).

## 8. Prices, inflation, interest rates and foreign exchange

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation vs high price level | Inflation is a rate of change in a price index; the price level is the index level. | Inflation can fall while prices remain high or continue rising more slowly. |
| Disinflation vs deflation | Disinflation is a decline in the inflation rate; deflation is a fall in the general price level over the relevant period. | Slower price increases do not mean that prices have returned to earlier levels. |
| Nominal vs real income or interest rate | Nominal values are stated in current money terms; real values adjust for a specified price change or inflation measure. | The choice of deflator and period affects the real estimate. |
| Policy rate vs market rate | A policy rate is set or administered by a central bank under its framework; market rates reflect trading, risk, term, liquidity and other conditions. | A policy-rate change does not mechanically pass through equally to all borrowers and assets. |
| Exchange rate vs purchasing power | An exchange rate is a price between currencies; purchasing power describes what money can buy in a defined basket and place. | A nominal currency depreciation and a change in relative domestic prices are connected but not identical. |
| Currency denomination vs residence | Denomination identifies the currency in which an instrument is expressed; residence identifies the institutional unit's economic residence under a statistical framework. | A naira-denominated claim is not necessarily owned by a Nigerian resident, and a resident may hold foreign-currency assets. |
| Gross flows vs net flows | Gross flows record movements in each direction; net flow is the difference under a defined scope. | Small net flows can conceal large two-way movements. |
| Transaction vs valuation change | A transaction is an economic event; a valuation change changes recorded value without necessarily representing a new transaction. | A change in the value of foreign assets may reflect exchange rates or prices, not new capital inflow. |

**Related notes:** [Inflation, Interest Rates and Purchasing Power](inflation-interest-rates-and-purchasing-power.md); [Foreign Exchange, International Money and Global Capital Flows](foreign-exchange-international-money-and-global-capital-flows.md).

## 9. Ownership, roles, power and distribution

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership vs control vs beneficial ownership | Legal title, practical decision authority and the natural person/entity that ultimately benefits or controls can differ. | A registry record alone may not fully reveal effective control or economic benefit. |
| Owner vs operator vs funder vs intermediary vs beneficiary | These are different roles in an economic arrangement. | A party performing one role should not automatically be assumed to perform another. |
| Access vs opportunity | Access is ability to reach or use a resource; opportunity is the realistic possibility of achieving an outcome through it. | Availability on paper does not guarantee practical or beneficial participation. |
| Inequality vs poverty | Inequality describes a distribution or relative difference; poverty applies a specified threshold or deprivation framework. | A society can reduce poverty while inequality rises, or reduce inequality without ending poverty. |
| Correlation vs causation | Correlation is observed co-movement; causation claims that changing one factor changes another under specified conditions. | A plausible mechanism and a statistical relationship are not alone proof of causality. |
| Risk vs uncertainty | Usage varies by discipline; often risk refers to outcomes with a modelled or estimable probability structure, while uncertainty concerns probabilities or outcomes that are not reliably known. | State the definition being used; do not pretend all unknowns are quantifiable risks. |
| Bargaining advantage vs wrongdoing | Leverage is a relative ability to influence terms; wrongdoing requires evidence against a specified legal, contractual or ethical standard. | Unequal terms alone do not establish illegality or improper intent. |
| Recipient vs beneficiary | The recipient receives a payment, service or asset directly; the beneficiary receives the ultimate advantage, which may be indirect. | Payment records may not establish who ultimately gains. |

## 10. Evidence terms: observation, indicator, estimate and explanation

| Distinction | Practical meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Concept vs indicator | A concept is the phenomenon to understand; an indicator is an operational measure intended to represent some aspect of it. | An indicator captures only the defined aspect and may omit important dimensions. |
| Indicator vs proxy | An indicator is a measure used to monitor a defined concept; a proxy is an indirect measure used when the target cannot be measured directly. A proxy may also function as an indicator. | Proxy validity needs justification, not just availability. |
| Observation vs estimate | An observation is a recorded value under a measurement process; an estimate infers a quantity from data and a method. | Estimates depend on assumptions, sample design, model choice and uncertainty. |
| Estimate vs forecast | An estimate assesses an unknown or incompletely observed quantity; a forecast predicts a future quantity or event. | A current-state estimate should not be presented as a future prediction. |
| Description vs explanation | Description establishes what is observed; explanation proposes how or why it occurred. | A trend chart can document a change without identifying its cause. |
| Mechanism vs causal proof | A mechanism explains a plausible pathway; causal evidence evaluates whether that pathway produced the observed outcome in the case at hand. | Plausibility is a starting hypothesis, not a completed causal test. |
| Official source vs complete evidence | An official source may be authoritative for a particular record or definition but still have a limited scope, delay or reporting error. | Source authority does not remove the need to check coverage and purpose. |
| Absence of evidence vs evidence of absence | No observed record may reflect no event, incomplete coverage, delayed publication or failed detection. | Do not interpret an empty search as proof that something does not exist. |

**Related note:** [Economic Measurement, Evidence and Inference](economic-measurement-evidence-and-inference.md).

## 11. A process-stage comparison: announcements are not outcomes

A recurring Lifeconomist investigation pattern is to trace a claim through its stages.

**Announcement → authorization/commitment → contract or obligation → disbursement/transaction → delivery or deployment → use → observed outcome.**

In plain language, each stage is a separate factual claim. An announcement proves that something was announced, not that money was transferred. A signed contract may create an obligation without immediate payment. A disbursement may be recorded without proving that the intended service reached users. Delivery does not automatically prove effectiveness. Evidence must match the exact stage being claimed.

The stages vary by domain: public finance, development programmes, investment funds, procurement, lending, supply chains and infrastructure projects each have their own legal and operational definitions. The chain is a guide for questions, not a universal accounting standard. Record the specific milestone, date, actor, amount or quantity, source and uncertainty.

## 12. How to use this register in research and decisions

When a report, dataset, public statement or conversation uses a term:
1. Preserve the source's exact wording.
2. Identify the relevant detailed concept note.
3. Establish the definition used in that source and whether it matches the intended question.
4. Record the unit, entity boundary, time basis, valuation basis and stage.
5. Identify whether the value is observed, reported, estimated, forecast or inferred.
6. Check whether the evidence supports only description or also a causal claim.
7. Use a precise term in Lifeconomist's own analysis and explain any translation between definitions.
8. If the definition changes the result materially, preserve both interpretations and document the decision.

Do not force a single universal definition where accounting standards, national accounts, financial-market conventions, law or everyday language legitimately use terms differently. Name the context and source standard.

## 14. Established principles, limits and open questions

### Established principles
- Related terms can differ by object, time basis, entity boundary, accounting convention or process stage.
- Stocks and flows must not be compared without a valid bridge.
- Financial transactions and productive capital formation are distinct.
- Revenue, profit and cash flow answer different questions.
- GDP is a defined production measure, not national wealth or a complete welfare measure.
- Commitments, approvals, disbursements, deployment and outcomes are separate milestones.
- A legal payer, immediate recipient, ultimate beneficiary and risk bearer may differ.
- A measure's availability does not establish that it is a valid proxy for the target concept.
- A plausible mechanism does not by itself establish causality.
- Definitions should be explicit when meanings vary across ordinary speech, accounting, economic theory, law and market practice.

### Limits
- This register is a practical conceptual guide, not a substitute for applicable accounting standards, statistical manuals, laws, contract terms or instrument documentation.
- It does not claim that every term has one universal definition across disciplines.
- It does not establish facts about a current company, transaction, government programme or market.
- It does not replace the detailed concept notes or the formal indicator specifications developed in Workstream B.
- Illustrative chains describe questions and mechanisms to investigate, not proof that a particular outcome occurred.

## 15. Short summary

This comparison register reduces conceptual errors across Lifeconomist by showing why commonly confused economic and financial terms must be separated by definition, unit, time basis, entity boundary, valuation basis, process stage and evidence. Money is not wealth; wealth is not income; revenue is not profit; profit is not cash flow; debt is not deficit; liquidity is not solvency; a financial-asset purchase is not necessarily productive investment; a commitment is not a disbursement; a reported measure is not automatically a valid indicator; and a plausible mechanism is not causal proof. These distinctions should guide research, measurement, monitoring, footprint investigations and practical decisions. The register is a map to the detailed concept notes, not a replacement for them.

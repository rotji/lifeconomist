# Concept 22 — Trade, Supply Chains and Distribution Networks

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; current sector-specific flows and formal indicators require separate, source-backed investigation  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 21 — Risk, Uncertainty and Financial Resilience](risk-uncertainty-and-financial-resilience.md)

## 1. Purpose

Economic activity is connected through the movement and transformation of goods, services, money, information and rights. A product sold to a household may depend on raw materials, intermediate inputs, energy, production, packaging, transport, wholesale distribution, retail, payment services and after-sales support. These stages can cross firms, regions and countries, but they do not necessarily share one owner or contract.

This note establishes vocabulary for understanding trade, supply chains, value chains, logistics, distribution, inventory and network dependencies. It is a conceptual foundation—not a map of any specific sector and not evidence that a proposed connection is active or important.

## 2. Trade, exchange and transactions

**Trade** is the exchange of goods or services between parties. In international statistics, trade in goods and trade in services have specific measurement rules, coverage and valuation methods. Everyday use may be broader.

A **transaction** is an event or arrangement between parties, such as a sale, purchase, transfer, service provision or financial settlement. A transaction can transfer an existing asset, provide a service, create a claim or settle an obligation; not every transaction creates new production.

**Domestic trade** occurs within an economy under the relevant statistical definition. **International trade** crosses an economic or customs boundary according to the applicable statistical framework. The exact boundary used matters: customs territory, national residence and ownership are not always identical.

Distinguish:
- **Trade flow:** a measured movement or transaction during a period.
- **Trade balance:** a defined difference between exports and imports for a stated scope and period.
- **Trade volume:** quantity or real-volume measure, with the unit and aggregation method specified.
- **Trade value:** monetary valuation, which can change because of prices, quantities, exchange rates or product composition.
- **Trade partner:** a country or entity identified under a particular attribution rule, such as origin, destination, consignment or residence.

An increase in trade value does not by itself show that physical quantities increased; prices, exchange rates and composition can also change.

## 3. Goods, services, information, payments and rights

A supply network may move several different things, which should not be conflated:

- **Physical goods:** tangible products, materials, components, equipment or inventory.
- **Services:** activities supplied to another party, such as transport, repair, finance, software, insurance or professional work.
- **Information:** specifications, forecasts, orders, tracking data, quality records and instructions.
- **Payments:** transfers or settlement of monetary obligations.
- **Legal or economic rights:** ownership, licences, claims, security interests, contractual rights or permission to use an asset.

These flows may be related but do not necessarily move together. Goods can be shipped before payment; payment can be made in advance; title can transfer at a contractual point different from physical delivery; and a service may be supplied digitally without a physical shipment.

For each flow, record what moves, between whom, in which direction, when, under what agreement and with what evidence. A payment record does not alone prove physical delivery; a shipping record does not alone prove final ownership or payment.

## 4. Supply chain, value chain and distribution network

A **supply chain** is a set of activities and relationships involved in sourcing, producing, moving and delivering inputs or products to users. The scope can include upstream suppliers, direct suppliers, producers, logistics providers, distributors, retailers and downstream customers.

A **value chain** emphasizes activities through which value is created, transformed, captured or supported. It may include research, design, production, marketing, service, software, finance and intellectual property, not only physical movement.

A **distribution network** concerns routes, facilities, intermediaries and arrangements through which goods or services reach intended recipients. A **logistics network** focuses more specifically on transport, storage, handling, fulfillment and related coordination.

The terms overlap but are not interchangeable:
- A supply-chain map may identify input dependencies without estimating value created at each stage.
- A value-chain analysis may examine design, branding and margin capture even where the physical route is simple.
- A logistics map may show warehouses and routes without proving who owns the goods.
- A distribution map may show delivery channels without revealing the full upstream supplier base.

State the scope and analytical purpose before drawing a network.

## 5. Upstream, downstream and chain stages

**Upstream** activities generally provide inputs or capabilities used by later stages. **Downstream** activities generally move output closer to distribution, use or final demand. The reference point must be stated: a retailer's upstream suppliers differ from a raw-material producer's.

A simplified chain may include:
1. Resource extraction or primary production.
2. Processing and intermediate inputs.
3. Component or product manufacturing.
4. Packaging and quality assurance.
5. Transport, freight and border processing.
6. Warehousing and inventory management.
7. Wholesale or other intermediary distribution.
8. Retail, installation or service delivery.
9. Final use, maintenance, reuse, recycling or disposal.

Not every product follows this sequence. Services can have different stages; production may be distributed across several sites; and returned, repaired or recycled products can create reverse flows.

A stage label is a functional description, not proof that a distinct company performs it. One company can perform several stages, and one stage can involve many firms.

## 6. Direct and indirect suppliers

A **direct supplier** has a direct commercial or contractual relationship with the focal organization under the scope being assessed. An **indirect supplier** provides inputs through another supplier or a more distant tier.

Tier labels require a reference point. “Tier 1” normally means a direct supplier to the focal buyer, while Tier 2 supplies a Tier 1 supplier, but organizations and datasets may define tiers differently.

A direct supplier list does not reveal every upstream dependency. A supplier may depend on a single component producer, energy source, port, cloud service or transport route. Conversely, a supplier's supplier may have substitutes and therefore may not be a critical dependency.

Do not infer a complete supply chain from a partial supplier list. Mark known links, reported links, estimated links and unknown tiers separately. A commercial relationship, a physical input relationship and a corporate ownership relationship are different kinds of links.

## 7. Producer, supplier, manufacturer, distributor, wholesaler, retailer and agent

These roles describe different functions, but firms may combine them:

- **Producer:** creates a good or supplies an output; the term may cover agricultural, extractive, industrial or service production.
- **Manufacturer:** transforms materials or components into products through a production process.
- **Supplier:** provides goods, services or other inputs to a buyer; it need not manufacture them.
- **Distributor:** moves, markets or supplies products through a distribution channel, with contractual roles varying by industry.
- **Wholesaler:** typically sells goods in larger quantities to businesses or other intermediaries rather than primarily to final consumers.
- **Retailer:** sells goods or services to final consumers, although some retailers also sell to businesses.
- **Agent or broker:** arranges transactions or represents a party, often without taking ownership of the goods; the actual legal role depends on the agreement.
- **Freight forwarder or logistics provider:** arranges or performs transport, handling, storage or related services; responsibilities depend on the contract.
- **Importer/exporter:** parties recorded as importing or exporting under a relevant legal, customs or statistical framework; they may not be the producer or ultimate owner.

Do not infer ownership, risk-bearing or legal responsibility from a role label alone. Contracts, invoices, transport documents, title terms and applicable law determine the specific relationship.

## 8. Production, shipment, delivery, sale and ownership transfer

Several milestones can occur at different times:

- An order is placed.
- A contract or purchase commitment is made.
- Goods are produced or services are performed.
- Goods are dispatched.
- A carrier accepts custody.
- Goods cross a border or arrive at a facility.
- The buyer inspects or accepts delivery.
- Legal title transfers under the contract.
- An invoice is issued.
- Payment becomes due.
- Payment is settled.
- Goods are sold onward or used in production.

These events are not equivalent. An order or announcement is not proof of production. Dispatch is not proof of arrival. Arrival is not always acceptance. An invoice is not proof of payment. Payment is not always proof that the contracted goods were delivered as agreed. Ownership and risk of loss can transfer at contractually defined points that differ from physical custody.

Evidence should be matched to the claim: contracts for terms; purchase orders for orders; production records for output; bills of lading or tracking for transport; proof of delivery and acceptance for receipt; invoices for billing; bank or settlement records for payment. Each record has limitations and should be cross-checked where the decision is consequential.

## 9. Physical flows, financial flows and contractual flows

A **physical flow** concerns the movement of goods or materials. A **financial flow** concerns money or financial settlement. A **contractual flow** is a sequence of rights and obligations among parties. An **information flow** concerns instructions, status, forecasts or records.

These flows are related but not identical. A distributor can arrange a physical shipment without owning the inventory. A bank can finance a transaction without handling the goods. A marketplace can facilitate an order while a separate seller fulfils it. A parent company can own a subsidiary that trades goods, but the group relationship does not prove which entity contracted or received payment.

A useful investigation separates:
1. Who made or supplied the product?
2. Who sold or invoiced it?
3. Who physically handled or transported it?
4. Who held title at each stage?
5. Who bore inventory, damage, currency and credit risks?
6. Who paid whom, when, and for what obligation?
7. Which records independently support each answer?

## 10. Inventory, stock, throughput and capacity

**Inventory** is a stock of goods or materials held at a point in time for production, sale, repair or other purposes. It can include raw materials, work in progress and finished goods, depending on the accounting and operational scope.

**Throughput** is the amount processed or moved through a process or system over a specified time. It needs a defined unit and boundary, such as units per day, tonnes per month or orders fulfilled per hour.

**Capacity** is the maximum or sustainable level of output or processing under specified conditions. **Utilization** compares actual activity with a defined capacity measure. **Lead time** is elapsed time between specified events, such as order and delivery. **Cycle time** measures the time required for a defined process or unit of work. **Inventory turnover** relates inventory use or sales to average inventory under a stated accounting method.

Inventory is a stock; throughput is a flow. A large inventory does not automatically mean high throughput or strong demand. High throughput does not necessarily mean high profit. High utilization can improve efficiency in some contexts but leave little slack when demand or supply changes.

Inventory measures require care because book value, physical units, usable inventory, obsolete inventory and goods in transit are different quantities.

## 11. Bottlenecks, constraints, scarcity and shortages

A **bottleneck** is a constraint that limits the throughput or performance of a process or network under specified conditions. A **constraint** can be physical, operational, contractual, regulatory, financial or informational.

**Scarcity** means resources are limited relative to competing uses or wants in a stated context. A **shortage** commonly means available supply is insufficient relative to demand at a given price, location and time, though formal definitions vary. A **stockout** occurs when a particular item is unavailable for a required transaction or use at a specified point.

These terms should not be used interchangeably. A product can be scarce in general but available at a particular retailer; a temporary stockout can arise from poor replenishment despite adequate aggregate supply; a bottleneck can exist in transport, testing, customs, electricity or working capital rather than production capacity itself.

To assess a suspected bottleneck, define the affected flow, location, period, capacity and demand. Check lead times, queues, utilization, backlogs, inventory, rejected orders, alternative routes and substitute inputs. An observed delay alone does not reveal its cause.

## 12. Procurement, sourcing and supplier management

**Procurement** is the process of identifying needs, sourcing, selecting suppliers, contracting, ordering and managing purchases. **Sourcing** focuses on finding and evaluating supply options. **Supplier management** concerns the ongoing relationship, performance, risk and compliance.

**Single sourcing** uses one selected source for a particular input; **sole sourcing** often means only one source is available or feasible, but terminology varies. **Dual or multiple sourcing** uses more than one source. A supplier may appear diversified but rely on the same upstream producer or transport route as another supplier.

Supplier selection may consider price, quality, delivery reliability, minimum order quantity, payment terms, distance, certification, currency, after-sales support and switching costs. Lowest purchase price is not necessarily lowest total cost if defects, delays, financing, storage or downtime are material.

A contract or purchase order creates specified obligations, but it does not prove delivery or performance. Monitor actual performance against agreed terms and record exceptions.

## 13. Trade terms, transport and border processes

Goods can move by road, rail, sea, air, inland waterways or combinations of modes. Route selection depends on cost, time, reliability, capacity, handling requirements, security, geography and legal restrictions.

**Freight** is the transport of goods and can also refer to the charge for that service. **Customs clearance** is the completion of applicable border formalities. **Tariffs** are customs duties applied under the relevant rules. Other trade costs can include insurance, port handling, documentation, inspections, storage, demurrage, financing and inland transport.

International commercial terms such as Incoterms allocate specified responsibilities, costs and risks between seller and buyer for defined aspects of delivery. They do not, by themselves, settle every question of title transfer, payment, product quality, dispute resolution or applicable law. The exact rule, named place and version matter.

Do not assume that a country named in a shipping record is the product's origin, that the importer is the manufacturer, or that the seller bears all transport risk. Use the underlying documents and applicable rules.

## 13.1 Comparative advantage, specialization and trade costs

**Comparative advantage** describes a situation in which an actor can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another actor, even if it is not absolutely more productive in every activity. It is a framework for explaining potential gains from specialization and exchange, not a guarantee that every participant or region benefits equally.

Actual trade patterns also depend on transport and energy costs, reliability, scale, skills, financing, tariffs and non-tariff measures, standards, market power, security, infrastructure and the ability to change production. A theoretical cost advantage may not translate into a commercially viable export if delivery is unreliable, finance is expensive or buyers cannot be reached. Adjustment costs and the distribution of gains matter as well.

**Specialization → exchange based on relative opportunity costs → potential aggregate gains → distribution shaped by capabilities, market access and bargaining power.**

In plain language, two places may both gain from concentrating on activities where each gives up less of its other production to make a unit of a product. But the total gain does not determine who captures it. Workers, firms, consumers and regions can experience different effects, especially when skills, infrastructure, ownership or the ability to move into new activities are unevenly distributed.

## 13.2 Trade finance, letters of credit and settlement risk

Trade finance comprises arrangements that help buyers and sellers manage the timing, trust and funding problems associated with a transaction. Depending on the arrangement, a bank or other intermediary may provide a loan, guarantee, documentary collection, letter of credit, insurance or another form of risk mitigation.

A **letter of credit** is a bank's undertaking to pay a beneficiary when the stipulated documentary conditions are met, subject to the applicable terms and rules. It is not a universal guarantee that goods are physically sound or that every commercial dispute disappears. Documentary compliance, fraud risk, issuing-bank capacity, country risk, fees, expiry dates, discrepancies and applicable rules remain relevant. Documentary collections and open-account trade allocate risks differently.

Keep these milestones separate:

- credit approval or facility commitment;
- issuance of a trade-finance instrument;
- shipment or delivery of goods;
- presentation and examination of documents;
- acceptance or rejection of documents;
- payment or reimbursement;
- final settlement and discharge of the obligation.

A bank's involvement may shift, share or finance a risk without eliminating it. A guarantee or letter of credit can create contingent exposure before any cash payment occurs. Evidence of an instrument's issuance does not alone prove shipment, compliant documents, payment or final settlement.

## 13.3 Import dependence, local content and substitution

**Import dependence** describes reliance on imported goods, services, components, technology, energy or intermediate inputs. It should be defined by product, value or volume measure, use, time period and geographic boundary. A high import share can indicate exposure to exchange-rate movements, external supply interruptions or foreign policy changes, but it does not by itself show that imports are inefficient or that domestic replacement is feasible.

**Local content** refers to the portion of a product's value, inputs, labor, services or activities attributed to a specified domestic economy under a stated definition or policy. Different measures can produce different results: local assembly does not necessarily mean domestic ownership of technology, high domestic value added, local sourcing of key inputs or domestic control of intellectual property.

**Import substitution** is the replacement of some imported products or inputs with domestic production. Assessing it requires more than observing a new local producer. Examine cost, quality, scale, reliability, imported intermediate inputs, foreign exchange requirements, productivity, competition, consumer prices and the opportunity cost of resources. A policy can reduce one dependency while creating another.

A useful investigation separates the imported final good, imported intermediate inputs, domestic value added, ownership of productive assets, technology origin and the location where income accrues. Trade data, input-output tables, company records and supplier mapping each answer different parts of that question.

## 14. Distribution channels and market access

A **distribution channel** is a route through which products or services reach buyers or users. Channels can include direct sales, wholesalers, distributors, retailers, agents, marketplaces, franchises, public procurement and digital delivery.

**Market access** is the ability to reach a market under its commercial, regulatory, technical and logistical conditions. **Market coverage** describes which customers or locations a channel reaches. **Last-mile delivery** concerns the final segment of distribution to the recipient or point of use.

Intermediaries can provide aggregation, storage, credit, transport, local knowledge, quality assurance, marketing, customer service and risk absorption. Their margins are not automatically waste or evidence of exploitation. Equally, a long channel can add costs, delay, opacity or bargaining-power imbalances. These questions require evidence about functions performed, alternatives, prices, service quality and who bears risk.

A direct-to-consumer channel may remove some intermediaries while creating new costs for delivery, customer acquisition, returns, payment processing and support.

## 15. Value creation, value capture, markups and margins

**Value creation** concerns the contribution of activities to the usefulness, quality, availability or economic output of a good or service. **Value capture** concerns which parties retain income, surplus or other economic benefits. These are related but distinct questions.

**Markup** commonly compares a selling price with a cost base, while **gross margin** compares gross profit with revenue. The denominator and cost definition must be explicit. A distributor's gross margin is not automatically its net profit, and a large price difference between producer and retailer is not necessarily pure profit: it may cover transport, losses, labour, rent, credit, taxes, marketing and other costs.

Price differences across stages can reflect product transformation, service quality, time, location, risk, bargaining power, taxes, market conditions or measurement inconsistencies. To attribute the difference to a specific cause, compare like-for-like products, units, dates, currencies, tax treatment, delivery terms and quantities.

Value added should not be calculated by simply summing each stage's gross sales, because intermediate inputs can be counted repeatedly. For a defined production unit, value added is generally output value less intermediate consumption under the relevant accounting framework. National accounts apply formal rules for sector boundaries and valuation.

## 16. Supply-chain concentration and dependency

**Supplier concentration** describes dependence on a limited number of suppliers under a stated measure. **Customer concentration** describes dependence on a limited number of buyers. **Route concentration** and **facility concentration** concern reliance on particular transport paths or sites. **Geographic concentration** concerns clustering in locations or jurisdictions.

Concentration can create vulnerability when substitutes are limited, switching takes time, capacity is constrained, or exposures share a common shock. It can also reflect specialization, economies of scale or quality requirements. Concentration alone does not prove fragility.

Assess the availability, capacity, qualification and switching time of alternatives; the substitutability of inputs; the geographic and infrastructure dependencies; contractual flexibility; inventory buffers; and the correlation of supplier risks. Two suppliers may not be independent if both rely on one upstream source, power grid, port or cloud provider.

## 17. Disruption, propagation and recovery

Supply disruptions can arise from equipment failure, weather, conflict, transport interruption, regulatory changes, quality problems, cyber incidents, supplier insolvency, labour shortages or financial distress. The effect depends on the exposed flow, timing, inventory, substitutes, contract terms and recovery time.

A possible propagation sequence is:
1. An upstream input or transport service is interrupted.
2. The downstream organization consumes available inventory or slows production.
3. Delivery times lengthen and contractual obligations become harder to meet.
4. Revenue, working capital or customer confidence is affected.
5. Suppliers, workers, lenders or customers face secondary effects.
6. Substitution, rerouting, inventory, policy action or demand adjustment may contain or amplify the impact.

This is a scenario, not a prediction. Some firms may have alternate suppliers, spare capacity or sufficient buffers. Others may face a small disruption that becomes severe because the input is essential and difficult to replace.

A strong causal claim requires evidence of the sequence, timing, relevant quantities, affected counterparties and alternative explanations. Do not infer system-wide shortage from one firm's stockout or infer a global shock from one delayed shipment.

## 18. Circular, reverse and secondary flows

Not all flows run from producer to final user. **Reverse logistics** concerns the movement of goods back from customers or downstream locations for return, repair, refurbishment, recycling or disposal. **Returns** may arise from defects, customer preference, excess stock or contractual terms.

**Reuse** keeps an item in service with limited processing; **repair** restores function; **refurbishment** restores or improves condition; **remanufacturing** rebuilds a product to a defined standard; **recycling** recovers materials for use. These processes have different resource, quality and accounting implications.

Used goods, waste, by-products and recycled inputs can create secondary markets. Their flows should be tracked separately where needed to avoid counting the original production and the same material's later circulation as if each were new primary output.

## 19. Digital networks and service supply chains

Modern supply networks can depend on software, cloud infrastructure, telecommunications, payment processors, identity services, data providers and platform marketplaces. A digital service can be a critical input even when it has no visible physical shipment.

Digital dependencies may be indirect and shared: multiple suppliers can depend on one cloud region, payment network, software library or telecommunications backbone. This can create correlated operational exposure, but the existence of a shared provider does not alone establish that a disruption will propagate to every dependent customer.

Map the service provided, criticality, substitutability, recovery time, data portability, contractual terms, geographic scope and any known single points of failure. Avoid assuming that a supplier's marketing statement proves the architecture or resilience of its service.

## 20. Supply-chain data and measurement

Common evidence sources include purchase orders, contracts, invoices, shipping records, customs records, warehouse and inventory systems, supplier disclosures, bills of materials, production records, company filings, trade statistics, transport schedules and interviews. Each source observes only part of the network.

Measurement choices should specify:
- Unit: money, physical quantity, weight, volume, hours, shipments, orders or service units.
- Period and timing rule: order date, shipment date, border crossing, delivery, invoice or payment.
- Geography and boundary: residence, customs territory, origin, destination or facility.
- Valuation: currency, exchange-rate convention, taxes, freight, insurance and price basis.
- Entity identity: legal entity, facility, brand, group, supplier identifier or country.
- Flow direction and stage: upstream/downstream, direct/indirect, forward/reverse.
- Status: ordered, produced, dispatched, in transit, delivered, accepted, paid, returned or cancelled.
- Evidence quality: official record, contractual record, company statement, third-party report, estimate or inference.

Trade statistics and firm-level records may use different units, coverage, time conventions and valuation bases. Reconcile definitions before comparing them. Missing data do not mean no relationship; estimated data do not become verified simply because they are structured.

### Input-output tables and the limits of aggregate tracing

**Input-output tables** describe how industries purchase inputs from and supply outputs to other industries within a defined economy and statistical period. They can help estimate direct and indirect production relationships and the industries that may be exposed when demand, prices or supply conditions change. A supply-use table may provide a related accounting framework.

These tables are aggregated models, not a live map of every supplier or shipment. Results depend on industry classifications, domestic/import splits, valuation conventions, data vintage and assumptions about production relationships. Fixed-coefficient calculations may not capture substitution, capacity constraints, price changes, inventory adjustments, informal activity or rapid technological change. Industry-level relationships cannot identify a specific firm's exposure without more granular evidence.

When using an input-output multiplier, state the table, year, price basis, model, shock definition and assumptions. Avoid treating a modelled indirect effect as an observed transaction or adding gross transaction values in a way that double-counts intermediate goods across stages.

## 21. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Trade vs logistics | Trade concerns exchange; logistics concerns movement, handling, storage and fulfillment. |
| Supply chain vs value chain | Supply-chain analysis emphasizes sourcing and delivery relationships; value-chain analysis emphasizes activities and value creation/capture. |
| Supply chain vs distribution network | A supply chain can cover upstream and downstream stages; a distribution network focuses on routes and arrangements to reach recipients. |
| Supplier vs manufacturer | A supplier provides an input and may resell it rather than manufacture it. |
| Distributor vs agent | A distributor's role may include buying/reselling inventory; an agent may arrange transactions without taking title. Actual roles depend on contracts and law. |
| Direct vs indirect supplier | The distinction depends on the focal buyer and defined tier system. |
| Physical custody vs ownership | Possessing or transporting goods does not automatically establish legal title. |
| Order vs delivery | An order or commitment does not prove goods were produced or delivered. |
| Shipment vs acceptance | Dispatch or arrival is not necessarily contractual acceptance. |
| Invoice vs payment | Billing does not prove settlement. |
| Stock vs flow | Inventory is measured at a point in time; throughput or shipments are measured over a period. |
| Inventory vs capacity | Inventory is held material or goods; capacity is the ability to produce or process under stated conditions. |
| Throughput vs output value | Physical or process throughput is not identical to monetary output or value added. |
| Bottleneck vs shortage | A bottleneck constrains a process; a shortage is insufficient availability relative to demand under a specified context. |
| Scarcity vs stockout | Scarcity is a broader resource condition; a stockout is item unavailability at a point of sale or use. |
| Price spread vs profit | Price differences may cover many costs and risks; they are not pure profit by default. |
| Markup vs margin | The denominator differs; definitions and cost bases must be stated. |
| Gross sales vs value added | Summing sales across stages double-counts intermediate transactions. |
| Trade value vs trade volume | Value can change with price, exchange rates and composition; volume aims to isolate quantities under a defined method. |
| Country of origin vs exporting country | Production origin, shipment route, seller residence and exporting country may differ under applicable rules. |
| Supplier concentration vs fragility | Concentration may increase dependence, but substitutability, buffers and shared risks determine vulnerability. |
| Shared supplier vs proven contagion | A common dependency is a possible channel; actual propagation requires evidence. |
| Contract vs performance | Contractual obligations define what should happen; performance evidence establishes what happened. |
| Missing data vs no connection | An unobserved link may exist, but it must remain unknown or inferred rather than asserted as fact. |
| Reverse logistics vs new production | Returned or recycled goods move through further stages, but their circulation should not be counted automatically as new primary output. |

## 22. Investigation checklist

Before making a supply-chain or trade claim, ask:

1. What product, service, sector, organization, location and time period are in scope?
2. Which flow is being traced: goods, services, money, information, rights or several?
3. What exactly is the relationship: supplier, customer, carrier, owner, agent, financier or service provider?
4. Is the link documented, reported, estimated or hypothesized?
5. Is the entity a brand, legal company, business group, facility or country?
6. What event does the record establish: order, production, dispatch, delivery, acceptance, invoice or payment?
7. Which unit, currency, valuation basis and timing rule apply?
8. Are upstream tiers visible, and where does evidence stop?
9. Does the named intermediary take title, physical custody, contractual responsibility or only arrange the transaction?
10. Are inventory, throughput, capacity and utilization being measured separately?
11. What mechanism creates the suspected bottleneck or shortage?
12. What alternative suppliers, routes, facilities or inputs exist, and how long would switching take?
13. Are apparent alternatives exposed to the same upstream supplier or common infrastructure?
14. What costs and services explain the difference between prices at different stages?
15. Are gross sales being confused with value added or profit?
16. Could changes in price, exchange rates, product mix or timing explain the observed trade-value change?
17. What contract terms govern delivery, title, payment, insurance and risk of loss?
18. What evidence would confirm actual disruption or propagation?
19. What evidence could disconfirm the hypothesis or show the effect was contained?
20. What uncertainty, missingness or measurement mismatch remains?
21. What decision does the analysis support, and what conclusions remain premature?

## 23. Connections to other Workstreams

- **Workstream A:** links production, value added, costs, markets, money, accounting, risk and institutions through concrete exchange and delivery processes.
- **Workstream B — Measurement:** trade, inventory, throughput, lead time, concentration and value-added indicators need defined units, boundaries, dates, valuation rules and source quality. No current trade or sector values are asserted here.
- **Workstream C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** distinguish a contract announcement, shipment, delivery, price signal, verified disruption and observed outcome. Track revisions, reporting delays and source independence.
- **Workstream D — Footprint Intelligence:** map entities, facilities, suppliers, transport routes, service providers, contracts, ownership and physical/financial/information flows as separate edge types. Record provenance and confidence; do not treat co-occurrence as a supply relationship.
- **Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence:** retailer and SME applications may include supplier alternatives, stockout monitoring, replenishment, price comparison, lead-time tracking and dependency mapping. A useful service must rely on data the business can reasonably access and translate findings into practical actions.
- **Risk and Resilience (Concept 21):** supply concentration, bottlenecks, maturity and payment terms, inventory buffers and common infrastructure can shape vulnerability; their actual impact must be established for the specific case.
- **Global Financial System Exploration Map:** separate the physical flow of goods from trade finance, payment settlement, credit claims, ownership and valuation. Do not add the value of the same goods, the invoice and the financing claim as if they were three independent units of production.

## 24. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Trade, logistics, ownership, contractual obligations and payment are related but distinct.
- Supply chains, value chains and distribution networks answer different analytical questions.
- Direct supplier visibility does not guarantee visibility into indirect suppliers.
- Stock, flow, throughput, capacity and utilization require different measures.
- A bottleneck, scarcity, shortage and stockout are not interchangeable.
- Price spreads are not equivalent to profits, and gross sales across stages should not be summed as new value added.
- Network links can transmit disruptions, but propagation depends on timing, substitutability, buffers and behavior.
- Missing data indicate a limitation in observation, not proof that a relationship exists or does not exist.

### Scope limits
- No particular product, firm, industry or trade corridor is mapped here.
- No current trade volumes, supply shortages, price spreads or supplier concentration estimates are asserted.
- Legal ownership, customs origin, delivery terms and contractual risk allocation require the applicable records, rules and dates.
- Trade and firm-level data can differ in scope and valuation; they should not be combined without reconciliation.

### Questions for later evidence-based work
- Which public datasets reveal Nigerian sector and product trade flows with usable detail and transparent methodology?
- What evidence can establish actual supplier and facility relationships when public data are incomplete?
- How can Lifeconomist estimate supplier concentration without overstating missing upstream tiers?
- Which indicators distinguish a temporary stockout from a structural supply constraint?
- How should prices, taxes, freight, currency and product specifications be normalized for cross-stage comparison?
- Which SME supply-chain monitoring services solve problems that retailers can validate and pay for?
- What is the smallest reliable dataset needed to identify reorder risks or alternative suppliers without requiring access to private accounts?
- How can physical, financial and information flows be represented without double-counting transactions or treating inferred links as confirmed?

## 25. Short summary

Trade and supply networks connect producers, suppliers, logistics providers, distributors, retailers, service providers and final users through multiple kinds of flows. Goods, money, information, contracts, ownership and delivery milestones must be tracked separately. Inventory is a stock, throughput is a flow, capacity is a capability, and bottlenecks differ from shortages. Price spreads do not automatically equal profit, and gross sales at several stages cannot be summed as new value added. Network connections may transmit disruption, but causal claims require evidence about the mechanism, timing, exposure and alternatives.

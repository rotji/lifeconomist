# Concept 19 — Accounting, Balance Sheets and Financial Statements

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; formal Nigerian accounting, corporate reporting and economic indicator specifications remain subject to Workstream B and applicable standards  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 18 — Financial Markets and Market Infrastructure](financial-markets-and-market-infrastructure.md)

## 1. Purpose

Accounting organizes information about an entity's resources, obligations, performance and cash movements under defined rules. It helps answer questions such as what an entity controls or owes at a date, what income and expenses were recognized over a period, and how cash was generated or used. But accounting figures are not interchangeable with cash, market value, economic welfare, or a complete assessment of resilience.

This note introduces the vocabulary needed to read balance sheets, income statements and cash-flow statements without confusing their purposes. Accounting standards and legal requirements differ by jurisdiction, entity type and reporting framework. The note is conceptual and does not give accounting, tax or investment advice, nor does it assert current figures for any Nigerian company.

## 2. What accounting does—and does not—measure

**Accounting** is a system for recognizing, classifying, measuring, presenting and disclosing economic events and positions according to a chosen reporting framework. **Financial accounting** primarily produces general-purpose information for users such as investors, lenders and other creditors. **Management accounting** supports internal planning, control and decisions. **National accounting** measures economic activity for an economy using statistical concepts and boundaries; it is not simply the sum of company financial statements.

Accounting information is purpose-built. Recognition rules decide when an item enters the statements; measurement rules determine its amount; classification and presentation affect where it appears; and disclosure provides additional context. Different frameworks may treat particular items differently.

Accounting statements can provide strong evidence about reported position and performance, but they do not automatically reveal every economic resource, future opportunity, externality, strategic dependency, informal transaction or distributional consequence. Read figures with the entity boundary, period, accounting policy, audit status and disclosures in mind.

## 3. The three core financial statements

The three commonly discussed statements answer different questions:

- **Balance sheet / statement of financial position:** what assets, liabilities and equity are reported at a specific date.
- **Income statement / statement of profit or loss:** what income and expenses are recognized over a reporting period, resulting in profit or loss under the applicable framework.
- **Cash-flow statement:** how cash and cash equivalents changed over a period, classified under the reporting rules, commonly into operating, investing and financing activities.

They are related but not interchangeable. An entity can report profit while using cash, or report a loss while generating cash during the period. A balance sheet is a snapshot at a date; the other two statements cover activity over a period.

Other reports and notes may include changes in equity, comprehensive income, accounting policies, segment information, commitments, contingencies, related parties, risks and subsequent events. A single headline number rarely provides a complete view.

## 4. The accounting equation

A basic balance-sheet relationship is:

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

**Assets** are resources meeting the applicable recognition definition; **liabilities** are present obligations meeting the relevant definition; **equity** is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets under the framework.

The equation is an accounting identity, not a claim that all assets can be sold for their recorded amounts or that the entity has enough cash to pay its obligations today. A balance sheet can balance while the entity faces a severe liquidity problem, uncertain asset values, concentrated risk or future losses.

The terms are defined by accounting rules and the entity's reporting boundary. For example, an internally developed capability may have economic importance but not qualify for recognition as an asset under a particular standard. Conversely, an asset's recorded amount may depend on cost, impairment, fair-value or other measurement rules.

## 5. Assets, liabilities and equity

An **asset** is not simply anything useful or valuable in everyday language. Recognition typically depends on the reporting framework's definition, control or rights, expected economic benefits or service potential, and measurement requirements.

A **liability** is not just an expense or a future intention to spend. It concerns a present obligation under the applicable framework. Whether a commitment, provision, guarantee or contingency is recognized as a liability depends on the relevant facts and standards.

**Equity** is the residual claim represented in the accounting equation. It can include contributed capital, retained earnings and other components. Equity is not the same as cash in the bank, and a positive book-equity balance does not guarantee liquidity or a market sale price equal to book value.

Assets may be current or non-current under common presentation frameworks. Liabilities may be current or non-current. These classifications help readers understand expected realization or settlement horizons but do not, alone, establish actual liquidity, refinancing access or the timing of every cash movement.

## 6. Asset categories and measurement bases

Common asset categories include cash and cash equivalents, receivables, inventories, property and equipment, intangible assets, investments, and certain contractual rights. Exact recognition and classification depend on the entity and standard.

A recorded asset amount may be based on historical cost, amortized cost, fair value, revaluation, net realizable value or another prescribed basis. These bases answer different questions and apply under specific conditions.

- **Historical cost** generally starts from the transaction cost and is adjusted under the applicable rules.
- **Fair value** is a defined market-participant-based measurement under relevant standards, not necessarily the price this particular holder could obtain immediately for its entire position.
- **Amortized cost** applies a measurement method that incorporates relevant repayments, fees, premiums/discounts and effective interest where applicable.
- **Impairment** recognizes a reduction under the applicable test when the required conditions are met.
- **Depreciation** allocates the depreciable amount of a tangible asset over its useful life under the chosen method.
- **Amortization** commonly allocates the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life when the applicable criteria are met.

Accounting depreciation or amortization is not the same as market-price decline, physical deterioration, maintenance spending or the national-accounts measure of consumption of fixed capital. Related concepts may be connected, but they use different definitions and purposes.

## 7. Liabilities, debt, provisions and contingencies

**Debt** commonly refers to an obligation to repay borrowed funds or another debt instrument under contractual terms. **Liabilities** is a broader accounting category that can include trade payables, lease obligations, provisions, tax payables, employee obligations and other recognized obligations, not just interest-bearing borrowing.

A **provision** is a liability of uncertain timing or amount recognized when the relevant accounting criteria are met. A **contingent liability** is treated under specific disclosure or recognition rules; it is not automatically a recognized balance-sheet liability. A guarantee or legal claim needs analysis under the applicable standard and facts.

A **commitment** can refer to a contractual or other commitment for future action or spending. Not every commitment is a recognized liability at the reporting date. Commitments and contingencies may still matter for future cash requirements and risk even if they are not presented as recognized liabilities.

Do not add debt, payables, provisions, guarantees and all commitments together without checking definitions and overlap. A broad exposure inventory can be useful, but its categories should remain explicit.

## 8. Equity, share capital, retained earnings and net worth

For a company, **share capital** or contributed capital reflects amounts recognized from owners under the relevant legal and accounting framework. **Retained earnings** generally accumulate recognized profits and losses not distributed or otherwise reclassified under the applicable rules. Equity can also include reserves and other components.

**Book equity** is the reported residual under accounting measurements. **Market capitalization** is commonly a share price multiplied by a defined number of outstanding shares. Market capitalization can be above or below book equity and can change as market prices move, without an equivalent change in cash held by the company.

For households, **net worth** commonly means the value of assets less liabilities under the chosen measurement approach. For an organization, people may use “net worth” informally to refer to net assets or equity, but the exact legal or accounting meaning depends on the context.

Equity is not a cash reserve. A company can have positive equity and insufficient cash to meet near-term obligations; it can also have substantial cash while its total liabilities exceed recognized assets. Liquidity, solvency, profitability and market valuation are separate analytical dimensions.

## 9. Revenue, income, expenses and profit

**Revenue** generally arises from an entity's ordinary activities under the applicable reporting framework. Recognition depends on the relevant standard and transaction facts; cash receipt is not always the recognition trigger.

**Income** can be broader than revenue in financial reporting, depending on the framework, and can include gains or other increases in economic benefits that increase equity other than owner contributions. In everyday use, “income” may refer to salary, business earnings or cash received. Always state the context.

**Expenses** are recognized decreases in economic benefits during a period, other than distributions to owners, under the relevant framework. An expense can be recognized without a contemporaneous cash payment, and cash can be paid for an item that is recognized as an asset rather than expensed immediately.

**Profit** is a residual result after recognized income and expenses under a defined measure and period. Gross profit, operating profit, profit before tax and net profit are distinct subtotals with different inclusions. They are not interchangeable; check the reporting framework and company presentation.

Profit is not the same as cash generated, cash available for distribution, economic rent, or a complete measure of social value. A profit figure is meaningful only when its period, entity boundary, accounting policies and component definitions are known.

## 10. Accrual accounting versus cash accounting

Under **accrual accounting**, transactions and events are recognized when the relevant criteria are met, rather than only when cash is received or paid. Revenue can be recognized when earned under the applicable standard before the customer pays; an expense can be recognized when incurred even if payment occurs later.

Under **cash-basis accounting**, receipts and payments are central to recognition, subject to the specific system and rules. Some organizations or reports use modified cash or hybrid approaches; do not assume all reports follow the same basis.

Accrual accounting can better match activity and obligations to the periods in which they arise, but it relies on estimates, judgments, cut-off rules and recognition policies. Cash records are essential for liquidity analysis but may not capture all earned income, unpaid obligations or non-cash events.

For example, a firm sells goods on credit. The sale may be recognized as revenue and a receivable before cash arrives. If the customer pays later, cash increases and the receivable decreases; the later receipt is not necessarily new revenue at that point. The exact accounting depends on the transaction and framework.

## 11. Profit versus cash flow

Profit and cash flow differ for several reasons:
- credit sales can generate recognized revenue before cash collection;
- purchases of inventory can use cash before the related goods are sold and expensed;
- depreciation and amortization are expenses that generally do not require a matching cash payment in the current period;
- borrowing brings in cash but is not normally revenue;
- repayment of loan principal uses cash but is not normally an expense;
- buying equipment may use cash immediately while the cost is recognized over time through depreciation;
- changes in receivables, payables and inventory can change operating cash flow.

**Operating cash flow** concerns cash flows classified as operating under the applicable framework. **Free cash flow** is a non-universal analytical measure whose definition varies; a common version subtracts capital expenditure from operating cash flow, but users must check what is included. **Cash flow** is not one number without a classification and time period.

Profit can be positive while cash is under pressure, for example when receivables grow or inventory absorbs cash. Cash can rise because of borrowing or an asset sale even when the entity is unprofitable. For a sound assessment, examine profit, operating cash flow, working capital, debt service, capital spending and financing together.

## 12. Income statement: recognition over a period

The income statement reports recognized income and expenses over a period. Common lines may include revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, operating profit, finance costs, tax and profit for the period. Presentation and terminology vary.

**Gross profit** is typically revenue less cost of sales under the entity's presentation. **Operating profit** reflects the specified operating items, but definitions and subtotals can vary. **EBIT** usually means earnings before interest and tax, though the exact calculation should be stated. **EBITDA** usually means earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization; it is a non-IFRS/non-universal subtotal unless defined by the applicable framework or regulation.

EBITDA is not cash flow: it typically does not deduct working-capital investment, capital expenditure, cash taxes, interest or debt principal. Adjusted profit measures can exclude items according to management-defined rules; their usefulness depends on transparent reconciliation and consistent definitions.

An income statement does not show the full stock of assets and liabilities or the timing of cash receipts and payments. It must be read with the balance sheet, cash-flow statement and notes.

## 13. Balance sheet: position at a date

The balance sheet reports recognized assets, liabilities and equity at a specific reporting date. It does not directly report all future cash inflows/outflows, the market sale value of every asset, or every risk that could arise after that date.

**Working capital** has multiple uses. A common accounting definition is current assets minus current liabilities. Some analysts use a narrower operating working-capital definition that excludes cash, debt or other items. The definition must be explicit before comparing entities or calculating changes.

**Current ratio** commonly means current assets divided by current liabilities. **Quick ratio** commonly uses a narrower set of more liquid current assets over current liabilities. These ratios can be informative, but inventory quality, receivable collectability, liability timing, restricted cash and access to financing affect interpretation. A ratio above one does not guarantee payment capacity; a ratio below one does not by itself prove failure.

Balance-sheet analysis should consider asset quality, liquidity, maturity, concentration, encumbrances, off-balance-sheet exposures, related parties, valuation uncertainty and subsequent events. Accounting net assets alone are not a full solvency stress test.

## 14. Cash-flow statement: movement over a period

A cash-flow statement reconciles opening and closing cash and cash equivalents through classified cash movements under the relevant framework. The main categories are commonly:

- **Operating activities:** principal revenue-producing activities and other activities not classified as investing or financing.
- **Investing activities:** acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments, subject to the applicable definitions.
- **Financing activities:** changes in the size and composition of contributed equity and borrowings under the relevant rules.

Classification of particular interest, dividends and other flows can vary by standard and entity type. Comparisons should check the framework and policy used.

A positive total cash change does not necessarily mean the core business generated cash; it may reflect borrowing, equity issuance or asset disposals. Negative cash flow is not automatically bad if it reflects planned investment funded sustainably, but it can be dangerous when recurring operations cannot meet obligations.

Always separate cash generated by operations from cash obtained through financing and cash used for investment. Then evaluate timing, restrictions, debt service and future commitments.

## 15. Depreciation, amortization, impairment and capital expenditure

**Capital expenditure (CapEx)** commonly means spending to acquire, construct or improve long-lived assets, subject to the entity's definition and accounting policies. Not every payment for a durable item is capitalized; thresholds and recognition rules matter.

**Operating expenditure (OpEx)** generally refers to costs treated as operating expenses in a defined management or reporting context. The boundary between capitalized and expensed spending depends on standards, policy and the nature of the expenditure. Labels should not be treated as universal economic categories.

**Depreciation** allocates a tangible asset's depreciable amount over its estimated useful life. **Amortization** allocates the amount of an eligible intangible asset over its useful life. **Impairment** recognizes a reduction when the applicable recoverability or measurement criteria require it. These measures have distinct triggers and mechanics.

Maintenance spending may preserve an asset's operating condition, while capital expenditure can expand, replace or improve capacity. The accounting treatment of maintenance and improvements depends on the work performed and applicable standards. Book depreciation may differ from economic depreciation, actual wear, replacement cost and national-accounts consumption of fixed capital.

Do not infer that a company invested little merely because depreciation expense is low, or that it expanded productive capacity just because CapEx was high. Examine asset additions, disposals, replacement needs, utilization, quality, useful lives and what the spending achieved.

## 16. Gross versus net; book value versus market value

**Gross** generally means measured before a specified deduction; **net** means after that specified deduction. The deduction must be named. Gross profit versus net profit, gross debt versus net debt, gross assets versus net assets and gross fixed capital formation versus net capital formation all involve different rules.

**Net debt** is not one universal figure. A common analytical calculation subtracts selected cash and cash equivalents from selected debt, but restricted cash, leases, derivatives, pensions and other obligations may be treated differently. Always state included and excluded components.

**Book value** is the amount reported under accounting rules. **Market value** depends on a market price or valuation methodology at a specified date. A traded share price may be observable, while some assets have no active market and require models or estimates. Fair value under an accounting standard is a specific measurement concept and should not casually be equated with every use of “market value.”

A company with market capitalization above book equity is not automatically overvalued, and one below book equity is not automatically undervalued. Differences can reflect expected profitability, intangible resources not recognized as assets, risk, accounting policy, market sentiment, distress or measurement limitations. A conclusion requires further analysis.

## 17. Entity boundary, consolidation and related parties

A **reporting entity** is the unit for which financial information is presented. It can be an individual legal entity, a parent company and its controlled subsidiaries on a consolidated basis, or another defined reporting unit under the framework.

**Separate financial statements** report a parent or investor entity under the applicable separate-accounting rules. **Consolidated financial statements** present a parent and controlled subsidiaries as a single economic group for reporting purposes under the relevant standards, eliminating specified intra-group balances and transactions.

Consolidation matters because an intra-group loan or sale can appear in each entity's separate accounts but is eliminated in consolidation under the relevant rules. Summing separate-company revenue, debt or assets without eliminating internal transactions can double-count activity or exposures.

**Related parties** can include entities or people with specified control, joint control, significant influence, key management or other relationships under the framework. Related-party transactions are not necessarily improper, but their terms, disclosures and economic substance may matter for understanding risk and incentives.

When tracing ownership or financing, distinguish legal entity, parent, subsidiary, beneficial owner, joint venture, associate, branch and reporting group. A brand name, business group label and legal reporting entity may not be the same thing.

## 18. Audit, estimates, restatements and accounting quality

An **audit opinion** reports an auditor's conclusion under the applicable auditing standards and scope. An unmodified opinion does not guarantee that an entity is financially healthy, fraud-free, liquid or certain to survive. A qualified, adverse or disclaimer opinion has a specific meaning and should be read with the report's basis and context.

Financial statements contain estimates and judgments, such as expected credit losses, useful lives, impairment assumptions, provisions and valuation inputs. Estimates can be reasonable while uncertain, and subsequent information can lead to revisions.

**Restatement** or correction can change previously reported figures because of errors or other requirements under the relevant rules. Changes in accounting policies, estimates, entity boundaries, classifications and presentation can also affect comparability. A change in a reported metric may reflect changed economics, changed estimates, changed standards or revised data.

Accounting quality assessment should examine audit status, timeliness, internal consistency, cash conversion, receivable and inventory trends, related-party disclosures, unusual adjustments, policy changes, debt covenants and notes. No single ratio or audit label substitutes for a full review.

## 18.1 Working capital, operating cycle and cash conversion

**Working capital** is often defined as current assets minus current liabilities, but analysts may use an operating definition that excludes cash, interest-bearing debt or other items. Always state the definition. A positive working-capital balance does not guarantee that obligations can be paid when due, because inventory may be slow-moving, receivables may be uncollectible, and cash may be restricted.

The **operating cycle** is the time required to acquire inputs, hold or process inventory, sell goods or services, and collect the resulting receivables. The **cash-conversion cycle** commonly measures the time between paying suppliers and collecting cash from customers, using inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days under a defined method. Sector, seasonality, payment terms and calculation choices affect comparability.

A business can report a profit while experiencing a cash squeeze if sales are made on credit, inventory accumulates, suppliers demand earlier payment, or growth requires cash before customers pay. Extending supplier-payment periods may temporarily support cash but can damage supplier relationships or expose the business to lost discounts, penalties or supply interruption.

**Cash-conversion cycle → timing gap between cash paid out and cash collected → need for working-capital finance or operating adjustment.**

In plain language, a business may have sold goods profitably but not yet collected the money, while it still needs to pay wages, rent or suppliers. The timing gap—not necessarily a lack of accounting profit—can create a need for cash reserves, credit or changes to purchasing, inventory and customer-payment terms.

## 18.2 Accounting profit, taxable profit and cash generated

**Accounting profit** is calculated under the financial-reporting framework and policies used for the statements. **Taxable profit** is calculated under the relevant tax law, including tax-specific adjustments, exemptions, disallowances, depreciation/capital-allowance rules, loss carryforwards and timing differences. The two figures can differ without either being a mistake.

**Cash generated** needs a defined boundary. Cash flow from operations is not the same as profit, free cash flow, cash available to owners, or the change in total cash. Financing inflows, asset sales, capital expenditure, debt repayment, dividends, tax payments, restricted balances and working-capital movements all affect cash availability.

A reconciliation should identify the measure and period, then explain material differences rather than assume one can be substituted for another. For example, a non-cash expense can reduce accounting profit without a current cash payment; capital expenditure may use cash but be recognized over time through depreciation; borrowing can increase cash without increasing profit; and tax adjustments can make taxable profit differ from accounting profit.

## 18.3 Off-balance-sheet exposures and contingent obligations

An exposure not shown as a recognized liability on the face of a balance sheet is not necessarily economically irrelevant. Depending on the reporting rules and facts, guarantees, letters of credit, commitments, certain contractual arrangements, securitization structures, leases, derivatives, litigation and support for related entities may appear in notes, separate schedules or other disclosures rather than as a simple headline debt figure. Some arrangements do create recognized assets or liabilities under the applicable standards; the term “off-balance-sheet” should not be used as a blanket classification.

**Disclosed or possible exposure → triggering event or contractual condition → potential cash or resource obligation.**

In plain language, a company may not currently owe the full amount of a guarantee, but it could have to pay if the guaranteed borrower defaults. The relevant analysis therefore asks what event activates the obligation, who is legally responsible, how likely and large the cash impact could be, whether collateral or recourse exists, and whether the exposure overlaps with amounts already recognized elsewhere.

Do not add all guarantees, commitments, derivatives, leases and debt together without examining their definitions, measurement bases, probability, overlap and reporting boundaries. An exposure inventory is useful only when its categories and potential triggers remain explicit.

## 19. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Asset vs expense | An asset is a recognized resource/claim; an expense is a recognized decrease in economic benefits during a period. |
| Liability vs expense | A liability is an obligation at a date; an expense is a period flow. A transaction can create both at different stages. |
| Equity vs cash | Equity is a residual accounting interest, not a cash balance. |
| Revenue vs receipts | Revenue recognition and cash receipt can occur at different times. |
| Revenue vs income | Revenue commonly arises from ordinary activities; income may be broader under reporting rules and has everyday meanings too. |
| Profit vs cash flow | Profit is an accrual-based residual; cash flow records cash movements under defined categories. |
| Profit vs revenue | Revenue is a top-line measure; profit deducts recognized expenses under a stated measure. |
| Accrual vs cash basis | Accrual recognition follows relevant events/criteria; cash basis focuses on receipts and payments. |
| Debt vs liabilities | Debt is a narrower category; liabilities can include many obligations beyond borrowing. |
| Commitment vs recognized liability | Some commitments matter economically without meeting recognition criteria for a balance-sheet liability. |
| Depreciation vs impairment | Depreciation allocates depreciable amount over useful life; impairment recognizes a reduction under specified tests. |
| Depreciation vs market-value decline | Accounting allocation is not the same as a market-price movement. |
| CapEx vs cash paid | CapEx is a defined spending category; timing and accounting treatment can differ. |
| EBITDA vs cash flow | EBITDA generally omits working capital, capital spending, interest, taxes and principal repayments. |
| Working capital vs cash | Working capital often includes receivables and inventory and subtracts current liabilities; it is not cash alone. |
| Book equity vs market capitalization | Accounting residual differs from market valuation of outstanding shares. |
| Gross vs net | Net means after specified deductions; the deductions differ by measure. |
| Separate vs consolidated accounts | Consolidation includes a defined group and eliminates specified intra-group balances and transactions. |
| Audit opinion vs financial health | An audit conclusion has a defined scope and does not guarantee solvency or future performance. |
| Accounting profit vs economic welfare | Accounting profit does not capture every externality, distributional outcome or social value. |
| Accounting accounts vs national accounts | Entity reporting and economy-wide statistical accounting use different boundaries, purposes and rules. |

## 20. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before interpreting a financial statement or accounting claim, ask:

1. Which legal entity or consolidated group is covered?
2. What is the reporting date and period, and is the statement audited or interim?
3. Which accounting framework, policies, currency and units are used?
4. Is the number a stock at a date or a flow over a period?
5. Is the item recognized, disclosed as a commitment/contingency, or only discussed outside the statements?
6. What are the definitions of revenue, operating profit, net profit, EBITDA, net debt and working capital in this report?
7. Are cash receipts being confused with revenue, or cash payments with expenses?
8. Are debt, payables, provisions, leases, guarantees and commitments being counted consistently without overlap?
9. What asset measurement basis applies—cost, amortized cost, fair value, impairment or another method?
10. Are depreciation, amortization and impairment being interpreted correctly?
11. What drove cash changes: operations, investing, financing, foreign exchange or other effects?
12. Is profit converting into operating cash over time, and what explains any gap?
13. Are receivables collectible, inventory saleable, and cash unrestricted?
14. Are debt maturities, interest, covenants, refinancing and contingent obligations visible?
15. Are related-party and intra-group transactions identified and eliminated where appropriate?
16. Have accounting policies, estimates, classifications or reporting boundaries changed?
17. What does the audit opinion actually say, and what does it not guarantee?
18. Is an accounting metric being used to infer economic value, productivity, welfare or intent without additional evidence?

## 22. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- The balance sheet is a position at a date; income and cash-flow statements describe activity over a period.
- Profit and cash flow differ because of accrual recognition, non-cash items, working capital, investing and financing transactions.
- Assets, liabilities and equity have defined accounting meanings that may differ from everyday use.
- Debt is not identical to all liabilities, and commitments are not automatically recognized liabilities.
- Consolidated statements eliminate specified intra-group items and should not be confused with simple sums of separate-entity accounts.
- Accounting values depend on recognition and measurement rules; they do not automatically equal market sale values or broader social value.

### Scope limits
- No current company financial data or Nigerian accounting figures are asserted.
- The note does not replace the applicable accounting standard, legal advice, audit procedures or professional judgment.
- Ratios and non-standard subtotals require source-specific definitions before comparison.
- Financial statements provide structured evidence but do not, by themselves, establish fraud, intent, welfare, resilience or future performance.

- What evidence distinguishes a change in economic performance from a change in accounting policy, estimate or reporting boundary?

## 23. Short summary

Accounting statements provide complementary views of financial position, recognized performance and cash movements. Assets are not expenses, liabilities are not costs, equity is not cash, and profit is not cash flow. A balance sheet describes a position at a date; income and cash-flow statements cover a period. Reported figures depend on the accounting framework, entity boundary and measurement basis. Consolidated accounts eliminate specified intra-group items, while accounting profit and book value should not be treated as complete measures of economic welfare or market value.

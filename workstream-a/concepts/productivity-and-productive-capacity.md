# Concept 7 — Productivity and Productive Capacity

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress

**Previous concepts:** [Production](production.md); [Output and Intermediate Inputs](output-and-intermediate-inputs.md); [Economic Value, Usefulness and Exchange Value](economic-value-usefulness-and-exchange-value.md); [Costs and Prices](costs-and-prices.md); [Revenue, Income and Receipts](revenue-income-and-receipts.md); [Value Added, Income Distribution and Profit](value-added-income-distribution-and-profit.md)  
**Purpose:** Explain productivity as a relationship between outputs and inputs, distinguish it from total production and effort, compare major productivity measures, and clarify the difference between current productivity and productive capacity.

## 1. The central idea

**Productivity is a ratio between a defined measure of output and a defined measure of input.** It asks how much output is produced per unit of input. The meaning of the ratio depends on what counts as output, which inputs are included, how quality and prices are treated, and the period and unit of analysis.

A production increase is not automatically a productivity increase. If a bakery produces more bread only because workers work more hours, total output rises but output per hour may stay unchanged. Productivity can rise when output increases with the same input, or when the same output is produced with less input.

Productivity is not a direct measure of how hard a person works, how valuable a product is to customers, whether a business is profitable, or whether its production is environmentally sustainable. These are related but distinct questions.

## 2. Basic formulas

For a specified period and consistent definitions:

**Labour productivity (per hour) = output measure ÷ hours worked**

**Labour productivity (per worker) = output measure ÷ number of workers**

**Capital productivity = output measure ÷ capital input measure**

**Multifactor productivity (MFP) = output measure ÷ combined input measure**, in a simplified ratio representation.

Formal productivity statistics often use volume indexes and index-number methods rather than simply dividing nominal currency totals. Multifactor productivity is commonly estimated through a growth-accounting framework that relates output growth to measured changes in labour and capital inputs; the remaining component is often called the MFP residual. Its interpretation depends on the model and data.

For business operations, output may be physical units, completed jobs, deliveries or service cases. For industry and economy-wide analysis, output is often measured using real gross value added (GVA) or another volume measure. These choices are not interchangeable.

## 3. Productivity versus production and output

- **Production** is the activity of creating goods or services.
- **Output** is the resulting quantity or value of goods and services under a defined boundary.
- **Productivity** relates output to input.
- **Productivity growth** is a change in that ratio over time.
- **Productive capacity** is the amount an entity or system can sustainably produce under stated resources, technology, organisation and operating conditions.
- **Capacity utilisation** is how much of available capacity is being used over a period, under a stated definition.

A factory may produce more because it added a second shift, installed more machinery, or improved its process. Only the last possibility clearly suggests a process improvement, and even then the measured productivity result depends on all relevant inputs. More shifts or machinery can increase output while productivity per worker-hour remains unchanged or even falls.

Capacity is not the same as actual output. A shop may have space and staff to serve 100 customers daily but serve only 60 because demand is low, stock is unavailable, or power and transport disruptions constrain operations.

## 4. Worked examples

### Example A — More output, unchanged labour productivity

A worker produces 40 items in 8 hours.

- Output per hour = 40 ÷ 8 = 5 items per hour.

Next day the worker produces 50 items in 10 hours.

- Output per hour = 50 ÷ 10 = 5 items per hour.

Total output increased by 25%, but measured labour productivity did not change. These hypothetical figures do not account for quality, defects, setup time, equipment or other inputs.

### Example B — Same output, higher labour productivity

A team completes 80 orders in 16 labour-hours: 5 orders per labour-hour. After a process change, it completes 80 comparable orders in 12 labour-hours: about 6.67 orders per labour-hour.

Measured output per labour-hour rises by about 33.3%. To interpret this as a meaningful improvement, the orders must be comparable in complexity and quality, and the analysis should check whether additional unmeasured inputs, overtime, outsourcing, software, energy or rework changed.

### Example C — Labour productivity rises because more capital is used

A farm worker harvests 2 hectares in a day using hand tools and 4 hectares in a day using a tractor, with the same recorded labour time. Measured labour productivity rises. But the result is not solely a change in the worker's skill or effort: capital services, fuel, maintenance, land conditions and other inputs also matter. A multifactor measure would account for more of these inputs, depending on its method.

### Example D — Output rises while productivity falls

A service team handles 100 cases in 100 labour-hours (1 case per hour). It later handles 120 cases in 140 labour-hours (about 0.86 cases per hour). Output rises by 20%, while cases per hour falls by about 14.3%. Case complexity or quality may also have changed, so the raw ratio is only a first signal.

All examples are illustrative, not empirical findings about any real worker, farm or business.

## 5. Major productivity measures

| Measure | What it relates | What it can help answer | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labour productivity per hour | Output to hours worked | How much measured output is generated per hour? | Also reflects capital, skills, materials, technology, organisation and other inputs |
| Labour productivity per worker | Output to worker count | How much output is generated per worker? | Does not account for part-time/full-time differences or hours worked |
| Capital productivity | Output to a measure of capital input or capital services | How much output is generated relative to capital input? | Depends on capital measurement, asset composition, utilisation and quality |
| Multifactor productivity (MFP) | Output to combined measured inputs, or output growth net of measured input contributions | How has output changed beyond measured labour and capital input changes? | Sensitive to methods, data quality, input quality and omitted factors; residual is not automatically pure technology |
| Unit labour cost | Labour compensation per unit of output, often using labour cost per hour divided by output per hour | How is labour cost per unit of output changing? | Not itself a productivity measure; also depends on compensation and output-price definitions |
| Public-service productivity | Defined public-service output or outcomes relative to inputs | What service output is produced from public resources? | Outputs and quality/outcomes may be hard to measure where market prices are absent |

The same term may have variants across statistical agencies. For a formal indicator, specify the numerator, denominator, coverage, unit, price/volume basis, seasonal adjustment, source and revision policy.

## 6. Why labour productivity is not the same as worker effort

Labour productivity is a partial measure: it divides output by labour input but does not isolate the worker's own contribution. A worker can appear more productive because the worker has better machinery, more reliable electricity, better materials, improved software, a stronger supply chain, better management or higher demand. A worker can appear less productive when equipment breaks down or inputs arrive late, even if their effort and skills have not changed.

Likewise, a simple output-per-hour measure may count each hour equally even when skills, experience, task complexity or output quality differ. Some advanced measures adjust for labour composition; ordinary ratios often do not.

Do not use productivity figures alone to conclude that an individual is lazy, diligent, efficient or inefficient. Such judgments require much more context and a fair comparison of comparable work.

## 7. Productivity, quality and measurement boundaries

A count of units can create misleading incentives if quality falls. A clinic that records more consultations per hour may not necessarily deliver better health outcomes; a support team closing more tickets may be reopening cases later; a manufacturer producing more units may also produce more defects or waste.

Possible quality adjustments include defect rates, durability, service outcomes, customer satisfaction or task complexity, but each needs a defensible and consistent method. These measures should be reported alongside the output/input ratio rather than silently combined unless the aggregation method is justified.

Boundary choices matter:
- Is output measured as physical quantity, real value added, revenue, or completed tasks?
- Are unpaid family workers and owner-hours included?
- Are outsourced inputs and contractors counted?
- Is the input hours actually worked, paid hours, worker count, or full-time-equivalent employment?
- Are rework, waste, defects and downtime included?
- Are output prices adjusted for inflation and quality?
- Are public or non-market services valued through cost-based estimates or other methods?

Comparisons are meaningful only when the chosen definitions are sufficiently consistent.

## 8. Productivity and productive capacity

**Productivity** describes an observed relationship between output and input. **Productive capacity** describes what can be produced under stated conditions. Capacity depends on available labour, skills, equipment, infrastructure, energy, materials, technology, management, logistics, regulation and the time horizon.

A business may have high potential capacity but low actual production because demand is weak or inputs are unavailable. Another may operate close to capacity but be unable to meet additional demand without investment. A productivity improvement can raise capacity from existing resources, but capacity can also increase by adding more resources even if measured productivity does not rise.

Capacity estimates are conditional, not timeless facts. They should specify the operating schedule, staffing, maintenance, input availability, quality requirements and acceptable downtime.

## 9. What can drive productivity changes?

Potential contributors include:
- better technology and equipment;
- skills, training and learning-by-doing;
- improved organisation, management and workflow;
- reliable electricity, transport, communications and other infrastructure;
- improved access to materials, finance and information;
- specialisation, scale and learning effects;
- better matching of workers, capital and tasks;
- process redesign and reduced defects, waiting or rework;
- changes in capital intensity and the mix of products or services;
- shifts in demand and capacity utilisation.

These are possible mechanisms, not automatic explanations. A productivity ratio alone does not identify which factor caused a change. Causal claims require additional evidence, comparisons and attention to alternative explanations.

## 10. Productivity, costs, prices and profit

Higher productivity can reduce resource use per unit, increase output from existing resources, or create room for higher wages, lower prices or larger margins. But these outcomes are not guaranteed.

For example:
- A firm may keep prices unchanged and earn a higher margin if unit costs fall and other conditions remain stable.
- Competition may lead the firm to pass cost reductions to customers through lower prices.
- Higher wages or input costs may offset productivity gains.
- A firm may produce more efficiently but still lose money if demand is insufficient or selling prices do not cover relevant costs.
- A productivity increase achieved by sacrificing quality, worker safety or environmental conditions may not represent an overall improvement.

Productivity is one input to business performance, not a substitute for analyzing costs, prices, demand, cash flow, profit, quality and external effects.

## 11. Economy-wide productivity and living standards

Over long periods, sustained productivity growth is an important source of growth in real output and can support higher real incomes and living standards. However, the distribution of gains depends on institutions, bargaining power, market structure, public policy, ownership and other conditions. Productivity growth does not guarantee that every worker's wages rise or that gains are evenly distributed.

GDP per hour worked is a common economy-wide labour-productivity indicator. GDP per capita is different: it is affected both by productivity and by how much labour is used relative to population, among other factors. Productivity should not be confused with total GDP growth, employment growth, or welfare as a whole.

## 12. Productivity measurement and inference checklist

Before interpreting a productivity figure, ask:

1. What is the unit of analysis: person, team, firm, industry, public service or national economy?
2. What output measure is used, and does it capture quantity, quality and the relevant production boundary?
3. What input is measured: hours, workers, capital services, materials, energy or a combined index?
4. Is output expressed in real/volume terms where price changes would otherwise distort the comparison?
5. Are the periods and units comparable?
6. Are worker hours actually worked or merely paid/contracted hours?
7. Did the mix of products, clients, tasks, skills or capital change?
8. Did outsourcing or other unmeasured inputs increase?
9. Are results revised, uncertain, seasonal or affected by data gaps?
10. Is the claim merely descriptive, or does it claim a cause? What additional evidence supports that causal explanation?
11. Are quality, safety, environmental effects and distributional consequences relevant?
12. Is the ratio being used to evaluate a process, a person, a business or a whole economy? These require different interpretations.

## 13. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Production and output:** Productivity relates output to inputs; it is not the same as production volume.
- **Costs and prices:** Productivity changes can affect unit costs but do not determine market prices on their own.
- **Revenue, income and receipts:** Revenue can rise due to higher prices or sales volume without any productivity improvement.
- **Value added and profit:** Economy-wide labour productivity is often measured using real GVA or GDP per hour. Business profit is not the same as the output numerator.
- **Capital formation:** Investment can increase capital services and measured labour productivity; its effects should be distinguished from changes in multifactor productivity.
- **Workstream B:** A formal productivity indicator needs defined output and input series, unit, price basis, coverage, revisions and methodology.
- **Workstream C:** A productivity trend can be monitored, but a short-run fluctuation should not automatically be interpreted as a structural change.
- **Workstream D:** Operational traces such as units completed, hours worked, machine downtime and defect rates may help investigate a process, but they do not prove a cause without comparison and triangulation.
- **Workstream E:** Productivity analysis can reveal a potential business problem or opportunity, but a ratio alone does not prove customer demand or commercial viability.

## 14. Evidence status and limitations

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Productivity is an output-to-input relationship, not the same as total output.
- Labour productivity is a partial measure and is influenced by non-labour inputs.
- MFP depends on the measured input framework; its residual is not automatically a pure measure of technology.
- Output per hour and output per worker are different measures.
- Productivity statistics depend on definitions, quality, price/volume treatment and data quality.
- Productivity growth can support higher real incomes over time, but distribution of the gains is not automatic.

### Illustrative only
- All numerical examples are hypothetical.
- The examples do not estimate Nigerian firm or national productivity.
- No productivity trend or causal driver is calculated in this note.

## 15. Short summary

Productivity is output per unit of input. Output can rise without productivity rising, and productivity can improve without total output increasing. Labour productivity depends on more than worker effort because capital, materials, technology, management, infrastructure, quality and demand also matter. Productive capacity is potential output under stated conditions, not the same as observed production. Interpret every measure through its definitions, time period, data quality and decision purpose.

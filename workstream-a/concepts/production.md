# Concept 1 — Production

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-26  
**Purpose:** Build a reality-first understanding of production before formalizing related measurements such as real GDP and labour productivity.

## 1. Plain-language meaning

Production is the activity through which people and organizations create goods or provide services that satisfy needs or wants. It can involve growing, making, processing, maintaining, storing, transporting, organizing, or providing information and other services.

Production does not always mean creating a new physical object. A transporter can produce a transport service by moving a good to another place. A warehouse can provide a storage service that makes a good available later and may help preserve its quality. An information provider can produce a service by gathering, checking, comparing, and interpreting information for a decision-maker.

This broad, everyday description must be distinguished from the more specific **production boundary used in national accounting to calculate GDP**. Not every useful human activity is counted in GDP in the same way.

## 2. Start with observable examples

### 2.1 A farmer grows maize

A farmer uses inputs such as seed, land, labour, tools, water, and other resources to grow maize. The harvested maize is agricultural output.

### 2.2 A parent cooks a meal at home

The parent turns ingredients and effort into a meal that provides a useful service to the household. The activity is useful and can be understood as household service production in an everyday sense. However, most unpaid household services—such as cooking, cleaning, and caring for family members—are generally outside the GDP production boundary.

### 2.3 A restaurant prepares a similar meal for sale

The restaurant provides a meal as a market service. Its market production is generally included in GDP. Although the meal may be similar to the one prepared at home, the national accounting treatment differs.

**Lesson:** Whether an activity is useful, whether it is production in a broad sense, and whether its output is included in GDP are different questions.

There are exceptions to a simple paid-versus-unpaid rule. For example, national accounts impute a housing service for owner-occupied dwellings, and certain goods produced for a household's own use may be included. Payment is not the universal test for GDP inclusion.

## 3. Production can occur through services

Consider a trader who buys maize at a farm and sells it in a town.

- **The farmer** produces maize.
- **The transporter** provides a service by moving the maize.
- **The warehouse operator** provides storage that can make maize available over time and help preserve it.
- **A market-information provider** may collect and verify prices, compare selling locations, estimate relevant costs, and provide decision support.
- **The trader** connects supply with buyers and may provide distribution and other services. The accounting treatment of trading output is not the same as counting the full resale price of the maize as new production by the trader.

Services can contribute to economic activity without creating a new physical object. Transport changes location and access; storage supports availability over time; information can reduce uncertainty and support decisions.

A service being performed does not, by itself, prove that it was profitable, that it benefited the customer as intended, or that its charge equals the economic value it added.

## 4. Worked example: maize transport

Use these hypothetical figures:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Trader's purchase price for maize | ₦500,000 |
| Transport charge | ₦100,000 |
| Trader's resale price | ₦750,000 |
| Difference between resale and purchase prices | ₦250,000 |
| Difference after subtracting the stated transport charge | ₦150,000 |

These figures illustrate distinctions; they are not claims about current market prices.

### What can be concluded?

1. The trader paid ₦100,000 for a transport service.
2. The maize was sold for ₦250,000 more than its purchase price.
3. Subtracting the stated transport charge leaves ₦150,000 before any other costs, assuming the figures are comparable and ignoring other items.
4. We cannot conclude from these figures alone that the trader made ₦150,000 profit, that the trader's value added was ₦250,000, or that the transport company created exactly ₦100,000 of value added.

Other possible trader costs include loading, unloading, storage, market fees, labour, financing, spoilage, and losses. The transporter also has costs and may have wages, depreciation, financing, and other expenses.

### Payment, output, value added, and profit are different

- **Transaction price/payment:** What was paid for the service or good.
- **Output/revenue:** The value of goods or services produced or sold, depending on the context and accounting definition.
- **Intermediate consumption:** Goods and services used up in producing output.
- **Value added:** In a simplified production-accounting expression, output value minus intermediate consumption.
- **Profit:** A business result after relevant expenses; its exact definition depends on which costs and accounting measure are being used.

For a simplified transport-service illustration, if output for the journey is ₦100,000 and intermediate consumption is assumed to be ₦40,000, value added would be ₦60,000. This is only an illustrative calculation under the stated assumption; it is not necessarily the transport company's profit. Labour compensation, depreciation, and other items affect how value added is distributed and how profit is calculated.

The price difference between a good's purchase and resale prices does not automatically equal profit or value added. National accounting records trade margins and related services under its rules; it does not simply count the full resale value of the same good as newly produced output each time it changes hands.

## 5. Production, usefulness, and market outcomes

A transporter may complete a journey even if the trader later cannot sell the maize. The transport service has still been performed. The trader's commercial result is a separate question.

Keep these ideas distinct:

- **Activity performed:** What work or service took place?
- **Usefulness:** Did it benefit someone or meet a need?
- **Market price:** What price was charged or paid?
- **Business outcome:** Did the provider or customer make or lose money?
- **Measured value added:** What does the applicable accounting method attribute to the productive activity?

These may be related, but they are not interchangeable. A market price is evidence of a transaction, not a complete measure of usefulness or value added.

## 6. Information as a service

Imagine a provider checks maize prices across five markets. Instead of only reporting one price, the provider verifies the observations, compares markets, estimates transport and market costs, notes how recent the data are, and explains uncertainty.

The provider may be supplying more than raw data: time saved, verified information, analysis, reduced uncertainty, and decision support.

A trader might pay for this service even if some prices are publicly available, because collecting, checking, and interpreting information takes time and effort. But payment is not guaranteed. The service must be accurate, timely, relevant, and useful enough to justify its cost. It cannot guarantee a profit because prices, demand, costs, and market access may change.

The economic value to the customer is distinct from value added in national accounts. Customer benefit can be greater or smaller than the provider's measured value added.

## 7. GDP's production boundary

GDP is not a measure of every useful activity, every act of effort, or all human welfare. It measures production within the defined national-accounts framework.

Most unpaid household services are excluded from GDP, even though they have real benefits. Market services such as restaurant meals are generally included. Some non-market or imputed production is included under specific accounting rules, so the distinction is not simply “payment means GDP, no payment means no GDP.”

This boundary is an accounting convention designed for systematic measurement; it does not establish that activities outside the boundary are useless or have no real value.

Detailed GDP measurement choices belong in Workstream B. This note records the conceptual distinction and does not replace the formal real-GDP measurement specification.

## 8. Production and productivity

Production and productivity are related but different.

- **Production/output:** The quantity or value of goods and services produced.
- **Labour input:** Labour used in production, often measured in hours worked or workers.
- **Labour productivity:** Output per unit of labour input.
- **Labour productivity growth:** The rate at which output per unit of labour input increases over time.

Illustrative farm example:

| Situation | Output | Labour hours | Output per labour hour |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Initial | 100 bags | 100 hours | 1 bag/hour |
| Expanded | 200 bags | 200 hours | 1 bag/hour |
| Improved method | 150 bags | 100 hours | 1.5 bags/hour |

If output and labour hours both double, total production increases but output per labour hour remains unchanged. If output increases while labour hours remain constant, labour productivity rises.

This example describes labour productivity, not necessarily total-factor productivity or the cause of the change. Labour productivity can change due to tools, skills, electricity, infrastructure, management, input quality, capacity utilization, and other factors.

A productivity improvement is not identical to an increase in total output, and neither alone establishes that all people are better off.

## 9. Important distinctions to preserve

| Concept | Core question |
|---|---|
| Production | What goods or services are produced? |
| GDP production boundary | Which production is counted under national-accounts rules? |
| Price/payment | What amount was charged or paid? |
| Revenue/output value | What value is recorded for sales or produced output under the relevant definition? |
| Intermediate consumption | What goods and services were used up in production? |
| Value added | What remains when intermediate consumption is deducted from output value? |
| Profit | What business result remains after relevant costs under a specified accounting measure? |
| Productivity | How much output is produced per unit of input? |
| Welfare/usefulness | How does an activity affect people's needs, choices, or well-being? |

## 11. Current conclusions and evidence boundaries

### Conceptual conclusions supported by the discussion

- Production can involve goods and services; it does not always create a new physical object.
- Transport, storage, and information services can be productive activities.
- Broad usefulness, production, and inclusion in GDP are distinct questions.
- The GDP production boundary is defined by national-accounting rules and excludes most unpaid household services while including certain imputed or own-use production under specific rules.
- Payment, sale-price differences, value added, and profit are not interchangeable.
- Output growth and labour productivity growth are different.

### Illustrations, not empirical claims

- All maize prices and cost figures in the worked example are hypothetical.
- The information-service example illustrates a possible mechanism; it does not prove that traders in any particular market will pay for such a service.
- The farm output and labour-hour examples are hypothetical.

### Questions to revisit

- How do national accounts record trade margins, transport, and storage services in detail?
- How is real GDP measured, and how do nominal output and price changes differ?
- How should the production boundary be explained alongside informal and unpaid activity in different economies?
- What is the precise definition of labour productivity used in the Lifeconomist model, and which output and labour-input series are suitable?
- Under what conditions does information materially improve a decision, and how could that benefit be tested in a real customer setting?

## 12. Short summary

Production is the creation of goods and provision of services. It can involve physical transformation, moving goods, storing them, or providing information and other services. Useful activity, GDP-counted production, transaction price, value added, profit, and productivity are related but distinct. We must begin with what happens in reality, then identify how it is measured and what conclusions the evidence permits.

## 13. Roadmap audit — coverage and remaining gaps

**Review date:** 2026-10-29  
**Purpose:** Identify what this living note already establishes before moving to the next concept; preserve the existing explanation rather than restarting it.

### Adequately established for the current stage

- Production can involve goods or services and need not create a new physical object.
- Everyday usefulness, broad production activity, and the GDP production boundary are different questions.
- Market and certain non-market or imputed production receive different treatment under national-accounting rules.
- The farmer–transporter–warehouse–trader examples show how different activities can contribute to an overall process.
- Transaction prices, sales/resale price differences, output, intermediate consumption, value added, profit, and productivity are distinct.
- The worked examples are explicitly hypothetical and do not establish real market prices or a validated information service.
- Workstream B is responsible for formal GDP and labour-productivity measurement choices.

### Gaps to keep open rather than silently treating as settled

1. **Output needs its own precise treatment.** The note sometimes uses output and revenue together for an accessible explanation. Their relationship depends on the context; output is not always identical to cash received or sales during the period.
2. **Intermediate inputs need a clearer boundary.** Goods and services used up or transformed in production should be distinguished from fixed assets that support production over multiple periods. The national-accounts concept of intermediate consumption has specific rules and is not simply every business expense.
3. **Inventory and timing require deeper treatment.** Production, sales, purchases, and cash receipts may occur in different periods. Work-in-progress and changes in inventories can matter when output is measured.
4. **Valuation rules need a sourced follow-up.** National accounts distinguish valuation bases and account for product taxes/subsidies and trade margins under defined rules. A simple market example should not imply that every industry is measured identically.
5. **Gross versus net value added is still only touched on.** The distinction depends on whether consumption of fixed capital is deducted.
6. **Examples should remain conceptually useful but not substitute for official definitions.** When the note makes technical claims about national accounting, it should be supported by authoritative sources; formal operational decisions remain in Workstream B.

### Audit conclusion

The note is a useful conceptual foundation and does not need a wholesale rewrite. The next note should clarify **output and intermediate inputs**, then link back to production. Later, update the production note only where the new understanding materially improves it. The next concept is a conceptual follow-on, not a claim that the production note is finished.

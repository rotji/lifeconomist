# Concept 5 — Revenue, Income and Receipts

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational explanation in progress  
**Started:** 2026-10-29  
**Previous concepts:** [Production](production.md); [Output and Intermediate Inputs](output-and-intermediate-inputs.md); [Economic Value, Usefulness and Exchange Value](economic-value-usefulness-and-exchange-value.md); [Costs and Prices](costs-and-prices.md)  
**Purpose:** Distinguish sales, revenue, income, earnings, cash receipts, receivables, payables, prepayments, profit and cash flow, and explain why the timing and accounting basis matter.

## 1. The central distinction

A business can produce something, sell it, recognize revenue, earn income, issue an invoice, receive cash and make a profit at different times. These are connected events, not synonyms.

- **Sales** usually describes goods or services sold during a period, but the exact meaning depends on the business's reporting convention.
- **Revenue** is recognized under the applicable accounting framework and policy. It often arises from providing goods or services to a customer, but recognition depends on the transaction and the relevant rules.
- **Income** is broader and context-dependent. In financial reporting it can include increases in economic benefits during a period, such as revenue and certain gains, subject to the applicable framework. In everyday speech it may mean wages, business earnings, investment returns or money received.
- **Cash receipts** are amounts of money actually received during a period.
- **Profit** is the result after deducting the expenses and other items included in a specified profit measure from the relevant income or revenue.
- **Cash flow** records cash entering and leaving an entity during a period, classified according to the applicable reporting framework.

When a figure is called “income,” “earnings” or “revenue,” ask whose measure it is, which accounting basis applies, what period it covers and whether it describes an earned amount or a cash movement.

## 2. A simple example: sale on credit

Suppose a shop sells goods for ₦100,000 on 29 October and gives the customer 30 days to pay.

At the time of sale, assuming the sale qualifies for recognition under the applicable accounting rules:
- the shop records the relevant sale/revenue;
- it may record a receivable from the customer;
- it has not necessarily received ₦100,000 in cash;
- the sale does not by itself establish profit because the cost of goods sold and other expenses must also be considered.

If the customer pays in November, the cash receipt occurs in November. Collection of the receivable generally converts one asset (the amount owed) into another (cash); it does not mean the same sale should automatically be recognized as revenue a second time.

The example is simplified. Actual recognition depends on the contract, transfer of control or performance obligations, returns, discounts, taxes, and the relevant accounting framework.

## 3. Cash received is not always revenue earned

Now reverse the timing. A customer pays ₦60,000 in advance for a service to be delivered next month.

The business has received cash, but it has not necessarily earned all of the related revenue at the moment of payment. Depending on the contract and applicable accounting rules, it may record cash and a liability for a service still owed. Revenue is recognized as the relevant obligation is satisfied.

Other examples include:
- a customer deposit;
- advance rent;
- prepaid subscriptions;
- advance payment for goods not yet delivered;
- a loan received by a business.

A loan receipt increases cash and creates a liability; it is not ordinary sales revenue. A customer prepayment may create an obligation to deliver goods or services. The precise treatment depends on the transaction and reporting framework.

**Key lesson:** Cash received answers “what money came in?” Revenue recognition answers a different question: “what amount qualifies as revenue for this period under the applicable rules?”

## 4. Income is broader than revenue

Revenue is commonly associated with an entity's ordinary activities, such as selling goods or providing services. Income may be used more broadly in financial reporting and economic analysis.

Depending on the framework and context, income may include:
- revenue from ordinary activities;
- interest or dividend income;
- rental income;
- certain gains from selling assets;
- other increases in economic benefits recognized under the applicable rules.

These items should not be casually added together without checking definitions. For example, a gain from selling an old machine is not necessarily sales revenue from the business's ordinary products. A household's disposable income, a worker's wage income, a company's accounting income and a country's national income are different measures with different boundaries.

“Earnings” is also ambiguous. It can refer to wages, business profit, a defined earnings measure such as earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT), or investment returns. The meaning must be specified.

## 5. Receivables, payables and working-capital timing

A **receivable** is an amount owed to an entity, commonly by a customer. A **payable** is an amount the entity owes to another party, commonly a supplier.

These arise because economic transactions and cash settlement need not occur simultaneously.

Example:
1. A retailer receives goods from a supplier and agrees to pay ₦200,000 in 14 days.
2. The retailer may record inventory and a payable, subject to the transaction and accounting treatment.
3. The retailer sells some of those goods before paying the supplier.
4. Customer payments may arrive before or after the supplier payment is due.

The retailer's cash position depends on the timing and certainty of receipts and payments, not only on whether sales have occurred or whether a profit is reported. A profitable business can face a cash shortage if customers pay late while suppliers, employees, rent or taxes must be paid sooner.

Receivables may not all be collected, and payables may have contractual terms, disputes or penalties. Therefore, amounts recorded as due should not always be treated as equivalent to cash available now.

## 6. Revenue, profit and cash flow are different

Consider a simplified business for a month:

| Item | Illustrative amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue recognized | ₦500,000 |
| Expenses included in the chosen profit calculation | ₦420,000 |
| Profit under that simplified calculation | ₦80,000 |
| Cash collected from customers during the month | ₦350,000 |
| Cash paid out during the month | ₦390,000 |
| Net cash movement from these listed receipts and payments | −₦40,000 |

These numbers are hypothetical. They show how a business can report positive profit but experience a negative cash movement in the same period. Some revenue may remain uncollected, and some expenses may be paid in a different period; purchases of assets, borrowing, loan repayments and owner transactions may also affect cash flows without entering profit in the same way.

The table is not a complete income statement or cash-flow statement. In a real analysis, the reporting period, accounting basis, cash-flow classifications and included items must be stated.

## 7. Profit is not the same as cash in the bank

Profit is an accounting result calculated under a defined framework. Cash is a stock of money available at a point in time; cash flow describes movements during a period.

Profit can differ from cash movement because of:
- sales on credit and changes in receivables;
- purchases on credit and changes in payables;
- inventory timing;
- depreciation or consumption of fixed capital, which is an expense without an equal current-period cash payment;
- buying long-lived assets, which can consume cash but is not necessarily an immediate expense equal to the entire purchase price;
- borrowing and loan principal repayment;
- owner contributions, withdrawals and distributions;
- taxes, interest and other items recognized or paid in different periods.

A positive profit does not guarantee liquidity or solvency. A negative cash movement does not by itself prove a business is unprofitable. The cause, timing, obligations and available financing must be examined.

## 8. Gross margin and profit measures

**Gross margin** generally describes revenue less a defined cost of goods sold or cost of sales, either as an amount or as a percentage of revenue. Exact definitions vary by sector and reporting convention.

**Operating profit** typically reflects the result from operations under a specified accounting presentation before certain financing and tax items, but exact definitions and adjustments vary.

**Net profit** is a specified bottom-line measure after the expenses, finance costs, taxes and other items included by the relevant framework or reporting convention.

These measures are not interchangeable. When comparing businesses, verify that their definitions, accounting policies, periods and included items are comparable. A business may report a healthy gross margin while having high rent, staff, logistics, financing or administrative costs. A gross margin is not the same as cash generated.

## 9. Household and worker examples

The same distinctions apply outside companies, but terms must be adapted carefully.

- A worker may **earn wages** in October but receive payment in November.
- A household may **receive a loan** that increases its cash balance, while also increasing its debt; the loan is not ordinary earned income.
- A household may receive a government transfer or gift. Whether it is classified as income depends on the framework and question.
- A household may have **income** but little cash immediately available because bills, debt payments or timing commitments absorb it.
- A household's **disposable income** is a defined measure after specified taxes and transfers; it is not identical to total cash deposits or wealth.

Do not assume a household's bank-account inflows equal its income. The inflows may include transfers between the household's own accounts, loans, asset sales or money received on behalf of another person.

## 10. National accounts: income is a defined aggregate

In national accounting, terms such as gross domestic product (GDP), gross national income (GNI), compensation of employees, operating surplus, mixed income and disposable income have formal definitions and accounting relationships. They are not simple totals of all cash received by residents, firms or government.

At a high level:
- GDP measures production within the domestic economic territory under the national-accounts framework.
- GNI adjusts GDP for the relevant balance of primary income flows between residents and the rest of the world.
- Disposable income reflects income available for final consumption or saving after the relevant current transfers, under the framework's definitions.

These are national aggregates with specified institutional boundaries, valuation rules and accounting identities. They must not be inferred by adding a random set of business revenues, household receipts, asset values or bank deposits. Workstream B will handle operational definitions, source series and calculation methods if Lifeconomist develops indicators using them.

The UN System of National Accounts and Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics provide the appropriate starting points for detailed definitions and methods.

## 11. A compact comparison

| Term | Main question |
|---|---|
| Sales | What goods or services were sold under the stated business convention? |
| Revenue | What amount qualifies as revenue for the period under the applicable accounting rules? |
| Income | What increases in economic benefits or earnings are included under the stated framework? |
| Earnings | What measure of wages, profit or returns is intended? |
| Cash receipts | What money was actually received during the period? |
| Receivable | What amount is owed to the entity? |
| Payable | What amount does the entity owe? |
| Prepayment | What amount was paid or received before the related good/service or obligation was fully delivered or satisfied? |
| Expense | What cost is recognized in the period under the relevant accounting rules? |
| Profit | What remains under a specified profit calculation? |
| Cash flow | What cash entered and left during the period, classified under the relevant framework? |
| Cash balance | How much cash is held at a particular point in time? |
| Disposable income | A defined income measure after specified taxes and transfers; context determines whether it is a household or national-accounts measure |

## 12. Common errors to avoid

1. Treating revenue as cash already received.
2. Treating every cash inflow as income or revenue.
3. Treating a loan as earned income.
4. Treating the collection of a receivable as a new sale.
5. Treating profit as the cash balance.
6. Treating a profitable business as necessarily liquid or solvent.
7. Treating all customer prepayments as revenue earned immediately.
8. Comparing “income” figures without checking whether they mean gross, net, disposable, accounting or national income.
9. Adding business revenues together and calling the result national income or GDP.
10. Treating gross margin, operating profit and net profit as identical.

## 13. Practical checklist for interpreting a reported figure

When someone reports revenue, income, earnings or receipts, ask:

- Who is the entity and what is the reporting boundary?
- What is the period, and is the figure a flow over that period or a balance at a point in time?
- Is it accrual-based, cash-based, or another stated basis?
- What transactions and accounting categories are included or excluded?
- Are taxes, refunds, discounts, returns and bad debts treated consistently?
- Are receivables, payables, inventories and prepayments material?
- Does the number represent a gross amount or a net amount?
- If comparing two figures, are the definitions and periods comparable?
- What source supports the figure, and is it observed, estimated or provisional?

## 14. Connections to other concepts and workstreams

- **Production/output:** Production and output can occur before sale, revenue recognition or payment.
- **Costs and prices:** Sales value depends on prices and quantities, but costs and payment timing determine neither revenue recognition nor cash flow by themselves.
- **Value added and profit:** Revenue is not value added or profit. Intermediate consumption, compensation of employees, depreciation, other operating items and valuation conventions matter.
- **Capital and finance:** Borrowing and owner contributions may provide cash without being ordinary revenue; investment in fixed assets may consume cash without being expensed all at once.
- **Workstream B:** Formal income, GDP, GNI and disposable-income indicators require authoritative definitions and complete accounting scope.
- **Workstream C:** Reported sales, receipts and payment delays can be useful signals, but each must be interpreted according to its timing and coverage.
- **Workstream D:** Invoices, receipts, contracts and bank transactions can corroborate parts of an economic story; no one document necessarily establishes total income, profit or cash availability.
- **Workstream E:** A service helping businesses monitor receivables or cash timing may solve a real operational problem, but this concept note alone does not establish demand or willingness to pay.

## 15. Evidence status and limitations

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Revenue recognition and cash receipt are different questions.
- A loan receipt is not ordinary revenue from selling goods or services.
- Profit and cash flow are different measures.
- Receivables and payables capture obligations that may be settled in a different period from the underlying transaction.
- National-accounting aggregates have formal boundaries and definitions.

### Illustrative only
- All business and household amounts are hypothetical.
- The examples are not a diagnosis of any actual business's accounting or liquidity.
- No business, household or national income figure is estimated here.

## 16. Short summary

Sales, revenue, income, receipts, profit and cash flow describe different aspects of economic activity. Revenue and income are recognized or defined under a stated framework; cash receipts describe money actually received. Receivables, payables and prepayments explain why transaction, recognition and payment dates can differ. Profit is not cash in the bank, and a loan receipt is not ordinary earned revenue. Always specify the entity, period, accounting basis, scope and source.

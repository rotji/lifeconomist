# Concept 20 — Business Structure, Ownership and Corporate Finance

**Workstream:** A — Deep Conceptual Foundations  
**Status:** Foundational conceptual note; legal definitions, corporate filings and formal ownership data must be verified for the relevant jurisdiction and period  
**Previous concept:** [Concept 19 — Accounting, Balance Sheets and Financial Statements](accounting-balance-sheets-and-financial-statements.md)

## 1. Purpose

A business is not just a name, a shop, a product or a bank account. It can be an activity conducted by an individual, a legally registered entity, a group of companies, a partnership, a cooperative, a nonprofit organization or another arrangement. Ownership, control, management, financing, legal responsibility and economic benefit may sit with different people or institutions.

This note establishes the vocabulary needed to investigate how businesses are organized, who owns or controls them, how they raise finance, how owners receive returns and how corporate transactions change business structure. These distinctions are essential for accounting analysis, capital-flow tracing and footprint intelligence.

Legal forms, rights, tax treatment and disclosure requirements vary by jurisdiction. The note is conceptual; it does not establish the ownership or control of any particular company and does not give legal, tax or investment advice.

## 2. Business activity, brand, legal entity and business group

A **business activity** is the economic activity of producing, trading or providing goods or services. A **brand** is a name, identity or set of associations used to distinguish a product, service or organization. A brand may be owned by a company, licensed to another operator or used under a franchise arrangement.

A **legal entity** is an organization or person recognized by law as having rights and obligations in the relevant context. Depending on the jurisdiction and legal form, an entity may own property, enter contracts, sue or be sued, borrow, employ people and incur liabilities in its own name.

A **business group** can consist of a parent and subsidiaries, affiliates or other linked entities. The word “group” may be used commercially even when its precise legal perimeter differs from the public-facing brand. One brand may cover several legal entities; one legal entity may operate several brands; and a store, factory, website or office may be a location rather than a separate entity.

To investigate a business, identify the exact entity name, registration identifier where available, jurisdiction, legal form, operating names, relevant locations, parent/subsidiary links and reporting boundary. Do not infer that a brand name is itself the contracting party or that all businesses sharing a brand have the same owner.

## 3. Common legal forms

Common forms, with names and rules varying by jurisdiction, include:

- **Sole proprietorship / sole trader:** business activity conducted by an individual under the applicable legal and tax rules. Business and owner obligations may not be separated in the same way as for a company with separate legal personality.
- **Partnership:** an arrangement in which two or more parties carry on a business under a partnership framework. Partner authority, liability and continuity depend on the form and law.
- **Limited liability company or corporation:** a legal entity whose owners' liability is generally limited under applicable rules, subject to exceptions, guarantees, misconduct and other legal doctrines.
- **Public or private company:** categories defined by local company law; the terms do not mean that a company is government-owned or government-funded.
- **Cooperative:** an organization owned or governed by members under cooperative principles and applicable law.
- **Nonprofit or charitable organization:** an entity organized for specified purposes and subject to rules governing distributions, governance and use of resources. “Nonprofit” does not necessarily mean it has no revenue or employees.
- **State-owned enterprise:** an enterprise in which a government has ownership or control as defined by law or policy. The exact ownership and control structure must be checked.
- **Joint venture:** a business or project undertaken jointly by parties, which may be organized through a separate entity or a contractual arrangement.

Legal form affects liability, governance, access to finance, reporting, taxation, transferability of ownership and continuity. No form is universally best; suitability depends on the activity, jurisdiction, risk, scale and objectives.

## 4. Owners, shareholders, partners and members

An **owner** is a broad everyday term. Its exact meaning depends on the legal form and the rights being discussed. **Shareholders** hold shares in a company; **partners** have rights and obligations under a partnership arrangement; **members** may hold governance or economic rights in a cooperative, association or other entity.

A shareholding can confer economic rights, voting rights or other rights under the share class, company documents and law. The percentage of shares held does not always equal the percentage of voting power or economic entitlement. Different share classes, shareholder agreements, restrictions, options and other arrangements may alter the relationship.

Ownership can be direct or indirect. A person may hold shares through a holding company, trust, nominee or other intermediary. Each layer requires evidence; a chain should not be treated as established merely because names are similar or entities share an address.

For every ownership claim, identify the relevant entity, class of interest, percentage or right, effective date, direct or indirect route, source and confidence. A historic filing may not prove the current position.

## 5. Directors, managers, employees, creditors and advisers

Different roles should not be conflated:

- **Shareholder/owner:** holds an ownership interest or other legally defined ownership rights.
- **Director/board member:** participates in governance and oversight under the entity's law and governing documents.
- **Executive/manager:** directs or manages operations within delegated authority.
- **Employee:** works under an employment relationship, which is distinct from ownership.
- **Creditor/lender:** has a claim for repayment or another obligation; lending does not itself confer equity ownership.
- **Guarantor:** undertakes specified responsibility if another party fails to meet an obligation, subject to the guarantee's terms and law.
- **Adviser/consultant:** provides services or advice and does not automatically obtain authority, ownership or decision rights.
- **Agent/representative:** acts for another party within the scope of authority granted.
- **Beneficiary:** receives benefits under a specified arrangement, which may differ from legal title or management.

One person may hold several roles simultaneously, but each role should be supported separately. A director is not necessarily a shareholder; a large lender is not automatically the owner; and a founder may retain economic interest while no longer managing day-to-day operations.

## 6. Legal ownership, beneficial ownership and control

**Legal ownership** concerns the ownership recognized under the applicable legal arrangement or register. **Beneficial ownership** generally refers to the natural person or persons who ultimately own or benefit from an interest, as defined by the relevant law or reporting framework. **Control** concerns the ability to direct relevant decisions or activities under legal rights, governance arrangements or, in some contexts, demonstrable practical influence.

These concepts overlap but are not identical. A registered shareholder may act as a nominee; a holding company may legally own a subsidiary while natural persons own the holding company; voting agreements can affect control; and a lender may exercise certain protective rights without becoming an owner.

**Ultimate beneficial owner (UBO)** definitions vary across legal regimes and may use thresholds, control tests or both. A threshold is a reporting rule, not proof that interests below it are economically irrelevant. Ownership transparency records may be incomplete, delayed, exempt under applicable rules or limited in scope.

A sound investigation distinguishes:
1. registered/legal holder;
2. direct owner;
3. intermediate entities;
4. ultimate natural-person owner where supported;
5. economic beneficiary;
6. voting rights;
7. appointment or removal rights;
8. contractual control rights;
9. practical decision-making influence, when evidence supports it.

Do not infer illegal concealment or improper conduct merely from a layered structure. Legitimate reasons can include liability separation, investment structure, regulation, joint ventures and succession planning. The structure alone does not establish intent.

## 7. Ownership percentage versus effective control

A simple majority of voting rights often gives substantial control, but the exact result depends on company law, voting arrangements, quorum rules, share classes, reserved matters, shareholder agreements and other rights. In some settings, a minority shareholder can exercise significant influence or control; in others, a large economic interest may carry limited voting rights.

**Economic ownership** refers to entitlement to economic benefits or losses under the relevant arrangement. **Voting power** concerns rights to vote. **Control** concerns decision-making authority under the applicable test. **Significant influence** commonly means the ability to participate in financial and operating policy decisions without necessarily controlling them, but precise accounting definitions and thresholds must be checked.

A person with 20% of shares does not automatically control 20% of every business decision or receive exactly 20% of every cash distribution. The answer depends on share class, dilution, preferences, contracts, restrictions, tax, distribution decisions and law.

When describing a person as “controlling” a business, specify the basis: voting majority, board appointment, contractual rights, accounting control test, regulatory UBO test or evidenced practical influence. Avoid using “control” as an unsupported synonym for ownership or influence.

## 8. Corporate governance and decision rights

**Corporate governance** concerns how an organization is directed, overseen and held accountable. It includes the allocation of authority among shareholders, boards, executives and other stakeholders; decision procedures; disclosure; risk oversight; and mechanisms for handling conflicts of interest.

The **board of directors** generally has governance responsibilities under the applicable framework, while executives manage delegated operations. Owners may vote on specified matters, appoint directors or approve transactions, but daily decision authority depends on law and governing documents.

**Reserved matters**, shareholder agreements, veto rights, debt covenants and regulatory approvals can limit or shape what management may do. These rights can matter more than ownership percentage alone for particular decisions.

Governance structures can create checks and accountability, but formal documents do not prove that procedures operate effectively in practice. To understand actual decision-making, combine governing documents, filings, board disclosures where available, transaction evidence and credible independent reporting.

## 9. Equity financing and debt financing

**Equity financing** provides funds in exchange for an ownership interest or an equity-linked claim. It can dilute existing owners' percentages or rights, depending on the issue and terms. Equity generally does not require repayment of principal on a fixed debt schedule, but investors expect returns and bear specified risks.

**Debt financing** provides funds under an obligation to repay principal and usually interest or other contractual amounts. It can include bank loans, bonds, notes, leases or other debt-like arrangements depending on the purpose and accounting framework. Debt can preserve ownership percentages but creates repayment, covenant, refinancing and default risks.

Other financing channels include trade credit, grants, customer advances, retained earnings, asset-backed financing, leasing, convertible instruments and public support. These are not all equivalent: some create liabilities, some ownership claims, some restrictions, and some non-repayable support under defined conditions.

A financing label alone does not show who bears risk, what rights are granted, when cash is due, how proceeds may be used or whether financing reached productive activity. Examine instrument terms, counterparty, drawdown, fees, collateral, covenants, repayment, conversion and use of proceeds.

## 10. Share capital, equity value, enterprise value and cash

**Share capital** is an accounting/legal concept tied to shares issued and the relevant company framework. It is not necessarily the current value of the company or the cash presently held. Issuance proceeds may already have been spent, invested, used to repay liabilities or retained as assets.

**Market capitalization** commonly equals a defined share price multiplied by the number of outstanding shares included in the measure. It values the equity at the quoted price convention; it is not cash raised or a guarantee that all shares can be sold at that price.

**Enterprise value (EV)** is a valuation measure intended to represent the value of operating business assets to capital providers under a stated methodology. A common approximation starts with equity market value and adds selected debt and other claims, then subtracts selected cash and cash equivalents. Definitions differ: leases, minority interests, preferred equity, pension obligations and other items may be included or excluded.

**Equity value** and **enterprise value** answer different questions. Equity value concerns the owners' residual claim under a valuation framework; enterprise value attempts to value the operating business before allocating value between certain financing claimholders. Neither is the same as accounting equity, gross assets, total funds invested over time or cash available for distribution.

A valuation multiple such as EV/EBITDA is meaningful only if the enterprise-value definition and EBITDA period/definition are consistent. Valuation is not a cash-flow statement.

## 11. Retained earnings, new capital and distributions

**Retained earnings** generally reflect accumulated recognized profits and losses not distributed or otherwise reclassified under the accounting framework. They are an equity component, not a segregated cash account. Retained earnings can be positive while cash is low, or negative while the entity has cash from financing or other sources.

**New capital contribution** means owners provide additional funds or resources under an arrangement that is recognized as equity or another specified instrument. It differs from revenue earned through customers and from borrowing that creates a repayment obligation.

A **dividend** is a distribution to shareholders under the applicable company law, governing documents and authorization procedures. It is not normally an operating expense in the income statement; it is generally treated as a distribution to owners. A dividend declared or paid can affect cash and equity, subject to the framework and timing.

Other owner-related transactions can include share repurchases, capital reductions, redemptions, loans to/from owners and returns of capital. Their legal and accounting treatment differs. A payment to an owner should not automatically be classified as salary, dividend, expense or loan repayment without checking its substance and documentation.

## 12. Corporate finance and capital structure

**Corporate finance** studies how an organization raises funds, invests resources, manages financial risk and distributes returns. Core decisions include capital budgeting, financing mix, working capital, liquidity, payout policy, risk management and valuation.

**Capital structure** describes the mix of financing claims, often including debt and equity, with the exact measure defined for the analysis. A debt-to-equity ratio can be based on book or market values and may include different debt categories; comparisons require a consistent definition.

Debt can create tax, governance or financing benefits in some contexts but also increases fixed obligations, refinancing needs and distress risk. Equity can absorb losses more flexibly but may be more costly from an investor's perspective or dilute ownership. No universal debt/equity ratio is optimal for all firms.

**Capital budgeting** evaluates potential long-lived investments, commonly using forecasts, risk assessment and methods such as net present value. A positive forecast does not guarantee success; results depend on assumptions, execution, external conditions and alternative uses of funds.

Corporate finance is not limited to maximizing one short-term accounting number. Decisions may be constrained by law, contracts, liquidity, strategy, stakeholder obligations and the distribution of risk and returns.

## 12.1 Revenue models, margins, operating leverage and unit economics

A **revenue model** describes how an organization earns revenue from the value it offers, such as selling units, charging a subscription, taking a transaction fee, licensing intellectual property, renting an asset or charging for access. A business model is broader: it also includes the customers served, activities, resources, partners, delivery arrangements and cost structure.

**Gross margin**, **operating margin** and **net margin** use different profit measures and denominators. A higher margin does not necessarily mean a business generates more total profit or cash: sales volume, fixed costs, capital needs, taxes, debt service and collection timing also matter. Margin comparisons require consistent accounting definitions, periods and business mix.

**Operating leverage** describes how changes in revenue can produce proportionally larger or smaller changes in operating profit because of fixed and variable costs. A business with high fixed costs may see profits rise quickly after revenue passes a break-even threshold, but it may also suffer disproportionately when sales fall. The result depends on capacity utilization, pricing, variable input costs and how quickly costs can adjust.

**Unit economics** examine revenue, contribution and cost for a defined unit, such as an item sold, order, customer, delivery, subscription or service engagement. The unit and time horizon must be explicit. For example, a contribution per order may subtract direct variable costs but exclude fixed overhead, customer acquisition, returns, bad debt, taxes or financing costs. A positive contribution therefore does not by itself prove that the whole business is profitable or viable.

**Revenue → contribution after defined variable costs → coverage of fixed costs and other obligations → operating profit and cash outcome.**

In plain language, selling more can improve a business's position when each additional sale contributes enough to cover overhead and other obligations. But if each sale has a negative contribution, growth can increase losses; and even positive contribution can coexist with cash stress if customers pay late or inventory must be funded in advance.

For comparisons, specify the revenue-recognition basis, unit, time window, included costs, returns/discounts, customer acquisition costs, utilization assumptions and whether the measure is accounting profit, contribution, operating cash or cash available after financing. Do not compare a company's gross margin with another company's net margin as if they were the same metric.

## 13. Funding, financing, commitment, disbursement and deployment

These terms describe different stages:
- **Funding source:** where the resources originate, such as retained earnings, an equity investor, a bank or a public program.
- **Financing arrangement:** the contractual or institutional mechanism that makes resources available.
- **Commitment:** an announced or contractually agreed amount, which may be conditional.
- **Approval:** authorization under a specified decision process; approval alone may not mean funds are available.
- **Disbursement/drawdown:** funds actually transferred or borrowed under the arrangement.
- **Deployment/use of proceeds:** what the recipient does with funds after receiving them.
- **Outcome:** the observed result, such as capacity built, working capital supplied, refinancing completed or a project failing.

An announced investment amount may be a target, commitment, valuation, total project cost or actual cash invested. These figures cannot be treated as equivalent. For analysis, record currency, date, instrument, recipient, stage, conditions, source and whether the amount is gross or net.

A completed financing transaction also does not prove that the proceeds were used as intended or achieved the promised impact. Those are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

## 14. Mergers, acquisitions, organic growth and restructuring

A **merger** combines entities or businesses under a defined legal and transaction structure. An **acquisition** occurs when one party obtains control of another business or assets under the relevant legal and accounting tests. A transaction may involve shares, assets, cash, debt, newly issued securities or a combination.

**Organic growth** is expansion through an entity's existing business activity, such as increased sales, new capacity or new customers. **Inorganic growth** often refers to growth through acquisitions, mergers or other external combinations. The terms are broad strategic labels and need operational definitions for measurement.

Buying an existing company transfers ownership and may reorganize control; it does not automatically create an equivalent amount of new productive assets. An acquisition may create efficiencies, new investment or expanded capacity, but those outcomes must be evaluated rather than assumed. Asset purchases can also have different accounting and legal consequences from share purchases.

**Restructuring** can refer to changes in operations, legal entities, debt terms, ownership, workforce or asset portfolio. A debt restructuring changes obligations; an operational restructuring changes business activity; a corporate reorganization changes legal or group structure. Specify which is meant.

Transaction value, purchase price, assumed debt, equity consideration, enterprise value and subsequent capital expenditure may all be different amounts. Avoid adding them without checking overlap and definitions.

## 15. Parent companies, subsidiaries, affiliates and joint ventures

A **parent company** controls one or more subsidiaries under the relevant legal or accounting test. A **subsidiary** is an entity controlled by another entity. An **affiliate** is a less precise term that may mean a related company or entity under common control; use the applicable definition. An **associate** may be an entity over which an investor has significant influence but not control under the relevant accounting framework. A **joint venture** involves joint control under the applicable arrangement.

A group structure can separate activities, assets, liabilities, licenses, financing, risk and jurisdictions. This can have legitimate operational or legal reasons. It can also make it difficult for outsiders to see the full economic relationship from one entity's accounts.

Consolidated accounts typically present a parent and its controlled subsidiaries as a group, eliminating specified intra-group balances and transactions. They do not necessarily consolidate every affiliate, associate, joint venture or entity with which the group has commercial ties.

To map a group, document each entity and jurisdiction, ownership percentages, voting rights, control basis, consolidation treatment, intercompany loans, guarantees, service agreements, shared directors and relevant dates. Avoid assuming that a common brand, address or director proves a parent-subsidiary relationship.

## 16. Related-party transactions, guarantees and intra-group finance

Related-party transactions may include sales, services, loans, guarantees, asset transfers, management fees, royalties and other arrangements between related entities or persons. Such transactions can be legitimate, but their terms can affect reported profit, tax, liquidity, creditor risk and the allocation of economic benefits.

An **intra-group loan** is a claim between entities in the same group. It may be visible in each entity's separate accounts but eliminated on consolidation under the relevant rules. A **guarantee** can expose one entity to another's obligations without making it the direct borrower or owner of the underlying business.

A **cross-guarantee** or shared collateral arrangement can connect entities that otherwise have separate legal personalities. A failure in one entity may affect another if the guarantee, collateral, cash pooling, covenant or insolvency rules create that exposure. The exact mechanism must be evidenced.

A **transfer price** is the price used for transactions between related parties. Transfer pricing rules seek to address how related-party transactions are priced for specified tax purposes; the legal standard and documentation vary. A high or low related-party price alone does not prove abuse without contextual evidence and applicable analysis.

When tracing financial footprints, record the actual instrument, parties, amount, date, contractual basis, cash transfer, collateral and accounting treatment. Do not treat every related-party transaction as improper or every intra-group amount as new external funding.

## 17. Insolvency, limited liability and creditor claims

**Insolvency** can refer to inability to pay debts as they fall due (cash-flow insolvency) or liabilities exceeding assets under a specified balance-sheet test. Legal definitions differ. A company can have valuable long-term assets yet lack liquidity to meet immediate obligations; it can also have cash today while facing a severe balance-sheet deficit.

**Limited liability** generally limits owners' responsibility for company obligations under the relevant legal structure, but exceptions can exist. Personal guarantees, security interests, statutory liabilities, fraud, wrongful conduct or other legal doctrines may create exposure beyond the owner's investment. The actual law and facts matter.

**Creditor priority** determines the order in which claims are paid from available assets under applicable insolvency and security rules. Secured creditors, employees, tax authorities, bondholders, trade creditors and shareholders may be treated differently. Shareholders commonly rank behind creditors in liquidation, but priority rules and exceptions vary.

A company's failure does not by itself prove that owners acted unlawfully. Nor does limited liability mean that no one bears the loss: creditors, employees, suppliers, investors, customers and the public may be affected in different ways.

## 18. Business valuation versus cash available to owners

Business value is an estimate or market measure under a specified method and date. Cash available to owners is a different question. A business can have a high valuation but little distributable cash, or cash on its balance sheet that is restricted, needed for operations or offset by obligations.

Distributable cash depends on operating requirements, working capital, debt service, capital expenditure, taxes, legal distribution restrictions, covenants, reserves and board/shareholder decisions. Accounting profit or retained earnings does not guarantee that a dividend can or should be paid.

A shareholder may realize value through dividends, sale of shares, buybacks or other transactions, each with distinct timing, tax, legal and market constraints. An unrealized increase in share value is not the same as cash received. A sale of a controlling stake can have a different price and conditions from small trades in listed shares.

For private businesses, valuation estimates can be especially uncertain where there is no active market, comparable transactions are limited or records are incomplete. State the method, assumptions, date, currency, valuation basis and uncertainty rather than presenting an estimate as a verified cash amount.

## 19. Commonly confused terms

| Terms | Core distinction |
|---|---|
| Business vs brand | Business activity and a market-facing brand are not necessarily the same legal or operational unit. |
| Brand vs legal entity | A brand may be licensed, shared or operated by several entities; the legal contracting party must be identified. |
| Legal entity vs business group | One entity is a legal unit; a group may include a parent, subsidiaries and other relationships. |
| Owner vs manager | Ownership rights and delegated operational authority are distinct. |
| Shareholder vs director | Shareholding does not automatically make someone a director, and directors need not be shareholders. |
| Creditor vs owner | Lending creates a claim; it does not by itself create an equity interest. |
| Legal vs beneficial ownership | Registered/legal title may differ from the person who ultimately benefits under the applicable definition. |
| Ownership vs control | Economic interest, voting power and decision authority may differ. |
| Minority stake vs no influence | A minority interest can carry significant rights; the facts and rules determine influence or control. |
| Equity financing vs debt financing | Equity provides an ownership or equity-linked claim; debt creates repayment obligations. |
| Share capital vs cash | Recorded share capital is not a statement of cash currently held. |
| Equity value vs enterprise value | Equity value concerns owners' claims; enterprise value estimates operating business value under a defined method. |
| Market capitalization vs funds invested | Market capitalization is a current equity valuation, not cumulative cash invested or cash available. |
| Retained earnings vs cash | Retained earnings are an equity component, not a separate bank account. |
| Dividend vs operating expense | A dividend is generally a distribution to owners, not an operating cost. |
| Revenue vs capital contribution | Customer revenue differs from owner-provided capital. |
| Guarantee vs direct borrowing | A guarantor may be exposed to another party's obligation without being the primary borrower. |
| Commitment vs disbursement | An agreed or announced amount may not yet have been transferred. |
| Disbursement vs deployment | Receipt of funds does not establish how the recipient used them. |
| Acquisition vs new productive investment | Buying an existing business transfers ownership; new capacity and outcomes require separate evidence. |
| Separate vs consolidated accounts | Group statements eliminate specified intra-group transactions and balances. |
| Related-party vs improper transaction | A relationship calls for context and disclosure; it does not alone prove wrongdoing. |
| Limited liability vs no personal exposure | Guarantees and legal exceptions can create exposure beyond invested equity. |
| Valuation vs distributable cash | An estimated business value is not cash an owner can withdraw today. |

## 20. Measurement and evidence checklist

Before making a claim about a business's structure, ownership, financing or control, ask:

1. What is the exact legal entity, jurisdiction, registration identifier and relevant date?
2. Is the name a brand, trading name, location, legal entity or group label?
3. Which legal form applies, and what rights and liabilities follow from it?
4. Who are the registered direct owners, and what source supports the claim?
5. Is beneficial ownership disclosed or established under the applicable legal definition?
6. What are the ownership percentage, share class, voting rights and effective date?
7. Is there evidence of control, significant influence, board appointment rights or contractual veto rights?
8. Are directors, executives, creditors, guarantors, advisers and beneficial owners distinguished?
9. Is the funding equity, debt, grant, retained earnings, customer advance, guarantee or another arrangement?
10. What amount was announced, approved, committed, drawn down and actually deployed?
11. Who received the funds, when, in what currency, under which instrument and with what conditions?
12. Is share capital, accounting equity, market capitalization or enterprise value being discussed?
13. What is the valuation method, date, currency, share basis and uncertainty?
14. Are dividends or owner payments legally authorized and correctly distinguished from salary, expenses and loan repayments?
15. Are there parent/subsidiary links, intra-group loans, guarantees, shared collateral or related-party transactions?
16. Are separate accounts being summed without eliminating internal balances and transactions?
17. Does an acquisition value include assumed debt, equity consideration, cash or other components that could overlap?
18. Are insolvency and liability claims based on the applicable legal test rather than a casual use of the term?
19. Could the structure have ordinary commercial or legal explanations, and what evidence would distinguish alternatives?
20. What remains unknown, stale, self-reported or unverified?

## 21. Connections to other Workstreams

- **Workstream A:** links accounting, money, capital, debt, investment, markets, ownership, governance and the allocation of economic returns.
- **Workstream B — Measurement:** company ownership, legal form, financing, valuation and corporate activity require dated source records, jurisdiction-specific definitions and careful unit/coverage checks. This note asserts no current ownership or financing facts about any company.
- **Workstream C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** distinguish ownership changes, capital raises, debt changes, dividend payments, acquisitions, restructuring announcements and completed transactions as separate events.
- **Workstream D — Footprint Intelligence:** map legal entities, direct/indirect owners, beneficial owners where evidenced, directors, lenders, guarantees, instruments, cash transfers, parent/subsidiary relationships and decision rights. Treat every link as a separate claim with a source and confidence level.
- **Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence:** when assessing a small business, separate owner withdrawals, sales, financing, profit, working capital and cash available for operations. Do not infer that an owner can pay a fee or make an investment solely from a valuation or reported revenue.
- **Global Financial System Exploration Map:** represent the source of capital, legal claim, recipient, ownership/control relationship, valuation and final use separately to avoid counting a financial claim, its funding and the underlying asset as three independent pools of wealth.

## 22. Evidence status and open questions

### Established conceptual distinctions
- Business activity, brand, legal entity and business group can have different boundaries.
- Owners, directors, managers, creditors, guarantors and beneficiaries hold different rights and responsibilities.
- Legal ownership, beneficial ownership, economic entitlement, voting power and control are related but distinct.
- Equity and debt financing create different claims and risks.
- Share capital, market capitalization, enterprise value, accounting equity and cash are not interchangeable.
- A commitment, approval, disbursement, deployment and outcome are different stages.
- Acquisition value does not automatically equal new productive investment.
- Group structures and related-party transactions require entity-level analysis and may create intercompany amounts that are eliminated in consolidation.
- Legal form and limited liability do not eliminate all possible personal, creditor or stakeholder exposure.

### Scope limits
- No particular company's ownership, control, legal status, valuation or financing has been asserted.
- Legal terms, beneficial-ownership thresholds, filing duties and insolvency tests vary by jurisdiction and can change.
- A public filing may be stale, incomplete or limited to a defined purpose; absence of a disclosure is not automatically proof that a relationship does not exist.
- Corporate structures and related-party transactions are not inherently improper.
- Valuation estimates are not equivalent to realizable or distributable cash.

## 23. Short summary

Business structure determines the legal unit, rights and obligations through which activity occurs. Ownership, control, management, financing and economic benefit may belong to different parties. Equity and debt finance create different claims; share capital, market capitalization, enterprise value and cash answer different questions. Announced commitments do not prove disbursement, and disbursement does not prove productive deployment or outcomes. Group structures, guarantees and related-party transactions require tracing each entity, instrument, relationship and source separately.

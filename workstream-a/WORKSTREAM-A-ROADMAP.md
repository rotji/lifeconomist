# Workstream A — Conceptual Foundations Roadmap and Terminology Inventory

**Status:** Approved working roadmap; exploratory and revisable  
**Created:** 2026-10-29  
**Scope:** Continue Workstream A from the existing production concept note, build a coherent map of economic and financial concepts, and investigate them in a connected sequence.  
**Authoritative starting note:** [Concept 1 — Production](concepts/production.md)

## 1. Purpose

Workstream A builds a reality-first understanding of economic concepts before they are used in measurement, monitoring, explanation, or practical decisions. The objective is not to produce a dictionary of disconnected definitions. It is to understand what concepts refer to in the real world, how they differ, how they relate, how they are measured, and what conclusions they do and do not support.

A recurring problem to prevent is using related terms as though they were interchangeable—for example, money, wealth, and capital; revenue, income, profit, and cash flow; or liquidity and solvency. Their meanings can vary across everyday speech, economic theory, accounting, financial markets, and national accounts. Notes must identify the context rather than silently merge definitions.

This roadmap preserves broad exploration while providing an orderly route through it. The inventory is a map of candidate concepts, not a claim that all topics have been researched, that every term has one universal definition, or that every item deserves an equally long note.

## 5. Standard for concept notes

Use the existing `workstream-a/README.md` structure as the starting template. Adapt it to each concept; not every section will be relevant.

For each substantial concept, aim to document:

1. **Plain-language definition:** What the term means in the context being discussed.
2. **Real-world referent:** What activity, object, relationship, claim, institution or process the term describes.
3. **Context of use:** Everyday language, economic theory, accounting, finance/markets, legal/institutional usage, or national accounting.
4. **Boundaries:** What it includes and excludes; common edge cases and exceptions.
5. **Types and variants:** Different forms that should not be collapsed.
6. **Mechanism:** How it works, including relevant counterparties, incentives, constraints and flows.
7. **Relationships:** Upstream/downstream connections, dependencies, feedback and relevant time dimension.
8. **Distinctions:** Closely related and commonly confused terms.
9. **Worked examples:** Concrete, transparent examples; label hypothetical numbers and assumptions.
10. **Measurement:** Units, denominators, stock/flow status, gross/net status, possible proxies and limitations; defer formal indicator specifications to Workstream B.
11. **Evidence status:** Separate established definitions, sourced claims, inference, illustration, hypotheses and unresolved questions.
12. **Practical implications:** Household, business, institutional, national or cross-border implications where relevant.
13. **Cross-workstream links:** Connections to measurement, monitoring, footprint investigation or opportunity assessment, without forcing a connection.
14. **Questions and change history:** Record what remains uncertain and material corrections.

Foundational concepts such as money, wealth, capital, production, credit, value added and productivity should receive deeper treatment than narrow specialist terms. Technical claims about accounting or institutions should be checked against reliable primary or authoritative sources before being stated as settled.

## 6. Research and reasoning safeguards

- Start with what happens in reality, then explain the technical label and its measurement.
- Never use money, wealth, capital, income, revenue, profit, cash flow, credit or liquidity as synonyms.
- State the accounting or theoretical context when a term has multiple legitimate meanings.
- Distinguish stocks from flows, levels from rates of change, and gross from net values.
- Identify both sides of financial claims and obligations where relevant; avoid double-counting.
- Do not add figures with incompatible definitions, time periods, units or consolidation boundaries.
- Distinguish a transaction price from total economic value, customer benefit, profit and measured value added.
- Do not treat a correlation, plausible mechanism, incentive, trace or example as proof of causation or intent.
- Label hypothetical calculations and examples. Do not present them as current market data.
- Use primary/official sources for definitions and measurement rules where feasible; record publication dates, coverage, limitations and unresolved discrepancies.
- Keep conceptual learning separate from formal indicator design, while explicitly recording when Workstream B needs to operationalize a concept.
- Use Workstream E implications when justified, but do not force every concept to generate a commercial idea.
- Preserve existing project architecture; this roadmap is part of Workstream A, not a new workstream.
- Revise this roadmap if actual investigation reveals gaps, redundancy or a better learning order. Record material scope changes in the decision register.

- **B — Measurement:** Turns selected concepts into operational definitions, formulas, series and quality checks.
- **C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** Uses observations and indicators to describe conditions and investigate changes without premature classification.
- **D — Footprint Intelligence:** Investigates observable traces, entities, relationships and dependencies where relevant.
- **E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application:** Examines practical applications and opportunities using evidence, affordable tests and explicit uncertainty.

The workstreams interact bidirectionally. A concept may expose a measurement problem; observed data may reveal a conceptual ambiguity; an investigation may expose missing relationships; and a practical test may show that an assumption needs revision. None of these connections means every concept must feed every workstream.

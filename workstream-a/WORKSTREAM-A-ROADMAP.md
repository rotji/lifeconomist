# Workstream A — Conceptual Foundations Roadmap and Terminology Inventory

**Status:** Approved working roadmap; exploratory and revisable  
**Created:** 2026-10-29  
**Scope:** Continue Workstream A from the existing production concept note, build a coherent map of economic and financial concepts, and investigate them in a connected sequence.  
**Authoritative starting note:** [Concept 1 — Production](concepts/production.md)

## 1. Purpose

Workstream A builds a reality-first understanding of economic concepts before they are used in measurement, monitoring, explanation, or practical decisions. The objective is not to produce a dictionary of disconnected definitions. It is to understand what concepts refer to in the real world, how they differ, how they relate, how they are measured, and what conclusions they do and do not support.

A recurring problem to prevent is using related terms as though they were interchangeable—for example, money, wealth, and capital; revenue, income, profit, and cash flow; or liquidity and solvency. Their meanings can vary across everyday speech, economic theory, accounting, financial markets, and national accounts. Notes must identify the context rather than silently merge definitions.

This roadmap preserves broad exploration while providing an orderly route through it. The inventory is a map of candidate concepts, not a claim that all topics have been researched, that every term has one universal definition, or that every item deserves an equally long note.

## 2. Current starting point

Concept 1, **Production**, is already documented in `concepts/production.md` as a living explanation. It covers goods and services, usefulness versus the GDP production boundary, examples involving farming, transport, storage and information, prices and costs, intermediate consumption, value added, profit, and labour productivity.

## 4. Investigation sequence

The inventory is broad; the investigation should proceed through connected groups rather than attempt to study every topic at once.

### Phase 0 — Preserve and audit the production foundation
1. Re-read `concepts/production.md` as the current authoritative concept note.
2. Identify which distinctions are already adequately explained, which questions remain open, and which details need reliable external references before being treated as technical accounting facts.
3. Do not rewrite material merely to make it longer. Correct, clarify, cross-link, or extend only where useful.
4. Keep Workstream B's formal GDP and productivity measurement choices separate from Workstream A's conceptual explanation.

### Phase 1 — Concepts that follow directly from production
1. **Economic value, usefulness, exchange value and welfare** — distinguish whose value is being considered, the valuation basis, willingness to pay, observed price, national-accounting value added and broader welfare effects.
2. **Costs and prices** — distinguish resource/economic cost, accounting cost, opportunity cost, market price, marginal cost, price-setting mechanisms, and nominal versus real prices.
3. **Revenue, income and receipts** — distinguish sales, revenue recognition, income/earnings, cash receipts, receivables/payables, prepayments, profit and cash flow.
4. **Value added, income distribution and profit** — distinguish gross/net value added, employee compensation, production taxes/subsidies, operating surplus/mixed income, and company gross/operating/net profit; clarify the relationship to GDP.
5. **Productivity and productive capacity** — distinguish output per input from output growth, labour productivity from multifactor productivity, measured productivity from worker effort, and actual output from capacity and utilisation.
6. **Capital formation, depreciation, maintenance and replacement** — distinguish investment in produced non-financial assets from financial investment and transfers of existing assets; explain gross/net capital formation, fixed assets, inventories, consumption of fixed capital and capital services.
7. **Money, wealth, capital and financial resources** — distinguish money and deposits, wealth/net worth, productive and financial capital, assets/liabilities, income, savings, funding and liquidity across household, business, banking and national-accounting contexts.
Study the connected family:
1. Output and intermediate inputs
2. Economic value, usefulness, exchange value and welfare
3. Costs and prices
4. Sales, revenue, income and receipts
5. Intermediate consumption and value added
6. Profit and its different definitions
7. Productivity and productive capacity
8. Capital formation, depreciation, maintenance and replacement

Use examples from households, farms, transporters, traders, retailers, service providers, banks, businesses and government where appropriate. Preserve the distinction between an illustrative example and empirical evidence.

### Phase 2 — The foundational financial vocabulary
Study money, wealth, capital, assets, liabilities and equity/net worth as a tightly connected comparison set. Explicitly distinguish:
- money used for payment from broader wealth and asset holdings;
- financial claims from underlying real assets where consolidation matters;
- capital as productive resources, accounting capital, or financing depending on context;
- a stock measured at a point in time from income, expenditure, investment and other flows over a period;
- gross values from net values after specified deductions;
- ownership, control, liquidity, valuation and availability.

This phase should become a conceptual anchor for later work on credit, investment, institutions, and global financial structures. Do not try to compute a single global total during this phase.

### Phase 3 — Credit, debt, banking and financial intermediation
Proceed through lending, borrowing, bank balance sheets, deposits, central-bank money, interest, collateral, leverage, debt service, refinancing, default, and financial intermediation. Explain how claims and obligations connect counterparties and why one party's financial asset may correspond to another party's liability.

### Phase 4 — Saving, investment, funding and capital allocation
Distinguish household saving from investment in national accounts; financial-asset trades from investment that creates or improves productive capacity; funding commitments from actual disbursement and deployment; and expected returns from realized results. Connect these concepts to business finance, institutional investors and capital allocation.

### Phase 5 — Prices, markets, inflation, interest and liquidity
Study price formation, competition, market power, inflation, real versus nominal values, interest rates, exchange mechanisms, market liquidity, funding liquidity, and solvency. Preserve differences between concepts that sound similar but describe different constraints or balance-sheet conditions.

### Phase 6 — National accounting, public finance and economic structure
Connect production and value added to GDP, national income, expenditure components, government budgets, public debt, taxes, sectoral structure, employment and household welfare. Separate conceptual explanations from Workstream B's operational indicator definitions and official series.

### Phase 7 — International finance, institutions and networks
Extend into currencies, cross-border flows, financial markets, institutional capital pools, supply chains, ownership/control, infrastructure, natural resources, digital/intangible activity, risk, and distribution of power. Use the Global Financial System Exploration Map as a complementary cross-workstream index; this roadmap does not replace it.

### Phase 8 — Synthesis and applied connections
Create comparison tables and relationship maps only after the relevant concepts have been explained. Identify which concepts affect Workstreams B, C, D or E, and send useful questions or findings across workstreams. Do not force every concept into a business idea, indicator, causal claim, or trading hypothesis.

## 5. Standard for concept notes

Use the existing `workstream-a/README.md` structure as the starting template. Adapt it to each concept; not every section will be relevant.

For each substantial concept, aim to document:

1. **Plain-language definition:** What the term means in the context being discussed.
2. **Real-world referent:** What activity, object, relationship, claim, institution or process the term describes.
3. **Context of use:** Everyday language, economic theory, accounting, finance/markets, legal/institutional usage, or national accounting.
4. **Boundaries:** What it includes and excludes; common edge cases and exceptions.
5. **Types and variants:** Different forms that should not be collapsed.
6. **Mechanism:** How it works, including relevant counterparties, incentives, constraints and flows.
7. **Relationships:** Upstream/downstream connections, dependencies, feedback and relevant time dimension.
8. **Distinctions:** Closely related and commonly confused terms.
9. **Worked examples:** Concrete, transparent examples; label hypothetical numbers and assumptions.
10. **Measurement:** Units, denominators, stock/flow status, gross/net status, possible proxies and limitations; defer formal indicator specifications to Workstream B.
11. **Evidence status:** Separate established definitions, sourced claims, inference, illustration, hypotheses and unresolved questions.
12. **Practical implications:** Household, business, institutional, national or cross-border implications where relevant.
13. **Cross-workstream links:** Connections to measurement, monitoring, footprint investigation or opportunity assessment, without forcing a connection.
14. **Questions and change history:** Record what remains uncertain and material corrections.

Foundational concepts such as money, wealth, capital, production, credit, value added and productivity should receive deeper treatment than narrow specialist terms. Technical claims about accounting or institutions should be checked against reliable primary or authoritative sources before being stated as settled.

## 6. Research and reasoning safeguards

- Start with what happens in reality, then explain the technical label and its measurement.
- Never use money, wealth, capital, income, revenue, profit, cash flow, credit or liquidity as synonyms.
- State the accounting or theoretical context when a term has multiple legitimate meanings.
- Distinguish stocks from flows, levels from rates of change, and gross from net values.
- Identify both sides of financial claims and obligations where relevant; avoid double-counting.
- Do not add figures with incompatible definitions, time periods, units or consolidation boundaries.
- Distinguish a transaction price from total economic value, customer benefit, profit and measured value added.
- Do not treat a correlation, plausible mechanism, incentive, trace or example as proof of causation or intent.
- Label hypothetical calculations and examples. Do not present them as current market data.
- Use primary/official sources for definitions and measurement rules where feasible; record publication dates, coverage, limitations and unresolved discrepancies.
- Keep conceptual learning separate from formal indicator design, while explicitly recording when Workstream B needs to operationalize a concept.
- Use Workstream E implications when justified, but do not force every concept to generate a commercial idea.
- Preserve existing project architecture; this roadmap is part of Workstream A, not a new workstream.
- Revise this roadmap if actual investigation reveals gaps, redundancy or a better learning order. Record material scope changes in the decision register.

## 7. Relationship to the wider Lifeconomist project

- **A — Deep Conceptual Foundations:** Defines and connects concepts in reality-first terms.
- **B — Measurement:** Turns selected concepts into operational definitions, formulas, series and quality checks.
- **C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** Uses observations and indicators to describe conditions and investigate changes without premature classification.
- **D — Footprint Intelligence:** Investigates observable traces, entities, relationships and dependencies where relevant.
- **E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application:** Examines practical applications and opportunities using evidence, affordable tests and explicit uncertainty.

The workstreams interact bidirectionally. A concept may expose a measurement problem; observed data may reveal a conceptual ambiguity; an investigation may expose missing relationships; and a practical test may show that an assumption needs revision. None of these connections means every concept must feed every workstream.

## 9. Completion criteria

This roadmap is not “completed” merely because all headings have notes. Progress is adequate when:
- foundational concepts have clear, context-specific explanations;
- commonly confused terms are compared explicitly;
- examples and calculations are transparent and not mistaken for empirical evidence;
- important mechanisms and relationships are explained without overclaiming causation;
- measurement boundaries and limitations are recorded;
- unresolved questions and authoritative source needs are visible;
- notes link coherently to one another and to the relevant workstreams;
- later investigations can use the concepts without repeatedly confusing their meanings.

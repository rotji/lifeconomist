# Research Methodology

## Research and development workstreams

The project uses four distinct but interconnected workstreams as its research and development framework. They are not isolated projects or a rigid one-way sequence; each may inform and improve the others.

### Workstream A — Deep Conceptual Foundations

**Purpose:** Understand the underlying meaning of economic, financial, institutional, technological, biological, and other relevant concepts.

Investigate what concepts represent in reality, how their mechanisms work, why they matter, how they connect to other systems, and what consequences they produce. Examine assumptions, incentives, feedback, observability, limitations, and ways a concept may mislead.

Go beyond surface definitions and textbook summaries. Where appropriate, begin with relatable situations—such as farmers producing and selling maize, parents preparing meals, restaurants selling meals, businesses borrowing, workers earning wages, households paying rent, or traders responding to price changes—before introducing complex terminology.

**Primary outputs:** Clear conceptual explanations, mechanisms, assumptions, relationships, and unresolved questions, preserved in durable research notes and terminology records.

**Initial planned subject:** The concept of production, beginning with everyday examples. This is a plan, not a claim that the investigation is complete.

### Workstream B — Measurement

**Purpose:** Define how the economic model's core indicators and supporting measurements are operationalized.

Specify definitions, formulas, units, authoritative data sources and series, observation periods, frequency, historical coverage, revisions, data vintages, comparability, limitations, missing observations, uncertainty, and validation requirements. Test measurement choices against historical evidence and explain what each indicator measures and cannot establish.

The model's six core indicators remain:

1. Real GDP growth.
2. Labor productivity growth.
3. Private-sector credit growth.
4. Private-sector credit relative to GDP.
5. Debt-service burden.
6. Productive investment.

The active measurement task is the draft specification for Indicator 1, Real GDP Growth, in `03-measurement/01-real-gdp-growth.md`. Its unresolved questions remain open; the workstream framework does not approve the draft specification or resolve those questions.

### Workstream C — Evidence-Based Monitoring

**Purpose:** Monitor current and historical economic developments, gather relevant evidence, and investigate how developments may affect the economic system.

Connect observed developments to the project's concepts, measurements, mechanisms, and possible economic consequences. Maintain an evidence trail and source register; investigate possible causes and transmission; connect observations across indicators, sectors, and institutions; compare alternative explanations; distinguish verified facts, interpretations, hypotheses, unresolved questions, and unsupported claims; record uncertainty and contradictory evidence; and assess potential implications and scenarios.

Monitoring does not automatically produce an economic-state classification. Formal classification remains subject to adequate measurement definitions, historical testing, and documented decision rules.

### Workstream D — Footprint Intelligence

**Purpose:** Develop a systematic, reusable capability for discovering, lawfully collecting where appropriate, assessing, connecting, and investigating observable traces that may reveal activities, capabilities, relationships, dependencies, changes, and emerging developments across real-world systems.

D asks: **What observable traces may reveal an activity, relationship, capability, change, or possible development, and how can those traces be investigated and verified?**

Footprints may be digital or physical. Sources may include corporate announcements, hiring activity, public filings and procurement notices; government publications, regulations, permits and public records; financial disclosures and documented capital flows; satellite and geospatial observations, infrastructure changes and environmental evidence; shipping, logistics, trade and supply-chain records; scientific publications, patents, technical disclosures and research activity; and biological, agricultural, ecological, health-related and demographic sources where lawful and appropriate. This is an illustrative, open-ended set.

Use two complementary approaches:

- **Top-down investigation:** Start with a question, system, entity, event, pressure, or suspected development, then identify traces that could help investigate it.
- **Bottom-up discovery:** Start with an observation, trace, pattern, change, or anomaly, then investigate what it may represent, which entities or systems may be involved, and whether independent evidence supports an interpretation.

These approaches may interact iteratively. Assess sources; identify entities; map relationships and dependencies; investigate patterns and anomalies; examine mechanisms and transmission pathways; seek corroboration; consider alternative explanations; and record uncertainty. A trace is not proof of the event or interpretation it appears to suggest. Distinguish direct observation from inference and retain provenance, dates, limitations, and contradictory evidence where available. Respect lawful access, privacy, consent, and research ethics.

**Primary outputs:** Trace inventories, source assessments, entity and relationship maps, change and anomaly records, evidence-linked hypotheses, corroboration results, alternative explanations, and intelligence findings with explicit uncertainty.

Footprint Intelligence is a reusable investigation capability, not an economic indicator and not another name for economic monitoring. It may be applied in domains such as the Global Economic State Transition Model, Global Power Observatory, geographic and physical exposure, corporate and industrial systems, finance and capital flows, supply chains, biological and bioeconomic systems, environment and ecology, technology and science, or other future domain-intelligence work. These are possible application areas, not separate workstreams or products that must be created now. Do not force every finding into the economic model; transfer relevant, sufficiently assessed evidence to Workstream C or another appropriate project.

The detailed proposed architecture, including Biological Intelligence, Bioeconomic Intelligence, Biological Power and Governance Intelligence, cross-domain mechanisms, and ethical safeguards, is documented in `FOOTPRINT-INTELLIGENCE-ARCHITECTURE.md`. Workstream D is formally designated, but this architecture describes proposed capabilities, not an implemented or validated system.

### Workstream boundaries and feedback

- **A asks:** What does this concept mean in reality, and how does its underlying mechanism work?
- **B asks:** How can this economic concept or condition be measured reliably?
- **C asks:** What is happening in the economic system, what evidence supports that assessment, and what might it mean?
- **D asks:** What observable traces can reveal an activity, relationship, capability, change, or possible development, and how can those traces be investigated and verified?

For a possible factory expansion, A explains production capacity, investment, employment, and their mechanisms; B defines measurements needed to assess changes in investment, output, or employment; C monitors relevant economic data and documented developments; D investigates traces such as public hiring notices, permits, procurement records, company announcements, shipping activity, or suitable satellite observations. A trace alone does not prove the expansion. D seeks corroboration, considers alternatives, and records uncertainty.

A, B, C, and D remain distinct even when one investigation uses all four. C focuses on economic monitoring and evidence-based interpretation within the economic model; D investigates footprints across relevant domains whether or not they immediately relate to an economic indicator. C may request a D investigation into an unexplained development; D may pass a potentially relevant finding, evidence, uncertainty, and limitations to C; and C may use sources that do not require a dedicated D investigation. These are optional feedback loops, not a compulsory one-way pipeline. Monitoring can reveal measurement gaps, measurement can require conceptual clarification, conceptual work can improve evidence interpretation, and footprint investigations can raise questions for any of the other workstreams. The workstreams may progress concurrently; none must be entirely complete before another can operate.

The same principle extends to Workstream E. **A–E operate concurrently and feed back into one another.** Work can move from A, B, C, or D toward E when understanding or evidence suggests an opportunity. It can also start in E: a trade result, investment outcome, business experiment, customer objection, loss, gain, criticism, missed opportunity, or surprising result may reveal a gap in concepts, measurement, monitoring, investigation, or decision-making. Route that question back to the relevant workstream(s), investigate it, and return the learning to E when useful. The starting point is determined by the real question, not by a fixed sequence.

This does not mean every A concept must produce a commercial opportunity, or that every E activity must wait for A–D to finish. When working on a substantial A concept, consider a parallel E question: *Does this understanding suggest a practical opportunity worth testing?* If no credible opportunity is apparent, continue the conceptual investigation without forcing one. When an E opportunity is pursued, record actual outcomes and feedback—including gains, losses, criticism, failed hypotheses, and unexpected effects—and use them to improve the whole system. Real-world use is a source of learning, not automatic proof that the original explanation was correct.

### Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application

**Purpose:** Translate knowledge, measurements, monitored developments, and investigated evidence into opportunities to create or capture economic value. E evaluates potential trading opportunities, investments, businesses, and commercial intelligence products or services. Its central question is: *Given what we understand about the world and what we can verify, where might there be an opportunity to create or capture economic value—and what evidence would justify acting on it?*

E is an application and decision-support workstream, not a promise that research will produce profits. A plausible idea is not a validated opportunity, and a validated opportunity can still fail. Its detailed charter and lifecycle are in [WORKSTREAM-E-ARCHITECTURE.md](WORKSTREAM-E-ARCHITECTURE.md).

**Initial opportunity domains:**
- **E1 — Trading:** Investigate testable market hypotheses, including costs, time horizons, downside scenarios, and invalidation conditions.
- **E2 — Investing:** Evaluate assets, businesses, sectors, and structural themes with attention to valuation, risk, liquidity, and thesis-invalidating evidence.
- **E3 — Business:** Investigate unmet needs and customer problems, willingness to pay, alternatives, competition, delivery costs, and feasibility.
- **E4 — Intelligence products and services:** Explore whether research capabilities can become useful paid reports, monitoring, alerts, due diligence, or data products. These are candidate directions, not commitments.

**Relationship with A–D:** A explains concepts and mechanisms; B establishes defensible measurements; C monitors and interprets economic evidence; D investigates observable traces across relevant domains; E uses relevant findings and additional market/customer evidence to identify, test, compare, and evaluate possible actions. This relationship is iterative, not a compulsory pipeline. E may reveal new questions for A–D; not every finding must produce an opportunity, and not every E investigation requires all other workstreams.

**Default opportunity lifecycle:** observation → explanation → opportunity hypothesis → evidence and verification → economic evaluation → low-cost test → decision (proceed, revise, monitor, defer, or reject) → outcome review and feedback. Adapt this process to the opportunity. Record assumptions, source quality, alternative explanations, costs, resources or capital, uncertainty, downside, time horizon, and invalidation conditions. Reject or defer candidates when evidence or feasibility is inadequate.

**Initial operating posture:** The long-term design is broad, while the practical starting posture favors affordable experiments. Trading hypotheses can first be assessed with historical analysis and paper trading; business ideas can first be tested through a narrowly defined customer problem and a small sample service. These are prudent options, not permanent restrictions. Do not build complex software or automate before a real decision need and a useful method are demonstrated.

**Evidence discipline:** An economic trend is not automatically a profitable trade or business. A price movement does not itself prove mispricing; an announcement or footprint does not establish commercial success; and customer interest does not prove willingness to pay. Consider legal, ethical, privacy, regulatory, and operational constraints. Evaluate downside alongside potential benefit, never imply guaranteed returns, and record failures as well as successes. No specific trade, investment, business, or product is approved merely by establishing E.

## The Reality-First Principle

Every economic, financial, or system concept must remain connected to observable reality and ordinary human life. Technical terminology, formulas, theories, statistics, financial models, and abstract frameworks describe real-world activities and relationships; they must not replace them. The aim is to understand what a concept represents, why it exists, how it works, what mechanisms drive it, how it connects to a wider system, how it can be measured, and what conclusions the evidence justifies—not merely to memorize definitions or vocabulary.

### Default conceptual investigation sequence

For concepts studied under Workstream A, use this sequence by default:

1. **Everyday reality:** Begin with a familiar situation that illustrates the phenomenon.
2. **Plain-language explanation:** Explain what is happening without relying on unexplained technical terminology.
3. **Underlying meaning:** Investigate what the concept represents, what problem it addresses, and which real-world relationships it describes.
4. **Mechanisms and causation:** Explain how it works, what influences it, what it influences, and what feedback relationships may exist.
5. **Technical terminology:** Introduce accepted technical terms and precise definitions after establishing an intuitive understanding.
6. **Measurement and evidence:** Explain how the phenomenon can be observed or measured, what measurements capture, and what they leave out.
7. **Assumptions and limitations:** Identify the concept's boundaries and limitations, and conditions under which an explanation may fail.
8. **Connections:** Relate it to other concepts and the wider economic, financial, institutional, or physical system.
9. **Practical implications:** Explain why it matters to households, workers, traders, farmers, businesses, governments, investors, or other relevant actors.
10. **Evidence boundary:** Distinguish established facts, illustrative examples, hypotheses, inferences, and conclusions requiring further verification.

Adapt this sequence where appropriate. It is not a mechanical checklist, and irrelevant sections should not be forced into an investigation.

### Examples and explanatory accuracy

Use relatable examples throughout the project, not only in introductions. Initial reference examples include a farmer growing and selling maize; a parent cooking a meal for the family without payment; and a restaurant preparing a similar meal and selling it to a customer. Return to these examples when useful for production, economic output, GDP, market transactions, unpaid work, prices, revenue, costs, and value added.

As work advances, use further everyday examples involving household budgets, borrowing and lending, interest, rent, wages, savings, business inventory, transport costs, taxes, government spending, exchange rates, debt, collateral, investment, and financial markets. Choose examples that illuminate the mechanism being studied and explain the connection to the technical concept rather than assuming it is obvious.

Plain language must not sacrifice technical accuracy. Explain important technical terms accessibly when first used and preserve meaningful distinctions between related concepts. An example may illustrate a mechanism, but it is not evidence that the mechanism caused a real event. Do not present assumptions, hypotheses, or disputed explanations as established facts. Explain what a statistic measures and cannot establish; for causal claims, identify what evidence would be needed. Where useful, explain a concept first in everyday language and then with the technical precision required for rigorous analysis.

### Application and review rules

This is a methodological standard, not merely a writing preference for Workstream A. Apply it to Workstreams A, B, C, and D; the Global Economic State Transition Model; related intelligence systems; financial analysis; research documents; and future models developed within the project. For example, analysis of real GDP growth should connect the formula to what production represents, why GDP includes certain activities, how price changes affect measured output, why nominal and real GDP differ, and what a growth rate can and cannot tell us about people's lives. Financial-market analysis should likewise connect abstract terms to actual transactions, claims, obligations, assets, cash flows, incentives, and decisions.

Apply these standing rules:

- **Rule A — Reality connection:** Every important concept must be explainable through its real-world meaning, mechanism, or a suitable concrete example.
- **Rule B — Evidence discipline:** A clear and relatable explanation is not automatically a verified explanation of a real event. Keep observations, interpretations, hypotheses, and verified conclusions distinct.

When reviewing important work, ask: **“If we remove the technical vocabulary, can we explain what is actually happening in the real world? If we restore the technical vocabulary, does the explanation remain accurate? And can we distinguish what we understand from what the evidence establishes?”** If not, the analysis needs further work.

## Research record
For each investigation, record:
- Research question and why it matters
- Evidence required
- Sources and source quality
- Publication date and observation period
- Method, definitions, and assumptions
- Findings and alternative explanations
- Limitations and uncertainty
- Conclusion status
- Next action

## Conclusion labels
- **Established fact:** directly supported by reliable evidence for the stated scope.
- **Supported interpretation:** evidence supports this explanation, but it is not uniquely proven.
- **Hypothesis:** plausible explanation that needs testing.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is insufficient or conflicting.
- **Contradicted:** reliable evidence conflicts with the claim.

## Data discipline
- Distinguish nominal values from inflation-adjusted values.
- Distinguish stocks measured at a point in time from flows measured over a period.
- Record units, currency, seasonal adjustment, frequency, and observation dates.
- Distinguish the publication date from the period the data describe.
- Record revisions and methodology changes where known.
- Mark missing or unreliable data; do not silently fill gaps.
- Avoid cross-country comparisons unless definitions and coverage are sufficiently comparable.

## Causal discipline
A plausible mechanism is not proof of causation. Consider timing, alternative explanations, confounders, policy changes, and strength of evidence.

## State-classification discipline
Do not classify an economic state from one indicator alone. Do not invent thresholds. Develop candidate rules only after definitions and historical behavior have been examined. Record confirmation and invalidation evidence.

## Change control
When a definition or conclusion changes, record the previous version, new version, reason, motivating evidence, and implications for earlier analyses.

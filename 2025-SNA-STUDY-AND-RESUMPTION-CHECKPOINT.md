### Stage 2 — Chapter 4, Lesson 1: Understanding stocks and flows

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-18, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover PDF. The lesson covered §§4.1–4.5, §4.11, and §§4.14–4.16 as starting points. This is one lesson within Chapter 4, not completion of the chapter.

**What the standard establishes (initial reading):**

- §4.1 describes the sequence of economic accounts as measuring stocks and changes in economic value and identifying the institutional units or sectors with claims on that value.
- §4.2 distinguishes stocks, measured at a point in time, from flows, measured over a period. It identifies balance sheets as the place for stocks; current accounts cover production, income and use of income; accumulation accounts cover capital, financial, and other changes in assets and liabilities between balance sheets.
- §§4.3–4.4 explain that a complete, consistent system captures all changes in economic value between stock measures at two points in time. Stocks at the beginning and end of an accounting period are connected by transactions and other flows during the period.
- §4.5 introduces assets as stores of value associated with economic benefits accruing to an economic owner over time, and liabilities as obligations of debtors to creditors under specified circumstances. The complete asset boundary and detailed rules remain open.
- §4.11 notes that accounts and tables record economic actions and events during a period and their effects on stocks of assets and liabilities between the beginning and end.
- §4.14 notes that entries are measured in monetary terms, sometimes using actual payments and sometimes estimates referenced to monetary values.
- §§4.15–4.16 introduce accounting-period considerations and the importance of consistent valuation, timing, classification and grouping. These rules have not yet been studied in full.

**Plain-language teaching examples (illustrative, not SNA data):**

- A balance-sheet example used cash of ₦2m, bank deposits of ₦3m, a delivery vehicle of ₦8m and inventory of ₦4m: total assets ₦17m; a bank liability of ₦5m; net worth ₦12m. This illustrated a position at a particular date.
- A January activity example listed sales of ₦5m, wages of ₦800,000, inventory purchases of ₦2m, a new loan of ₦1m and loan repayment of ₦200,000. These were examples of events over a period, not a complete set of national-account entries. Sales must not be conflated with cash receipts, income, or net-worth change.
- A simplified asset reconciliation showed opening machinery value 20m + acquisition 5m − consumption of fixed capital 2m + revaluation 1m = closing value 24m. This was illustrative arithmetic only; the formal SNA classification of the different changes is still to be studied.

**Core distinction reinforced:** A stock is a position at a point in time; a flow records an event or change over a period. The opening and closing balance sheets show positions, while the flow accounts explain the changes between them. The accounting system distinguishes current accounts from accumulation accounts; not every flow changes every individual asset or liability directly.

**Knowledge-check clarification — Question 3:** If a vehicle's recorded value rises because market prices rise, while the business does not buy or improve the vehicle, the increase is not a purchase transaction. It is a change in the asset's value arising from a price change (a revaluation). In the SNA's accounting structure, this belongs among other flows, specifically revaluation, rather than transactions. The asset's closing value can therefore be higher even though no acquisition occurred during the period. The formal distinctions among revaluation, other volume changes and transactions are the subject of the next lesson and must be checked against the relevant paragraphs before treating the taxonomy as fully studied.

**Study limitation:** This lesson introduced the structure and basic relationship of stocks and flows. It did not yet systematically study transactions versus other flows, the complete reconciliation of balance sheets, economic ownership, time of recording, or the full valuation rules. Chapter 4 remains in progress.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 2: Transactions versus other flows

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-18, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, particularly §§4.6–4.7 and §4.18. This lesson builds on Lesson 1 but does not complete Chapter 4.

**What the standard establishes (initial reading):**

- §4.6 describes economic flows as reflecting the creation, transformation, exchange, transfer or extinction of economic value. Flows involve changes in the volume, composition or value of assets and liabilities.
- §4.7 divides economic flows into **transactions** and **other flows**.
- A **transaction** is an economic interaction between institutional units by mutual agreement or through the operation of law. The SNA also treats certain actions within an institutional unit as transactions where analytically useful, including cases where a unit acts in two different capacities.
- **Other flows** are changes in the value of assets and liabilities that are not transactions. They comprise **other changes in the volume of assets and liabilities** and **revaluations**.
- §4.18 locates transactions in the relevant flow accounts, while other flows are recorded in the two accumulation accounts specifically concerned with other changes in volume and revaluations. The lesson introduced this account placement; the full account structure and recording rules remain to be studied.

**Plain-language teaching examples (illustrative, not SNA data):**

1. A business purchases a new generator from another business: this is a transaction between institutional units.
2. A warehouse is destroyed by an unexpected fire: the loss is an other flow, specifically an other change in the volume of assets, rather than a purchase or sale.
3. Land increases in recorded value because market prices rise, with no purchase or sale: the increase is a revaluation, not a transaction.

**Knowledge-check result and clarification:** The three examples above were used in a short knowledge check. The intended classifications were: (1) transaction; (2) other flow — other change in volume; and (3) other flow — revaluation. The key lesson is that not every change in an asset's recorded value is caused by a transaction. Some changes arise from events that alter the volume of assets, while others arise from price changes.

**Study limitations:** This was an introductory lesson on the distinction and top-level categories. It did not systematically study the detailed classification of other changes in volume, the full range of revaluation cases, economic ownership, time of recording, valuation rules, or the complete sequence of accumulation accounts. The warehouse-fire example is a teaching illustration; actual classification depends on the SNA's detailed rules and facts of the event. Chapter 4 remains in progress.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 3: Other changes in the volume of assets and liabilities

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-18, using the official UN 2025 SNA White Cover text, Chapter 4, §§4.73–4.81. This lesson introduces the categories and selected examples; it does not complete the detailed treatment or Chapter 4.

**Source-based structure:**

- §4.73 defines other flows as changes in the value of assets and liabilities not due to transactions. They comprise other changes in the volume of assets and liabilities and revaluations. It also notes a restriction in the external accounts context.
- §4.74 distinguishes the two relevant accounts: the other changes in volume account records changes associated with changes in the quantity or physical characteristics of assets; the revaluation account records changes in asset and liability values and net worth due only to changes in the level and structure of prices.
- §4.75 introduces three main categories of other changes in volume.
- §§4.76–4.78 cover the first category: appearance and disappearance of assets and liabilities other than through transactions. Examples include naturally occurring mineral and energy resources; recognition of valuables with significant or special value that were not previously recorded in balance sheets; creditor write-offs; monetization and demonetization of gold bullion; distinctions between debt forgiveness and debt cancellation; assumption of debt following activation of guarantees and debt rescheduling; and certain crypto-assets without a corresponding liability. The paragraph distinguishes some cases that are transactions from those recorded as other changes in volume.
- §4.79 covers effects of externalities and disasters, including uncompensated removal of an asset and destruction through natural disaster or war. It distinguishes these from transactions reflecting normal rates of loss or damage, such as depreciation, depletion or changes in inventories.
- §4.80 covers changes arising from reclassification of institutional units among sectors, changes in institutional-unit structure, or reclassification of assets and liabilities. The example is an unincorporated enterprise becoming a quasi-corporation, with associated assets and liabilities moving from the household sector to the non-financial corporations sector.
- §4.81 covers changes in the status of existing financial claims and liabilities arising when individuals change residence from one economy to another. These are treated as other changes in volume rather than transactions.

**Plain-language teaching examples (illustrative, not SNA data):**

1. A previously unrecorded valuable object is recognized in the balance sheet because it is found to have significant or special value. Its recognition may be an appearance of an asset, not a new purchase. The detailed facts and criteria matter.
2. A creditor writes off a claim. The treatment is not automatically the same as debt forgiveness: the standard distinguishes debt forgiveness, which is recorded as a transaction in the described non-commercial setting, from some debt cancellations that may be recorded as other changes in volume. In commercial settings, absent specific information, the standard allows debt cancellation to be treated as an other change in volume.
3. A major disaster destroys assets. The destruction may be an other change in volume; normal expected losses are treated differently and may be reflected in transactions such as depreciation, depletion or changes in inventories.
4. An unincorporated enterprise becomes a quasi-corporation and its assets and liabilities are reclassified between institutional sectors. This is a reclassification, not a sale of those assets.
5. An individual's change of residence between economies changes the residence classification of existing financial claims and liabilities. The classification change is not itself a purchase or sale.

**Important clarification to the earlier teaching example:** The previous lesson used a warehouse destroyed by an unexpected fire as a simplified illustration. The official text's §4.79 discusses externalities and disasters, including natural disasters and war, and distinguishes exceptional destruction from normal expected losses. A fire example must therefore be assessed using the facts and the relevant classification rules; not every loss by fire should automatically be assigned the same treatment.

**Paragraph-reference correction:** The preceding live lesson referred to §§4.74–4.81 for some of these details. Verification against the official White Cover text establishes the relevant sequence as §§4.73–4.81: §4.73 defines other flows; §4.74 distinguishes the two accounts; §4.75 introduces the three categories; and §§4.76–4.81 explain the categories and residence-status case. This checkpoint records the verified paragraph sequence.

**Study limitations:** This was an introductory survey of the categories and selected cases, not a complete study of all other changes in volume, their valuation, detailed asset-specific treatments, or Chapter 13's dedicated other-changes-in-assets-and-liabilities accounts. Revaluations and holding gains/losses have not yet been studied in detail. Economic ownership, time of recording, valuation and the full accounting sequence remain open. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 4: Revaluations (holding gains and holding losses)

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official UN *System of National Accounts 2025 — White Cover Version*. The lesson began with Chapter 4, §§4.82–4.83 and used Chapter 3, §§3.117–3.122 to clarify the revaluation account and the distinction between nominal, neutral and real holding gains and losses. It does not complete Chapter 4.

**What the standard establishes (initial reading):**

- §4.82 explains that positive or negative **nominal holding gains** accrue to owners of assets and liabilities as a result of price changes during the accounting period. The standard prefers the term *holding gain* to *capital gain*, because the gain accrues from holding an asset or liability over time without transforming it. The concept applies to fixed assets, land, financial assets and inventories, including work-in-progress. A holding gain can accrue over any part of the accounting period; it is not restricted to assets appearing on both the opening and closing balance sheets.
- §4.83 explains that nominal holding gains depend on changes in asset and liability prices over time, measured by reference to prices at which assets may be sold in the market. Nominal holding gains can be decomposed into **neutral holding gains** and **real holding gains**.
- §§3.117–3.119 describe the revaluation account. It records holding gains and losses; the nominal amount captures the full change in value due to price changes since the beginning of the period or since the asset or liability entered or left the stock. For assets, a positive revaluation is a holding gain. For financial liabilities, an increase in the liability's value is a holding loss for the debtor; a decrease is a holding gain. The account's balancing item is changes in net worth due to nominal holding gains and losses.
- §§3.120–3.122 distinguish the neutral component, which reflects the general price level and preserves purchasing power, from the real component, which is the difference between nominal and neutral holding gains or losses and reflects changes in relative prices.

**Plain-language explanation:**

- A **nominal holding gain** is the total price-related increase in an asset's value while it is held; a nominal holding loss is the corresponding decrease. It is not a purchase, sale, production activity or physical improvement.
- A **neutral holding gain** is the amount that would simply keep the asset's value in line with the general price level. It does not represent an increase in purchasing power relative to the economy-wide price level.
- A **real holding gain or loss** is the amount left after removing the neutral component. It shows whether the asset's price moved more or less than the general price level.

**Illustrative arithmetic (not observed data and not a complete compilation method):**

Suppose land is initially valued at ₦10 million. During the period, the general price level rises by 10%, while the land's market price rises by 15%, giving it a closing value of ₦11.5 million.

- Nominal holding gain: ₦11.5m − ₦10m = **₦1.5m**.
- Neutral holding gain: 10% × ₦10m = **₦1.0m**.
- Real holding gain: ₦1.5m − ₦1.0m = **₦0.5m**.

If the nominal holding gain were ₦800,000 while the neutral holding gain were ₦1.5 million, the real holding result would be a **₦700,000 real holding loss**. The asset's nominal price would have risen, but by less than the general price level.

**Asset/liability direction clarified:**

- Asset value rises because of a price change → holding gain for the asset owner.
- Asset value falls because of a price change → holding loss for the asset owner.
- Liability value rises because of a price change → holding loss for the debtor.
- Liability value falls because of a price change → holding gain for the debtor.

The cause of the change matters. A debt repayment is a transaction, not a revaluation. The liability direction above concerns a price-related change in its value.

**Key conceptual result:** A nominal price increase does not automatically mean a real gain. If an asset's price and the general price level rise by the same proportion, the real holding gain is zero in the simplified illustration. If the asset price rises by less than the general price level, a nominal gain can coexist with a real holding loss.

**Study limitations:** This was an introductory lesson on revaluations, not a full study of the revaluation account or Chapter 4. The detailed calculation of neutral and real holding gains, price indices, asset-specific valuation, financial-instrument cases, continuous accrual, economic ownership, timing of acquisition and disposal, and the complete accounting entries remain to be studied. The numerical examples are teaching illustrations only. No Nigeria implementation, dataset calculation, or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 5: Holding gains, economic ownership and timing

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, particularly §§4.182–4.183 and §§4.218–4.219.

**What the standard establishes (initial reading):**

- §4.182 states that holding gains and losses accrue continuously and apply to non-financial and financial assets and liabilities. In general, they are estimated by deducting from the total change in asset value the changes attributable to transactions and other changes in volume.
- §4.183 explains that holding gains and losses are calculated between two relevant points: the beginning of the accounting period or the time an asset/liability is acquired or incurred, and the end of the period or the time it is sold or extinguished. It also emphasizes matching holding gains and losses on financial assets and their counterpart liabilities.
- §4.218 specifies the practical time points for computing nominal holding gains/losses: from the beginning of the period, acquisition of ownership, or production of an asset, to the end of the period, relinquishment of ownership, or consumption in production.
- §4.219 explains that contractual commitments are treated as contingent until a party performs its obligation, such as transferring ownership, providing a service, or providing labour or capital. A unit can incur holding gains/losses only on assets or liabilities over which it has economic ownership. Signing a contract alone does not automatically give the buyer a holding gain on the asset before the relevant performance and ownership change.

**Plain-language explanation and examples (illustrative, not SNA data):**

1. A buyer acquires land for ₦10m in March and sells it for ₦12m in October. If no other relevant changes affect the value, the buyer experiences an illustrative ₦2m nominal holding gain during the period of ownership. The land may be absent from both the opening and closing balance sheets, yet a holding gain can still accrue while it is owned.
2. A buyer signs a contract to acquire land, but the seller has not yet performed the relevant obligation or transferred economic ownership. A market-price increase before that point does not automatically create a holding gain for the buyer merely because the contract was signed.
3. For financial assets and liabilities, the SNA emphasizes consistency: holding gains on an asset should be matched by holding losses on the corresponding liability, and vice versa, where the positions are counterparts.

**Key lesson:** Holding gains are linked to the period of economic ownership, not simply to whether an asset appears in opening and closing balance sheets or to the date a contract is signed.

**Study limitations:** This was an introductory lesson, not a complete study of economic ownership or all timing rules. The precise economic-ownership criteria, complex contracts, leases, repurchase agreements, transaction timing, accrual accounting and instrument-specific cases remain open. The land example is illustrative arithmetic only. Chapter 4 is not complete; no Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 6: Time of recording and accrual accounting

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.185–4.193.

**What the standard establishes (initial reading):**

- §§4.185–4.188 explain that activities and transactions can have several economically relevant moments, including contract/signing, delivery or performance, payment becoming due, and actual payment. The timing basis selected affects the period in which flows are recorded.
- §4.189 notes that recording every successive stage would overburden the integrated framework. The standard therefore distinguishes cash, due-for-payment, commitment and accrual bases, and recognizes other timing bases used in some source data. The integrated framework recommends accrual recording.
- §4.190 explains that cash accounting records cash payments when they occur, but payment timing may differ significantly from the underlying economic activity and cash accounting cannot capture non-monetary flows generally.
- §4.191 explains due-for-payment recording: flows are recorded when payment becomes due without additional charges or penalties, alongside actual payments; a receivable/payable bridges the interval between due date and actual payment.
- §4.192 defines accrual accounting as recording flows when economic value is created, transformed, exchanged, transferred or extinguished. Ownership-changing flows are recorded when ownership changes; services when provided; output when products are created; and intermediate consumption when materials and supplies are used. Later cash settlement is recorded separately, with a financial claim/payable bridging the timing gap.
- §4.193 makes economic ownership central to the timing of transactions in goods, non-financial assets and financial assets. Economic ownership involves the practical transfer of risks, rewards, rights and responsibilities. Legal ownership generally changes with economic ownership, but not always; financial leases and repurchase agreements illustrate why the distinction matters.

**Plain-language teaching example (illustrative, not SNA data):**

A business orders goods on 15 December, receives them and acquires economic ownership on 20 December, payment becomes due on 10 January, and cash is paid on 15 January.

- Commitment basis: 15 December.
- Accrual basis: 20 December, assuming that is when economic ownership changes.
- Due-for-payment basis: 10 January.
- Cash basis: 15 January.

The example illustrates why the same underlying purchase can appear in different periods under different recording bases. The SNA's accrual approach records the underlying economic event rather than simply following the cash movement.

**Key lesson:** Cash paid/received, payment due, contract commitment and economic event are not necessarily simultaneous. The SNA generally uses accrual accounting so economic activity is recorded in the period in which it occurs.

**Study limitations:** This was an introductory lesson, not a complete treatment of the detailed timing rules. Specific timing for goods, services, distributive transactions, taxes, transfers, financial instruments, output, intermediate consumption, inventories, depreciation, depletion and other flows remains to be studied. The example assumes the delivery date is the economic-ownership transfer date; real cases require examining the relevant facts and rules. Chapter 4 is not complete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 7: Time of recording for goods and services

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.196–4.197.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4.196: Acquisition of goods is recorded when economic ownership changes. When this is unclear, the transaction partners' accounting entries may indicate the timing; failing that, physical possession and control may be used. For imports and exports, ownership change is the principle. Customs records of physical movement across the frontier may often serve as an approximation when direct ownership-transfer dates are unavailable. Similar rules apply to non-produced non-financial assets.
- §4.197: Services are recorded when provided. Services supplied continuously, including operating leasing, insurance and housing services (including owner-occupied housing services), are recorded continuously over the period the contract lasts or the dwelling is available.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A buyer orders goods on 1 December, the goods are dispatched on 5 December, economic ownership changes on delivery on 8 December, and payment is made on 15 December. Assuming ownership changes on delivery, the acquisition is recorded on 8 December, not automatically on the order, dispatch or payment date.
2. For imports and exports, the statistical compiler may use customs records of goods crossing the national/customs frontier as an approximation where the precise ownership-change date is not available. The frontier-crossing date is not the underlying principle.
3. A service such as insurance is recorded over the period in which coverage is provided, not wholly on the date the premium is paid.

**Key distinction:** Goods are generally recorded when economic ownership changes; services are recorded when provided, continuously where provision is continuous.

**Study limitations:** Introductory lesson only. The detailed treatment of consignment, goods sent for processing, repairs, complex delivery arrangements and other special cases has not been systematically examined. Chapter 4 is not complete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 8: Time of recording of distributive transactions

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.198–4.204.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4.198: Distributive transactions are recorded when the related claims arise. Remuneration of employees, interest, social contributions and benefits are recorded in the period in which the amounts payable accrue. For some distributive transactions, accrual timing depends on the unit's decision about when to distribute earned income or make a transfer. Employee-stock-option remuneration has additional guidance in Chapter 25.
- §4.199: Interest accrues continuously because financial resources are made available continuously. For instruments where the debtor pays only at maturity, the accrued interest before maturity has counterpart financial entries: additional acquisition of a financial asset by the creditor and equal incurrence of a liability by the debtor.
- §§4.200–4.201: Dividends are recorded when shares go ex-dividend, not merely on declaration or cash settlement. Between ex-dividend and settlement, the amount payable is recorded as other accounts receivable/payable. Withdrawals from quasi-corporations are recorded when they actually occur; reinvested earnings are recorded as retained earnings accrue.
- §4.202: Taxes and other compulsory transfers are recorded when the activities, transactions or events occur that create the government's claim. Income taxes and social contributions based on income are in principle attributed to the period in which income is earned. Practical flexibility may allow source-deducted taxes and regular prepayments to be recorded when paid, and final liabilities when determined.
- §4.203: Fines, penalties and property forfeitures are recorded when the issuing unit has an unconditional claim. If an appeal remains possible, the unconditional claim arises only after the appeal is resolved.
- §4.204: Conditional grants are recorded when all requirements and conditions giving rise to the recipient's legal claim are satisfied. Where no legal claim arises, the transfer is attributed to settlement. More generally, timing of voluntary transfers follows the change in economic ownership of the resources that form their counterpart entries.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. Salary earned by an employee in November but paid in December is attributed to November, when the amount payable accrues.
2. Interest on a loan that accumulates monthly but is paid once at maturity is recorded as accruing over the loan period, with corresponding creditor asset and debtor liability entries.
3. A declared dividend is recorded on the ex-dividend date; the payable/receivable is then shown until settlement.
4. An appealed fine is not recorded as an unconditional claim while the appeal remains unresolved; the claim becomes unconditional when the appeal is resolved.
5. A grant requiring eligible expenditure is recorded when the specified conditions for a legal claim have been met, not automatically when the programme is announced.

**Key lesson:** Distributive transactions are generally recorded when the associated entitlement or claim arises, rather than simply when cash is paid. The precise timing rule depends on the type of transaction and any applicable conditions or practical conventions.

**Study limitations:** Introductory lesson only. Detailed treatment of remuneration, social contributions and benefits, property income, taxes, grants and employee stock options must be studied in the relevant chapters, especially Chapters 8, 9 and 25. Chapter 4 remains incomplete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 9: Time of recording of transactions in financial assets and liabilities

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-21, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.205–4.210.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4.205: Transactions in financial assets are recorded when economic ownership changes. Implicit trade credit and advances associated with a non-financial transaction are recorded when that underlying transaction occurs.
- §4.206: Where the timing of a financial transaction cannot be established precisely, the standard provides guidance for consistent recording by the parties. For payment floats, the creditor's receipt of payment is relevant to determining the time of the change in economic ownership.
- §4.207: Securities transactions are recorded when ownership changes, which may precede settlement/delivery. Significant timing differences give rise to other accounts receivable/payable; where settlement delay is short, settlement may be used as an acceptable proxy.
- §4.208: Debt repayments are recorded when the debt is extinguished, including by payment, rescheduling or forgiveness. When arrears arise, no new transaction is imputed merely because payment is overdue; arrears remain in the same instrument until extinguished. Activation of one-off guarantees is cross-referenced to Chapter 25.
- §4.209: Introduces the time of recording for flows associated with one-off guarantees; the detailed accounting treatment is referred to Chapter 25. This lesson did not attempt a full treatment of guarantee accounting.
- §4.210: Employee stock options are recognized at grant date. Associated remuneration accrues over the period to which the option relates, generally from grant to vesting, with earlier service periods considered where the option covers them. Detailed flows are referred to Chapter 25.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A supplier delivers goods worth ₦2m with payment due in 30 days. The supplier records a receivable and the buyer a payable as the implicit trade credit associated with the goods transaction.
2. An investor buys securities on Monday, with settlement on Wednesday. If economic ownership changes on Monday, the transaction is recorded on Monday; a significant gap is represented through other accounts receivable/payable. For a short delay, settlement may be an acceptable proxy.
3. A loan instalment becomes overdue. The debt remains recorded under the same instrument; the mere occurrence of arrears is not a new borrowing transaction.
4. An employee receives an option at grant and earns the related remuneration over the relevant service period, generally until vesting. This is an introductory description; the detailed accounting is in Chapter 25.

**Key lesson:** The standard distinguishes when a financial claim or liability is created, when it is settled, when a debt is extinguished, and when arrears merely arise. These are not interchangeable events.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Payment-float mechanics, complex securities settlement, debt rescheduling/forgiveness, guarantee activation and employee stock-option accounting require deeper study, including Chapter 25. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 9: Time of recording of transactions in financial assets and liabilities

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.205–4.210.

- §4.205: Transactions in financial assets are recorded when economic ownership changes. Implicit trade credit and advances associated with a non-financial transaction are recorded when that underlying transaction occurs.

Changes to instrument characteristics under the existing contract may be reclassifications; renegotiation or a change in instrument category can create new transactions.
- §4.209: Activation of one-off, non-standardized guarantees creates a new liability and financial transactions. The timing is determined by the events activating the guarantee; detailed treatment is in Chapter 25.
- §4.210: Employee stock options are recognized at grant date. Associated remuneration accrues over the period to which the option relates, generally grant to vesting, with any relevant pre-grant service period also considered. Detailed treatment is in Chapter 25.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 10: Time of recording of output, intermediate consumption, changes in inventories, depreciation and depletion

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.211–4.214.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4.211: On an accrual basis, output is recorded over the period in which production takes place. Additions to work-in-progress are recorded continuously as work proceeds. When production ends, accumulated work-in-progress is transformed into finished product ready for delivery or sale.
- §4.212: Intermediate consumption of a good or service is recorded when it enters the production process, not when it was acquired by the producer.
- §4.213: Inventories include materials and supplies held as inputs, output not yet sold, and products held by wholesale and retail traders. Additions are recorded when products are purchased, produced or otherwise acquired; deductions are recorded when products are sold, used as intermediate consumption or otherwise relinquished.
- §4.214: Depreciation and depletion are costs that accrue over the whole period the fixed asset or natural resource is used for productive purposes. Allocation across accounting periods depends on the applicable rate of depreciation or depletion; timing is therefore linked to valuation.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A building project lasting a year generates output progressively as work is performed; work-in-progress accumulates before completion.
2. A bakery purchases flour on Monday but uses it in production on Wednesday. Acquisition adds to inventory; intermediate consumption is recorded when the flour enters production.
3. A retailer buys 100 bags of rice, sells 30 and retains 70. The purchase is an inventory addition, the sale is a deduction, and the remaining stock is 70 bags (ignoring other changes).
4. A productive machine's depreciation accrues over the periods in which it is used; mineral-resource depletion accrues over the productive extraction period.

**Key lesson:** Acquisition, use in production, output creation, inventory holding and consumption of productive capacity are distinct events with different recording points.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed valuation and measurement of work-in-progress, inventories, depreciation and depletion remain open for later study in the relevant chapters. Chapter 4 remains incomplete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 11: Composite transactions, other flows, external timing adjustments and balance-sheet dates

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.215–4.224.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4.215: Transactions measured as the balance of two or more other transactions follow the timing of the constituent basic flows. The example given is implicit financial services on loans and deposits, recorded as interest accrues.
- §4.216: The same general rule applies to balancing items, but interpretation requires attention to the different timing characteristics of the underlying flows. Corporate saving, for example, combines operating surplus and other components such as dividends payable, whose timing may differ.
- §4.217: Other changes in the volume of assets and liabilities are generally discrete events and are recorded when the events occur.
- §4.218: Revaluations can occur continuously as prices and exchange rates change. Nominal holding gains/losses are computed over the relevant holding interval: from the start of the accounting period, acquisition from another unit or production of the asset, until the end of the period, relinquishment of ownership or consumption in production.
- §4.219: Contractual commitments are treated as contingent until a party performs its obligation. A unit incurs holding gains/losses only on assets or liabilities over which it has economic ownership. Merely signing a contract does not, by itself, mean the buyer has acquired the asset or bears its price/exchange-rate risk under the SNA.
- §§4.220–4.221: Changes in structure and classification are recorded when the unit or asset moves category under SNA rules. All entries for a reclassification are recorded at the same time; reclassifications should not be stockpiled and entered years later. Records should allow historical series to be reconstructed.
- §§4.222–4.223: Partner economies may record external transactions at different times because of time zones, mail, clearing and settlement delays, or imperfect data sources. Material divergences from the required basis should be adjusted. Sources recording the actual event, such as loan drawings rather than authorizations/program dates, are preferable.
- §4.224: The closing balance sheet of one accounting period is identical to the opening balance sheet of the next. Price changes, reclassifications and other economic flows must be recognized through the sequence of accounts so no unexplained changes remain.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A bank's implicit service on a loan accrues over time with interest; it is not postponed until the borrower pays the full amount.
2. A company's saving is a balancing item derived from flows with potentially different timing, such as operating surplus and dividends payable.
3. A factory destroyed in a discrete event is recorded when the destruction occurs, subject to the detailed classification rules.
4. A buyer signs a land-purchase contract in January but economic ownership transfers in March. The buyer does not record land holding gains for the pre-ownership period merely because the contract was signed.
5. A loan facility is authorized in one period but actually drawn in another. The drawing date is the relevant event for recording the funds advanced.
6. A sector's closing asset/liability positions at year-end must carry forward as the next period's opening positions.

**Key lesson:** The integrated accounts must align the timing of underlying flows, discrete other-flow events, ownership-based revaluations, reclassifications and balance-sheet positions. The closing position of one period must reconcile with the opening position of the next.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed treatment of composite transactions, reclassification cases, international asymmetries and balance-sheet compilation remains open. Chapter 4 remains incomplete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 12: Unit of account and currency conversion

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.225–4.241.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §§4.225–4.226: A common unit of account is needed to express heterogeneous transactions and positions consistently. The SNA discusses domestic-currency and other-currency presentations.
- §§4.227–4.229: Domestic currency is defined by the currency issued by the monetary authority of the economy; other currencies are foreign. SDRs are treated as foreign currency. Currency includes banknotes, coins and other means of payment issued by financial corporations.
- §§4.230–4.235: Distinguishes currency of denomination (the currency in which the value is fixed) from currency of settlement (the currency actually used for payment). They can differ. The text also covers equity, foreign-currency-indexed debt, multicurrency instruments and currency exposure in financial derivatives.
- §4.236: Foreign-currency flows are converted at the rate prevailing when the flow occurs; positions are converted at the rate prevailing on the balance-sheet date. The midpoint between buying and selling rates is recommended. Foreign-currency credit transactions and subsequent payments can have different domestic-currency values; the exchange-rate difference is recorded as a holding gain or loss over the relevant period.
- §4.237: The actual rate for each transaction is preferred. Daily or shortest-period averages may approximate transaction rates where necessary; period averages may be used for continuous flows such as accrued interest.
- §4.238: Derived measures should not, in principle, be converted directly. Convert the underlying flows first, then calculate the derived measure in the target currency. Different currencies can produce different, potentially opposite-signed derived measures because exchange rates and flow timing vary.
- §§4.239–4.241: Multiple exchange rates can incorporate implicit tax/subsidy elements. Positions use the applicable rate for the relevant assets/liabilities. Parallel-market transactions cannot simply be ignored and are generally converted at the applicable parallel rate; whether parallel rates are included in a unitary rate depends on the degree of integration and official sanction/intervention. Further detailed guidance is referred to supplementary material and BPM7 compilation guidance.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A contract fixes an equipment price at USD 10,000 but settlement is made in naira: denomination is USD, settlement currency is NGN.
2. A USD 10,000 payable is recorded at ₦1,500/USD when the equipment is acquired (₦15m). If settled later at ₦1,600/USD (₦16m), the ₦1m difference is not an additional equipment purchase; it is a holding loss on the foreign-currency liability, subject to the applicable recording rules.
3. A flow and a balance-sheet position require different conversion dates: transaction-date rate for the flow; balance-sheet-date rate for the position.
4. Revenue and expenditure occurring at different times should be converted separately before deriving a surplus in another currency.

**Key lesson:** Currency values carry a denomination, settlement, timing and valuation basis. Flows, positions and derived measures must be converted consistently; exchange-rate changes can create holding gains/losses and multiple-rate systems require explicit treatment.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed multiple-exchange-rate compilation, derivative denomination, multicurrency instruments and practical source selection remain open. Chapter 4 remains incomplete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 13: Aggregation, netting, consolidation and symmetry of reporting

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.242–4.257.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §§4.242–4.245: National accounts aggregate flows and stocks of individual units using classifications, especially institutional sectors or industries crossed with transactions, other accumulation entries or assets. Additional breakdowns can be used. Aggregates are conceptually sums of elementary components, although compilation often requires estimates and adjustments because underlying data are incomplete or inconsistent.
- §§4.246–4.251: Netting offsets entries on opposite sides. Gross recording is generally preferred in the sequence of accounts, while some concepts inherently involve netting. Financial flows are recorded as net changes in assets and net changes in liabilities for each instrument. “Net” also appears in specific SNA measures and should not be treated as a single universal operation.
- §§4.252–4.253: Consolidation eliminates transactions and positions between units within the relevant group. The sequence of economic accounts is generally not consolidated; consolidation can be useful for particular analyses, including general government and financial corporations. Output and intermediate consumption are recorded at establishment level, and deliveries between establishments of the same institutional unit are not eliminated through consolidation.
- §§4.254–4.257: Symmetry of reporting is essential for consistency and comparability. Quadruple-entry accounting supports counterpart consistency. Differences in valuation, timing and classification in microdata require adjustment. Individual business accounting standards may not ensure horizontal consistency between counterparties.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A bank reports interest received and interest paid separately (gross) as well as their net difference.
2. A government institution's payable to another institution within the consolidated government group is eliminated against the counterpart receivable when producing consolidated positions.
3. A supplier's sale and receivable correspond to a buyer's acquisition and payable; both parties generate two entries, giving four entries across the two units.
4. Importer and exporter records can differ because of timing, valuation or classification; harmonized rules and reconciliation are needed.

**Key lesson:** Aggregation organizes microdata into useful statistical totals; netting offsets specified entries; consolidation removes internal group relationships for a defined consolidated view; symmetry aligns the records of counterparties. These operations are distinct.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed consolidation boundaries and exceptions, full account-by-account netting rules, reconciliation procedures and practical compiler adjustments remain open. Chapter 4 remains incomplete. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 14: Valuation methods for transactions

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover annex, §§4A.1–4A.14.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4A.1 introduces the methods for valuing transactions and stocks, in order of preference where applicable; stock valuation is distinct from initial recognition, which is covered under transaction valuation.
- §4A.2: Observed exchange values (observed market prices) are generally the most appropriate measure. Conceptual exceptions may arise for distorted transfer prices between affiliated enterprises and concessional pricing, although adjustments are often not made in practice for feasibility and international consistency.
- §§4A.3–4A.4: In the absence of actual exchange values, prices of similar goods, services or assets may be used. Comparability/homogeneity, similar market conditions and sufficiently established markets are prerequisites; hedonic adjustment may address differences but requires substantial data.
- §4A.5: Certain transactions use indirect valuation, including reinvested earnings, non-life insurance output and implicit financial services.
- §§4A.6–4A.11: Sum-of-costs valuation applies in relevant cases where market prices/equivalents are unavailable, including non-market output of government, NPISHs and the central bank, and own-account production. Components include intermediate consumption, compensation of employees, other taxes less subsidies on production, consumption of fixed capital and depletion, net return to capital, and rent payable on rented non-produced non-financial assets. The standard discusses owner/family labour in unincorporated enterprises, the capital base and exceptions, and approaches to estimating a net return to capital.
- §§4A.12–4A.13: The annex addresses unpaid household services produced for own final use, including replacement-cost and opportunity-cost approaches to labour valuation. Replacement cost is considered more appropriate for approximating exchange prices in the integrated SNA framework.
- §4A.14 summarizes the preferred methods: observed exchange values for monetary market transactions; market-equivalent prices for barter and suitable own-use goods/services; market-equivalent prices for owner-occupied housing services; sum of costs as the default for other unpaid household services where relevant data are difficult to obtain, own-account capital formation (unless comparable market prices are suitable), and non-market government/NPISH output.

**Plain-language examples (illustrative):**

1. A genuine vehicle purchase for ₦15 million provides an observed transaction price.
2. Owner-occupied housing services can be valued using comparable rents, provided the comparators reflect similar property characteristics and market conditions.
3. Reinvested earnings and implicit financial services require derived/indirect calculations rather than a direct service-sale price.
4. A non-market hospital's output can be estimated using the specified sum-of-costs components. A teaching illustration used hypothetical inputs and costs; it was not Nigerian data.
5. For unpaid household services outside the integrated production boundary, replacement-cost labour valuation is distinguished from opportunity cost.

**Key lesson:** The SNA seeks exchange values. It prefers observed exchange prices, uses comparable market prices where appropriate, applies indirect derivation in specified cases, and uses sum-of-costs methods in relevant non-market and own-account contexts.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. The detailed boundaries, exceptions, capital-service calculations and practical estimation of each component require further study. Stock valuation in §§4A.15 onward has not yet been studied. Chapter 4 remains incomplete; no Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 15: Valuation of stocks of assets and liabilities, Part 1

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover annex, §§4A.15–4A.28.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4A.15 distinguishes non-financial from financial assets and stresses consistency between financial assets and corresponding liabilities.
- §4A.16: Observed current market prices are the most direct method for valuing balance-sheet positions. Markets with considerable trading volumes and regular quotations are preferred; recent transactions may be used when trading is occasional.
- §§4A.17–4A.22: Market-equivalent prices and expert estimates are considered where direct prices are unavailable. Comparability and the characteristics of the asset matter. Special-purpose assets may have weak or misleading second-hand market prices; written-down replacement cost may better represent their value in continued productive use.
- §4A.23: If market and market-equivalent prices are unavailable, valuation based on past expenses is considered. The two basic methods are historical acquisition price and written-down replacement cost, depending on whether depreciation applies.
- §§4A.24–4A.25: Written-down replacement cost starts from the current replacement price and deducts accumulated depreciation, normally using the perpetual inventory method (PIM) for fixed assets. PIM requires information/assumptions about acquisitions, disposals, price changes, service lives, age-price or age-efficiency profiles and discard patterns.
- §§4A.26–4A.28: Nominal value is used for many non-negotiable financial instruments, such as loans, deposits and other accounts receivable/payable. It represents the amount the debtor owes to extinguish the claim, including accrued interest. The text distinguishes nominal value from market value and discusses supplementary information on non-performing loans and the treatment of loans that become negotiable.

**Teaching examples (illustrative):**

1. Listed shares valued by multiplying units held by the observed balance-sheet-date market price.
2. A specialised machine valued by reference to comparable equipment, expert evidence or written-down replacement cost when its second-hand market is unsuitable.
3. A machine with a current equivalent replacement price of ₦30 million and accumulated depreciation of ₦12 million has an illustrative written-down replacement cost of ₦18 million.
4. A loan with ₦10 million original principal, ₦2 million repaid and ₦500,000 accrued interest has an illustrative nominal outstanding amount of ₦8.5 million.
5. A bond's face value can remain fixed while its market value changes as market interest rates change.

**Terminology caution:** Nominal value, face value, market/fair value, amortized value, book value and historical cost are distinct concepts. The lesson introduced distinctions; the complete instrument-specific rules are not exhausted here.

**Key lesson:** Balance-sheet values are current values at the reporting date, not automatically the original acquisition prices. The appropriate method depends on the asset or instrument and the availability and suitability of market evidence. Financial asset and liability counterpart values must remain consistent.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. The detailed PIM, instrument-specific valuation rules and non-performing-loan treatment require further study. The valuation annex §§4A.1–4A.40 has now received an introductory first pass; detailed technical treatment and chapter-wide review remain open. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 16: Indirect valuation and present value of future economic benefits

A focused introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, using the official 2025 SNA White Cover annex, §§4A.29–4A.40.

**What the standard establishes:**

- §4A.29: Indirect valuation may be used for financial assets and related liabilities, often for unlisted equity; a corporation's intrinsic value is a valid starting point. Chapter 14 gives further guidance.
- §§4A.30–4A.31: Present value of future economic benefits is an alternative when earlier valuation methods cannot be applied. Typical applications include defined-benefit pension entitlements, unlisted equity where other methods are less appropriate, and natural resources. Actuarial and unlisted-equity details are referred to other guidance/chapters.
- §4A.32: For natural resources, future economic benefits are resource rents. The residual value method derives resource rent from output at basic prices related to extracted resources, less intermediate consumption, remuneration of employees, and other taxes less subsidies on production; plus specific taxes less subsidies on extraction; less depreciation related to capital used in production and the net return to capital used in production. Resource rent is described as depletion plus return to the natural resource.
- §4A.33: For non-financial assets, present-value valuation requires a direct link between future benefits and the asset, with no other assets assumed to have generated the residual income. It requires assumptions about asset life, extraction path, regeneration for renewable resources, future revenues and expenditures, and discount rate; it is often a last-resort method for certain assets such as natural resources.
- §4A.34: Where a legal owner (often government) grants extraction rights and does not appropriate the full resource rent, the SNA recommends the split-asset approach. The resource asset is recorded in the accounts of the legal owner and extractor in proportion to the resource rent appropriated by each. This is not an automatic arbitrary split; it depends on the actual economic arrangement and respective appropriation of resource rent.
- §4A.35: The SNA points to the SEEA Central Framework and natural-resource compilation guidance for further detail.
- §4A.36: For other assets, written-down replacement cost is usually preferred to present value; this also applies to assets produced in-house.
- §4A.37: Present value and written-down replacement cost are both discussed for human capital outside the integrated framework; the SNA makes no firm recommendation pending practical experience.
- §§4A.38–4A.40 summarize the different preferences for financial and non-financial assets: market/market-equivalent prices preferred for financial instruments where available; nominal value generally viable for non-negotiable instruments; specific indirect/present-value methods for unlisted equity and defined-benefit pension entitlements; written-down replacement cost commonly for fixed assets; present value of future earnings often the only alternative for natural resources; expert estimates may be necessary for valuables.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**

1. A privately held corporation has no quoted share price; intrinsic value can provide a starting point for estimating unlisted equity.
2. A simplified residual-value calculation illustrated how output less production costs and the return to other capital yields resource rent.
3. A hypothetical stream of future resource rents was discounted to a present value. The illustration simplified assumptions and did not constitute an official compilation method or valuation.
4. A hypothetical resource value split illustrated the concept only. Actual allocation requires estimating the shares of resource rent appropriated by the legal owner and extractor; the example percentages were not empirical.

**Key lesson:** Present-value valuation converts expected future economic benefits into a current value, but depends heavily on assumptions and requires a defensible direct link between the benefits and the asset. For natural resources, resource rent is central; the split-asset approach aligns recorded asset ownership with the shares of resource rent appropriated by legal owner and extractor.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed actuarial methods, unlisted equity valuation, natural-resource resource-rent compilation, discount-rate selection, forecasting, split-asset implementation and the referenced SEEA guidance remain open for later study. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 17: Structured review, Part 1 — how the accounting system fits together

An introductory synthesis lesson was completed on 2026-11-22, based on Chapter 4 and the material already studied.

**Synthesis:**

- Stocks are measured at a point in time; flows are measured over a period (§§4.1–4.4).
- Changes between opening and closing stocks are explained through transactions, other changes in volume and revaluations (§§4.6–4.7 and related sections).
- Institutional units and sectors identify the units to which assets, liabilities and flows are attributed (§§4.5, 4.13 and 4.18).
- Accumulation accounts explain changes in assets and liabilities between balance sheets; the capital account, financial account, other changes in volume account and revaluation account have distinct roles (§4.2 and related sections).
- Consistency in valuation, timing, classification and counterpart recording is essential. The chapter introduces quadruple-entry recording for transactions between institutional units (§4.18).
- Aggregation, netting and consolidation are distinct presentation operations and must not be treated as synonyms.

**Illustration:** A simplified machinery reconciliation connected an opening value of ₦20m, acquisition of ₦5m, a simplified −₦2m volume change and +₦1m revaluation to a closing value of ₦24m. This is arithmetic for teaching, not a full SNA compilation example.

**Key lesson:** The chapter is an integrated system. It identifies economic units and their positions, records flows and other changes during the period, and reconciles them to closing balance sheets using consistent accounting rules.

**Limitations:** This was a synthesis, not a paragraph-by-paragraph audit. Timing/accrual, economic ownership, monetary and non-monetary transactions, exchange versus transfer, rerouting, partitioning, reassignment, internal transactions, detailed debit/credit entries, and the exact aggregation/netting/consolidation rules still need systematic examination. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 18: Monetary transactions, exchanges/transfers and rerouting

An introductory lesson was delivered on 2026-11-22, based on the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.23–4.36.

**Verified White Cover paragraph coverage:**

- §4.23 defines monetary transactions: one institutional unit makes/receives a payment or incurs/acquires a liability/financial asset stated in currency; the parties express their agreement in monetary terms. Recording in monetary terms alone does not make a flow a monetary transaction.
- §4.24 lists common monetary transactions, including purchases, securities acquisitions, wages and salaries, interest, dividends, rent, taxes and cash social assistance benefits.
- §§4.25–4.26 distinguish exchanges (quid pro quo/recompense of commensurate value) from transfers (no recompense in return).
- §4.27 explains why contingent benefits and collective services do not constitute the relevant recompense: social insurance contributions, non-life insurance premiums and taxes remain transfers.
- §4.28 distinguishes current transfers (redistribute income) from capital transfers (redistribute wealth; linked to acquisition/disposal/transfer of non-cash, non-inventory assets, assumed/forgiven liabilities, or addressing accumulated losses over multiple years). Capital transfers leave saving unaffected.
- §4.29 introduces statistical rearrangements: actual observed transactions may be rearranged in the sequence of accounts to reveal underlying economic relationships. Three types affect channels, number of transactions or units involved.
- §4.30 defines rerouting and distinguishes recording a direct A-to-C transaction through B from recording a transaction of one kind A-to-B with a matching transaction of a different kind B-to-A.
- §§4.31–4.36 provide examples: employer social security contributions; lottery/gambling transfer elements; retained earnings of foreign direct investment enterprises; property income on life insurance and annuity entitlements; government fiscal activity through a non-resident unit; implicit taxes/subsidies under multiple exchange-rate regimes.

**Important source-version correction:** The previous live lesson used an incorrect paragraph range (§§4.26–4.40) and several incorrect individual references. The official White Cover PDF was reopened and the actual numbering verified. The correct coverage for this lesson is §§4.23–4.36. Subsequent lesson references below use the White Cover numbering.

**Teaching illustrations:** Credit purchase, exchange-versus-transfer, current-versus-capital transfer, employer social contribution rerouting, and retained earnings of a foreign direct investment enterprise. These were explanatory examples, not measured observations.

**Key lesson:** A monetary transaction is defined by the monetary terms of the parties' agreement, not merely by the fact that accounts eventually express values in currency. Exchanges and transfers differ by whether a recompense is received. Statistical rerouting changes the recording path to represent the underlying economic relationship, without claiming that the administrative payment route physically changed.

**Limitations:** Introductory only. Partitioning (§§4.37–4.42), reassignment (§§4.43–4.48), and non-monetary transactions (beginning §4.49) remain to be studied. Detailed treatment of the examples is deferred to the relevant chapters. Chapter 4 is not complete.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 19: Partitioning and reassignment of transactions

An introductory lesson was delivered on 2026-11-22, based on the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.37–4.48.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**

- §4.37: Partitioning records a single transaction from the parties' perspective as two or more differently classified transactions. Financial lease rentals are partitioned into repayment of principal and interest; financial leasing is viewed as financing the purchase of a fixed asset, with a loan from lessor to lessee.
- §4.38: A household car used partly for production (e.g. taxi services) and partly for personal use is partitioned between gross fixed capital formation and final consumption expenditure.
- §4.39: Interest payable by financial intermediaries on deposits and payable to intermediaries on loans is partitioned into SNA interest and implicit financial intermediation services. This affects intermediate/final consumption and GDP, but not the saving of the units concerned.
- §4.40: Settlement of a financial derivative by delivery of the underlying asset is recorded as a derivative transaction and a separate transaction in the underlying asset.
- §4.41: Wholesalers' and retailers' output is measured by trade margins; their contribution is the service of storing, displaying and making goods conveniently available, rather than the full resale value of goods.
- §4.42: Package-tour prices are unbundled into the various service components.
- §4.43: Reassignment records a financial transaction arranged by a third party as taking place directly between the principal parties; the facilitator records its fee/service output, not the principal transaction.
- §§4.44–4.48: Tax revenue is generally attributed to the government unit with authority to impose the tax and final discretion to set/vary its rate. The paragraphs address tax-sharing, delegated authority, joint arrangements, jurisdiction-specific rates, and analogous treatment of subsidies/social benefits.

**Teaching illustrations:** Financial lease payment split; mixed-use household car; implicit financial service component; trader's margin; commercial agent; tax collection by one government for another. All numerical examples were hypothetical and explanatory, not observed data.

**Key lesson:** Partitioning separates components of one observed transaction that have different economic classifications. Reassignment attributes a transaction to its principal parties rather than the third-party facilitator. These are distinct from rerouting, which changes the statistical channel or records linked transactions to reveal the underlying economic relationship.

**Limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed financial lease rules, implicit financial service calculations, trade margin compilation, package-tour allocation and government tax attribution require further study in their relevant chapters. Non-monetary transactions beginning at §4.49 remain open. Chapter 4 is not complete.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 20: Non-monetary transactions, two-party forms

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 21: Internal or intra-unit transactions

An introductory lesson was delivered on 2026-11-22 using the official UN 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.59–4.64.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.59: Internal/intra-unit transactions are actions within a single institutional unit treated as transactions to provide a more analytically useful picture of production and final uses of output.
- §4.60: Some households, all NPISHs and general government units, and the central bank act as both producers and final consumers. Where the unit consumes its own output, the SNA constructs and values an internal transaction to record both output and consumption.
- §4.61: Goods produced by persons and used by those persons or members of their households for final consumption are included in output in a manner analogous to market sales; the SNA assumes delivery to self/household. The same principle applies to household services within the production boundary, with owner-occupied housing services as the principal example.
- §4.62: Government and NPISH establishments' non-market services to households or society are recorded at their costs as internal final expenditure by the owning government/NPISH unit. The central bank is treated similarly. Household acquisition is recorded separately as social transfers in kind.
- §4.63: Own-account fixed-asset production is recorded as delivery by the enterprise to itself as subsequent user. The decline in value of fixed assets used in production is charged as a cost of production.
- §4.64: Deliveries between establishments of the same enterprise are cross-referenced to §7.114.

**Teaching illustrations:** Household own-use potato production; owner-occupied housing; government/NPISH services; enterprise construction of a warehouse for own use; fixed-asset depreciation; movements between establishments. Numerical values were hypothetical.

**Clarification:** The household own-use example is subject to the SNA production boundary and relevant exclusions; the lesson did not study those detailed boundaries. Government/NPISH internal final expenditure and household social transfers in kind are separate accounting entries and must not be conflated.

**Key lesson:** An institutional unit can produce and use output itself. The SNA sometimes constructs internal transactions to record production and final use consistently even though no exchange occurs between separate units.

**Limitations:** Introductory only. Detailed production-boundary rules, own-account production valuation, owner-occupied housing valuation, non-market output valuation, depreciation methodology and establishment-level recording remain open. Chapter 4 is not complete.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 22: Externalities and illegal actions

An introductory lesson was delivered on 2026-11-22 using the official UN 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.65–4.72.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §§4.65–4.66: Introduce externalities and their positive/negative effects on other institutional units without the affected units' agreement.
- §4.67: Explains why assigning monetary values to externalities in the central integrated accounts is problematic: no mutually agreed market values, inconsistent valuations among parties, and accounting adjustments could imply institutional/economic arrangements that do not exist.
- §4.68: Pollution example: where a producer pollutes without charge or penalty, private production costs may be below social costs. The SNA does not simply impute externality costs into the producer's accounts. Policy charges/taxes may internalize some externalities and alter behaviour.

- §4.70: Illegal market transactions meeting the characteristics of transactions, notably mutual agreement, are included in principle, even if the activity is illegal. Excluding them can distort household saving, asset acquisition and external trade balances.
- §4.71: Theft and violence are not transactions because there is no mutual agreement; they are conceptually extreme externalities.
- §4.72: Significant redistribution or destruction of assets through theft, violence or war is recorded as other flows, not transactions.

**Teaching illustrations:** Factory pollution; private versus social costs; illegal consensual market exchange; theft and asset loss. Numerical amounts were hypothetical and explanatory only.

**Key lesson:** Economic illegality alone does not exclude a market transaction from national accounts. Mutual agreement is a key distinction. Externalities are not automatically transactions, while significant asset effects of theft, violence and war are recorded through other flows.

**Limitations:** Introductory only. Externality valuation and SEEA methods remain outside this lesson. Detailed other-flow classifications follow in §§4.73–4.81. Chapter 4 is not complete.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 24: Stocks, economic benefits and ownership

An introductory lesson was delivered on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover text, §§4.84–4.93.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.84: Stocks/positions are measured at a point in time.
- §§4.85–4.86: Economic benefits include benefits from using or holding items, including goods/services and monetary means to acquire them. Benefits may be enjoyed now or later; assets and liabilities can transfer benefits across accounting periods, and borrowing can bring present benefits in exchange for commitments involving future benefits.
- §§4.87–4.88: Legal ownership identifies the unit legally entitled to claim benefits associated with an item. Government may hold ownership on behalf of a community; items without an individual or collective owner able to claim the associated benefits are not recognized as assets in the integrated SNA framework.
- §§4.89–4.91: Production and the transfer of benefits across time involve risks. The standard distinguishes uncertainty associated with production from uncertainty associated with financial developments and explains that economic agents compare present and future benefits alongside the risks involved.
- §§4.92–4.93: Economic ownership belongs to the institutional unit entitled to the economic benefits of using an item in economic activity and accepting the associated risks. Legal and economic ownership often coincide; where they differ, the SNA generally uses economic ownership when referring to ownership.

- A farmer's uncertain future harvest illustrated production risk; a loan illustrated the transfer of benefits and obligations across accounting periods.

**Key lesson:** The SNA's ownership concept is economic, not merely legal. To understand who owns an asset in the accounts, identify who receives its economic benefits and accepts the associated risks. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 27: Types of assets and liabilities — initial definitions

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.100–4.102.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.100: Economic benefits may be exchanged between institutional units by means of payments. This provides the basis for defining financial claims and liabilities. The integrated SNA framework does not recognize non-financial liabilities; the term liability therefore refers to a financial liability.
- §4.101: A liability is an obligation under which a debtor must provide funds or other economic resources to a creditor under specified circumstances. - A long-standing government social-security pension expectation illustrated a constructive liability and the distinction between a valid expectation and recognition in the sequence of economic accounts.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 28: Financial claims and the asset boundary

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.103–4.107.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.103: Whenever a liability exists, the creditor has a corresponding financial claim against the debtor. Financial claims are financial instruments giving rise to an economic asset with a counterpart liability. They include shares and other equity in corporations. A creditor may also have a claim that permits them to demand payment at their discretion; once demanded, the debtor's payment obligation is unconditional.
- §4.104: Financial assets consist of all financial claims and gold bullion held by monetary authorities as a reserve asset. Such reserve gold is classified as a financial asset even though the holder has no claim over another designated unit.
- §4.105: All items meeting the SNA asset definition are within the integrated framework's asset boundary. Assets that are not financial assets are non-financial assets; these can be distinguished as produced and non-produced. The balance-sheet classification separately groups natural resources to emphasize their special character.
- §4.106: All assets can be represented by a monetary value reflecting the market's view of the economic benefits embodied in the asset. Where a direct market view is unavailable, the value must be approximated by other methods, discussed in the chapter annex.
- §4.107: Non-financial assets enter an economy's asset boundary when their economic owners are resident in that economy. For most natural resources and immobile fixed capital owned by non-residents, a notional resident unit is established. Portable non-financial assets physically in the economy but owned by non-residents are excluded from the domestic balance sheet; portable assets owned by residents remain included even when physically abroad. The paragraph gives aircraft owned by a domestic airline as an example and points to Chapter 23 for difficult MNE intellectual-property ownership cases.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A bank loan illustrated the creditor's financial claim and debtor's corresponding liability.
- Central-bank reserve gold illustrated the special financial-asset treatment, contrasted with gold held by a jewellery business.
- A tractor, warehouse and farmland illustrated produced and non-produced non-financial assets.
- A foreign-owned immovable mine in Nigeria illustrated the notional resident-unit treatment; an aircraft owned by a Nigerian airline illustrated why physical location alone does not determine the domestic asset boundary.

**Key lesson:** The SNA asset boundary is not simply a map of where physical objects happen to be. It depends on the asset definition, economic ownership and residence rules. Financial claims and reserve gold form financial assets; other recognized assets are non-financial assets.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 29: Contingent liabilities and provisions

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.108–4.109.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.108: A liability established by a contract is unconditional once both parties agree to the contract. A liability established by long and well-established custom rather than legal contract is a constructive liability. A contingent liability arises where a legal contract requires payment only if specified conditions prevail. In general, the sequence of economic accounts includes legal liabilities but not constructive or contingent liabilities.
- §4.108 exception: Standardized guarantees are treated differently. Although each individual guarantee is contingent, a sufficiently large group of similar guarantees establishes an actual liability for the proportion likely to be called. The paragraph also refers to the implicit recognition of obligations as negative asset positions when valuing equity of limited-liability shareholders in the presence of implicit guarantees or significant reputational risk; see Chapter 14, §§14.86–14.89.
- §4.109: Corporations may set aside funds for unexpected events, customer defaults or terminal costs related to asset disposal. These amounts are called provisions. They are not treated as liabilities in the SNA sequence of economic accounts because they are not the subject of the kind of legal contract associated with a liability. Provisions remain part of the corporation's net worth; they designate the intended purpose of funds rather than constituting financial assets or liabilities in themselves.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A bank guarantee on a business loan illustrated a contingent obligation that becomes payable only if specified conditions occur.
- A large pool of similar guarantees illustrated why the expected proportion likely to be called is recognized under standardized guarantee arrangements.
- A bank's provision for possible loan losses illustrated why earmarking funds does not itself create a liability or an asset of the borrowers.

**Key lesson:** A possible future payment, a customary expectation and an unconditional contractual obligation are not interchangeable. The SNA distinguishes legal liabilities, constructive liabilities, contingent liabilities, standardized guarantees and provisions, with specific recognition rules.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. Detailed accounting for standardized guarantees, implicit guarantees, equity valuation and provisions remains for the relevant chapters, particularly Chapter 14. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 30: Entry and exit of assets from the balance sheet

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.110–4.113.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.110: Assets owned by resident or notional resident units appear on the domestic economy's balance sheet. The first-level classification distinguishes produced non-financial assets (excluding produced natural resources), non-produced non-financial assets (excluding non-produced natural resources), natural resources, and financial assets and liabilities. Natural resources are grouped separately to support improved accounting for the environment.
- §4.111: Produced non-financial assets generally enter through production or imports. Produced natural resources, such as cultivated biological resources yielding repeat products, follow the same general principle. Historical monuments and artefacts treated as valuables are special cases that may enter recognition without current-period production. Produced assets leave through exhaustion or sale to resident units that will no longer use them in production as a source of future economic benefits, or through sale to non-residents.
- §4.112: Non-produced non-financial assets other than natural resources include (i) contracts, leases and licences that restrict general use or supply and thereby provide enhanced economic benefits; (ii) crypto assets without a corresponding liability designed to act as a medium of exchange; and (iii) purchased goodwill and marketing assets. Such contracts, leases and licences enter when the agreement and enhanced benefits arise and leave when restrictions or benefits cease. Miners and validators are treated as producers of validation services, not producers of these crypto assets. Goodwill and marketing assets are recognized in the integrated framework only when evidenced by a sale.
- §4.113: Financial assets and liabilities arise when one unit commits to make payments to another and cease when that commitment no longer exists, for example when the agreement expires or is otherwise extinguished.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A locally manufactured or imported tractor illustrated the entry of produced assets.
- A historic monument or newly recognized artefact illustrated exceptional recognition without current production.
- A restrictive distribution licence illustrated a qualifying contract-based non-produced asset.
- Bitcoin-like crypto assets without a corresponding liability illustrated the SNA's non-produced asset treatment; validation services are distinct from production of the asset.
- Purchased goodwill illustrated the sale-evidence condition.
- A loan illustrated the creation and extinguishment of a financial asset and its counterpart liability.

**Key lesson:** Different asset classes enter and leave the balance sheet through different economic events. The classification and recognition event matter; physical existence or a business's own description of something as valuable is not sufficient on its own.

Detailed classification and accounting of natural resources, crypto assets, licences, goodwill, and the relevant financial instruments remain for later chapters. ### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 31: Exclusions from the asset boundary

- Education, skills and experience illustrated human capital, which is important but not a core integrated-framework asset.
- Forest ecosystem services illustrated why SEEA Ecosystem Accounting complements, rather than simply forms part of, the integrated SNA asset boundary.

**Key lesson:** The boundary of the integrated SNA is a defined accounting boundary, not a complete inventory of everything valuable to society or the environment. Supplementary and extended accounts and complementary systems can measure important matters outside that core boundary.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 32: Balancing items

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-22 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.118–4.121.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.118: A balancing item is an accounting construct obtained residually as the difference between the total value of entries on one side of an account and the total value on the other side. It cannot be measured independently, does not relate to a specific set of transactions or assets, and has no independent price or quantity unit.
- §4.119: Balancing items are not merely devices for making accounts balance; they are key macroeconomic indicators. Examples in the sequence of economic accounts include value added or domestic product, operating surplus, disposable income, saving, and net lending/net borrowing.
- §4.120: Net worth (B90) is the balance-sheet balancing item. It is the value of an institutional unit's assets less its outstanding liabilities, including shares and other equity. It cannot be measured independently of the other balance-sheet entries.
- §4.121: Changes in net worth can be derived from the transactions and other flows that change the levels of assets and liabilities during the period. Changes in net worth can therefore be explained by tracing those underlying transactions and other flows.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A simplified business example illustrated a balancing item as the residual between revenue and expenses. This is an analogy, not a substitute for the SNA's full account structure.
- A hypothetical balance sheet with ₦50 million in assets and ₦20 million in liabilities illustrated net worth of ₦30 million.
- Changes in income, asset acquisition, borrowing, price revaluation and asset wear illustrated why changes in net worth need to be traced through transactions and other flows.

**Key lesson:** Balancing items are derived from the structure of an account, but many of them are among the most important economic indicators. Net worth connects the balance-sheet stock position to the transactions and other flows that change it over time.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. The detailed derivation of individual balancing items belongs to the relevant account chapters. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 33: Accounting rules and quadruple-entry bookkeeping

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.122–4.127.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.122: The accounting-rules section covers quadruple-entry accounting, valuation, time of recording, classification of accounting entries and grouping of transactions. Detailed application is developed in the chapters dealing with the relevant accounts and flows.
- §4.123: Three bookkeeping principles are distinguished: vertical double-entry bookkeeping, horizontal double-entry bookkeeping and quadruple-entry bookkeeping.
- §4.124: Vertical double-entry bookkeeping means each transaction gives rise to at least two entries (traditionally a debit and a credit) in the books of the transactor. Total credits and debits for transactions balance within the unit.
- §4.125: Other flows have counterpart entries directly in changes in net worth. Vertical double-entry bookkeeping supports the balance-sheet identity: assets equal liabilities plus net worth.
- §4.126: Horizontal double-entry bookkeeping ensures that counterpart institutional units record the same transaction amount consistently. For example, dividends payable across the economy should equal dividends receivable, taking transactions with the rest of the world into account.
- §4.127: The simultaneous application of vertical and horizontal double-entry bookkeeping is quadruple-entry bookkeeping. A transaction between two counterparties gives rise to four entries. Counterpart financial assets and liabilities must be identically valued, recorded at consistent times and classified consistently.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A business buying computer equipment for cash illustrated vertical double-entry: an increase in equipment and a decrease in bank deposits.
- A price change in equipment illustrated an other flow with a counterpart change in net worth, rather than a transaction.
- A sale between two businesses illustrated horizontal consistency: both counterparties record the same transaction amount.
- The combined example illustrated why one transaction between two units generates four entries across their books.

**Key lesson:** National accounts require both internal balance within each institutional unit and consistency between counterparties. Quadruple-entry bookkeeping combines these requirements so the accounts of many units can be integrated into coherent sectoral and national totals.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. Detailed application of valuation, timing, classifications, grouping and reconciliation of inconsistent source records remains for subsequent sections and chapters. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 34: Types of accounting entries

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.128–4.129.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.128: The sequence of economic accounts uses conventions for recording flows with the rest of the world. For example, imports are a revenue of the rest of the world used in the domestic economy; payments for imports represent a drawdown of wealth for the domestic economy and revenue for the rest of the world. Treating the rest of the world as a pseudo-sector permits application of quadruple-entry accounting. External accounts show the consolidated accounts of domestic sectors relative to the rest of the world and are an exact mirror image of the corresponding rest-of-world accounts, with equal coverage, measurement and classification despite the reversal of account sides.
- §4.129: In current transaction accounts, credit entries are termed revenues and debit entries expenditures. For transactions in assets and liabilities, including capital transfers, the terminology is changes in assets, changes in liabilities and changes in net worth. The same terminology applies to other flows affecting asset and liability levels. Balance sheets refer to assets on one side and liabilities and net worth on the other.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A hypothetical Nigerian machinery import from a German supplier illustrated the relationship between domestic imports and the corresponding rest-of-world export/receipt entries.
- A tractor's import, subsequent use, sale and disposal illustrated how distinct events affect different accounts and positions.

**Key lesson:** The rest of the world is represented as a pseudo-sector so cross-border transactions can be integrated consistently into the accounting system. The SNA's terminology distinguishes current-account revenues and expenditures, changes in assets and liabilities, other flows, and balance-sheet positions.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. Detailed external-account compilation and reconciliation are reserved for the relevant chapters and BPM7. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 35: General rules of valuation

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.130–4.133.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.130: The integrated SNA expresses varied economic phenomena in a common accounting unit. It measures the current exchange value of flows and stocks in monetary terms; it does not attempt to measure their utility.
- §4.131: Current exchange value, often labelled market price, is the value at which goods, services, labour or assets are exchanged, or could be exchanged, for cash (currency or transferable deposits). Exchange prices form the basis for valuation of transactions in the integrated SNA.
- §4.132: An exchange price for a specific transaction is the amount paid by a willing buyer to a willing seller, between independent parties and on commercial considerations (at arm's length). It is specific to that exchange and should not be confused with a quoted, world-market, going or fair-market price intended to represent a general price for a class of exchanges. An exchange transaction need not occur in a perfectly competitive market; it can occur in monopolistic, monopsonistic or other market structures.
- §4.133: Where parties agree a price in advance, that contractual price is the exchange price for the transaction, regardless of prices prevailing when the transaction takes place.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- The comparison between a highly useful household food item and a high-priced luxury item illustrated the difference between utility and exchange value.
- A hypothetical sale of 100 bags of potatoes for ₦2 million illustrated the exchange price of a specific transaction.
- The distinction between an actual transaction price and a general market quotation illustrated why the SNA does not treat all price observations as equivalent.
- A forward-agreed potato supply price illustrated why the contractual price remains the exchange price even if the market price changes before delivery.

**Key lesson:** The SNA's valuation objective is current exchange value, not subjective utility. Actual exchange prices are specific to transactions between independent parties on commercial terms; contractual prices agreed in advance remain the exchange prices for those transactions.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. The distinction between observed transaction prices, market quotations, market-equivalent prices and valuation of transactions without observable prices is continued in §§4.134 onward. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 36: Valuation of transactions

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.134–4.138. The lesson was initially presented at a high level; this checkpoint entry records the fuller paragraph-level rules from the official text to avoid losing important qualifications.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.134: Observed market transactions usually represent exchange values. Dumping and discounting transactions still represent exchange prices. Prices of goods and services include appropriate taxes and subsidies. The exchange price is the amount payable by the buyer after rebates, refunds and other adjustments from the seller.
- §4.135: Financial asset and liability transactions are recorded at acquisition or disposal prices excluding commissions, fees and taxes, whether explicit, included in the purchaser's price or deducted from the seller's proceeds. These charges are recorded separately in appropriate categories so counterparties record the same financial-instrument amount. This differs from non-financial assets, for which costs of ownership transfer are included in valuation.
- §4.136: When exchange prices are not observable, market-price equivalents can approximate them. Prefer prices for the same or similar items traded currently in sufficient numbers and under similar circumstances. Where necessary, adjust prices of similar goods or services for quality and other differences.
- §4.137: If no appropriate market exists for a close analogy, prices from less closely related markets may be considered. Ultimately, some goods and services can only be valued by current production cost; output valued this way should include a mark-up reflecting the net return to capital used in production.
- §4.138: Further transaction-valuation methods are explained in the Chapter 4 annex; valuation of particular flow types is elaborated in the relevant chapters.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A seller's rebate or refund reduces the amount payable by the buyer and therefore affects the exchange price.
- A financial instrument purchase with a broker commission illustrates why the instrument value and the service fee are recorded separately.
- Similar goods traded in a sufficiently active and comparable market can provide a market-price equivalent, subject to quality adjustments.
- Non-market output may require a sum-of-costs valuation including a net return to capital.

**Important clarification to the lesson delivery:** The initial explanation covered the broad market-equivalent and production-cost methods but did not adequately explain all details in §§4.134–4.135, including the treatment of taxes, subsidies, rebates, financial commissions and transfer costs. Those rules are now captured here and should be treated as part of the lesson's documented coverage.

**Key lesson:** Use observed exchange prices where available and appropriate. If not, use carefully selected market equivalents or a justified cost-based method. Financial-instrument values must be separated from related fees and taxes, while non-financial asset ownership-transfer costs are treated differently.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. The detailed valuation annex, special valuation cases and valuation of particular flows and positions remain for subsequent study. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 37: Agricultural products sold from the farm and barter transactions

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.139–4.140.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.139: Market-equivalent prices require care for agricultural products sold directly from farms. A farm-gate price may be substantially below an observable price in the nearest market because the latter includes the costs of bringing produce to market. If only a small fraction of a crop reaches the market, the observed price may also be higher than the price that would prevail if the full available crop were traded. Observed prices are suitable only when similar products are traded in sufficient numbers and under similar circumstances; otherwise adjustments are required.
- §4.140: Barter products must be valued both when produced and when acquired for consumption or capital formation. Barter does not automatically exempt products from product taxes; subsidies are conceptually possible, although unlikely to be significant. Barter has no wholesale or retail margins. Transport costs may nevertheless matter: if the supplying unit provides transport, the barter package generally includes transport services and the recipient's purchaser price includes that cost. If the receiving unit must arrange transport, this may reduce the valuation of the goods to the recipient.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A farm-gate sale of potatoes was contrasted with a market price that includes transport and other costs of getting produce to market.
- A small share of a crop reaching the market illustrated why a thin or unrepresentative market price may overstate the price applicable to the full harvest.
- A hypothetical exchange of agricultural produce for another good illustrated that barter output and acquisition must both be valued, and that transport supplied as part of the exchange package affects the recipient's purchaser price.

**Key lesson:** A market quotation is not automatically an appropriate farm-gate valuation. The observed market must be sufficiently representative and comparable, with adjustments where it is not. Barter is still an economic transaction requiring consistent valuation, including attention to product taxes and transport services.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. The detailed valuation of new versus existing goods in barter, differing party valuations, non-simultaneous barter, quotation-period contracts and transfers in kind is reserved for §§4.141–4.145. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 38: Further valuation rules for barter, quotation prices and transfers in kind

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.141–4.145.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.141: Barter may involve new or existing goods acquired by one party. The value to that acquiring party is the cost of acquisition for new goods, or the realizable value for existing goods.
- §4.142: Barter involves at least two units and two products, and the parties may assign different values to the items exchanged. Since the integrated SNA requires one value to be recorded by both parties, a pragmatic simple average of the parties' valuations may be used after allowing for taxes and transportation costs.
- §4.143: Barter need not be simultaneous. If one side of the exchange occurs before the other, an account receivable/payable is recorded even though neither part is settled in money.
- §4.144: For goods contracts with a quotation period established after the goods have changed hands, estimate the exchange value at the time ownership changes. Revise the estimate when the observed exchange value becomes known. The contract price determines the exchange value even if it is not known at the time of change of ownership.
- §4.145: Non-financial resources transferred without a quid pro quo are valued at the prices that would have been received if sold in the market. If no observable market price exists, donor and recipient valuations may differ; the suggested rule of thumb is to use the donor's assigned value as the basis for recording.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A party acquiring a new item through barter uses its acquisition cost; an existing item is valued at its realizable value to the acquiring party.
- If two barter parties assign different values, a single common value is needed for the integrated accounts; the paragraph gives a simple average as a pragmatic approach after relevant tax and transport adjustments.
- If a farmer delivers produce now but receives the counter-good later, a receivable/payable is recorded in the intervening period.
- A commodity delivered before the contract's quotation period is finalized is initially valued using an estimate at the change-of-ownership date and revised when the observed exchange value becomes known, while the contract price governs.
- A donated non-financial resource is valued at the market price it could have fetched; where no market price is observable, the donor's valuation is the suggested basis.

**Key lesson:** Barter, delayed exchanges, commodity contracts and transfers in kind are all recorded within the same exchange-value framework. Non-monetary settlement does not remove the need for consistent valuation, timing and counterpart entries.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. The next paragraphs address acquisition of goods under financial lease and transfer pricing. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 40: Transfer pricing — invoice values, market equivalents and practical limitations

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.148–4.150.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.148: Invoice values may deviate systematically or substantially from prices paid for similar items, indicating that the sums paid cover more than the specified transactions. Transfer pricing between affiliated enterprises may be set artificially high or low to effect an unspecified income payment or capital transfer. Where the value is considerable and would hinder interpretation of the accounts, such transactions should preferably be made explicit. Replacing book values with market-value equivalents is desirable in principle when distortions are large and relevant data (for example customs or tax-authority adjustments or partner-economy information) make it feasible. Choosing suitable equivalents requires cautious, informed judgment.
- §4.149: Goods exchanged within affiliated enterprises may be specialized and not traded between independent parties; services such as management support and technical know-how may also lack close independent-market equivalents. Comparable market values may therefore be difficult to determine. Compilers may have to accept values based on explicit production costs or other values assigned by the enterprise.
- §4.150: Because of the complexity of making consistent adjustments and the possible lack of information about distortions, national and external accounts often refrain from approximating true market prices. Observed exchange values, even when motivated by global tax avoidance or other reasons and not based on market prices or commercial considerations, still represent an economic reality of their own.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A hypothetical affiliated-company invoice substantially below the price of comparable independent-market transactions illustrated potential transfer pricing and an unspecified income or capital transfer.
- A specialized component usable only within a particular production process illustrated why no reliable independent-market comparison may exist.
- Management services and technical know-how illustrated the difficulty of finding comparable market transactions.
- The lesson distinguished an adjustment that is desirable in principle from an adjustment that is feasible and defensible in practice.

**Key lesson:** Transfer prices can distort the measurement of transactions and income across economies. Market-equivalent adjustments may improve the accounts when distortions are material and reliable evidence exists, but they require judgment and consistent counterpart recording. When evidence or comparability is inadequate, compilers may retain observed enterprise values while recognizing their limitations.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. No specific multinational enterprise, tax arrangement or Nigerian transfer-pricing case was investigated. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist measurement design was undertaken.

### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 43: Valuation of produced non-financial assets

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §§4.155–4.156.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.155: Two basic approaches are distinguished for valuing produced non-financial assets: exchange prices for similar second-hand assets, and the contribution of capital services (including depreciation) to production over the asset's remaining service life. The latter is usually approximated through written-down replacement cost, adjusted for price changes. The perpetual inventory method (PIM), when properly applied, replicates the present value of future capital services derived from the asset.
- §4.156: For assets with active second-hand markets, such as generic transport equipment and dwellings, second-hand prices may closely follow values derived from capital services because economic agents can choose between new and used assets. Many production assets, however, are specialized and traded in very thin second-hand markets. Dismantling, transport and reinstallation costs may be substantial; observed prices may therefore be close to scrap value and fail to represent the remaining capital services or value of the asset in a going concern. The traded second-hand item may not be comparable to the asset as used in production.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- A hypothetical processing machine with a current replacement cost of ₦30 million and an illustrative written-down value of ₦18 million demonstrated the replacement-cost concept; this simplified arithmetic is not an official SNA depreciation calculation.
- A generic used truck illustrated an asset for which second-hand market prices may be informative.
- A specialized factory machine with a low scrap price illustrated why disposal value may fail to represent its productive value in continued use.

**Key lesson:** Current value of a produced asset is not necessarily its original cost or scrap price. Valuation should reflect comparable second-hand exchange prices where appropriate, or the value of remaining capital services, with PIM and written-down replacement cost providing a way to estimate the latter.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. Detailed PIM, depreciation and capital-services methods are reserved for later chapters, particularly Chapter 17. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist asset valuation model was undertaken.


### Stage 3 — Chapter 4, Lesson 44: Valuation of non-produced natural resources

An introductory lesson was completed on 2026-11-23 using the official 2025 SNA White Cover Version, Chapter 4, §4.157.

**Verified paragraph coverage:**
- §4.157: Non-produced natural resource stocks are generally not traded in markets. Occasional transactions in such stocks cannot necessarily be used to value similar resources because natural resources are heterogeneous. The balance-sheet value may be approximated by the present value of future economic benefits using the residual value method: output generated through exploitation minus all costs associated with that exploitation. Exploitation rights are often granted by government in return for rent payments. Those payments may not capture the full resource rents generated by the resource; the unit holding exploitation rights may appropriate part of the resource rents and future capital services. In such cases, the resource value is split between the legal owner (usually government) and the exploiting unit. The paragraph refers to Chapter 27, §§27.55–27.62 for further detail.

**Teaching illustrations (not source data):**
- Oil, gas and mineral deposits illustrated why a resource stock may not have a directly observable market price.
- A simplified hypothetical calculation illustrated the residual nature of resource rent after accounting for output and exploitation costs. It was a teaching aid, not a complete official compilation formula.
- A hypothetical three-year stream of future resource rents illustrated the present-value idea. Its numerical result was not a measured resource value.
- A hypothetical government-granted exploitation right illustrated how economic value may be split between the legal owner and the exploiting unit.

**Accuracy clarification:** The present-value formula and discounting illustration explain the underlying valuation concept; §4.157 itself states the residual value method in terms of output generated through exploitation minus all associated exploitation costs. The detailed specification of costs, resource-rent calculations, discounting assumptions and ownership allocation requires the relevant later chapters and should not be inferred from this introductory lesson alone.

**Key lesson:** Natural-resource stocks often lack directly observable market prices. Their value may be estimated from future economic benefits generated by exploitation, net of associated costs, and the total resource value may be shared between the legal owner and the unit holding exploitation rights.

**Study limitations:** Introductory only; no claim of mastery or exhaustive treatment. No particular Nigerian resource deposit, reserve estimate, licence, royalty arrangement or government contract was verified. No Nigeria implementation or Lifeconomist valuation model was undertaken.

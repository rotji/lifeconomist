**Date:** 2026-10-31  
**Status:** First narration draft for editorial and factual review only. Not approved for recording, production, or publication.  
**Audience:** Everyday bank customers — people who save, borrow, and use bank accounts.  
**Style:** Balanced documentary: clear narration, explanatory diagrams, restrained cinematic visuals.  
**Outline:** [Preliminary Explainer Outline](outline-bank-loan-deposit-creation.md)  
**Source audit:** [Source Audit — Commercial-Bank Loan and Deposit Creation](source-audit-bank-loan-deposit-creation.md)

## Editorial intent

Answer one everyday question accurately and accessibly: when a commercial bank grants a loan and credits a customer's account, where does that deposit come from? Avoid sensationalism, unsupported Nigeria-specific claims, and the implication that banks can create unlimited money.

## Draft narration and visual plan

### Scene 1 — The question

**Visual:** Restrained cinematic establishing shot of an ordinary bank exterior or a person checking a bank-account balance. Move quickly to the question on screen: “When a bank gives you a loan, where does the money come from?”

**Narration:**

When you borrow money from a bank, it is easy to imagine the bank taking money that other customers have saved and passing it to you.

Savings and funding matter to banks. But that picture leaves out something important about how modern commercial-bank money works.

In a common type of bank loan, when the bank approves the loan and credits your account, the bank creates a new deposit.

What does that mean in practice? Let's follow the entries.

### Scene 2 — Follow the entries

**Visual:** Show a simple, clearly labelled hypothetical example: a hypothetical loan of $100. Split the screen between “The bank” and “The borrower.”

**On-screen graphic:**

| The bank | Change |
|---|---:|
| Loan it expects to be repaid — an asset | +$100 |
| Deposit it owes to the customer — a liability | +$100 |

| The borrower | Change |
|---|---:|
| Deposit in the bank account — an asset | +$100 |
| Loan owed to the bank — a liability | +$100 |

**Narration:**

Imagine the bank grants a hypothetical loan of $100 and credits your account.

On the bank's side, it records a loan: you owe the bank $100. That is an asset for the bank because it has a claim on you. At the same time, it records a deposit of $100 in your account. That is a liability for the bank because the bank owes you that $100 account balance under the terms of your account.

On your side, the deposit is an asset you can use to make payments, and the loan is a debt you must repay.

The entries match. The bank has not simply handed you a debt-free gift. A new deposit has been created alongside a new obligation.

The $100 amount is hypothetical and is used only to make the accounting easier to see. Dollars are simply the denomination chosen for this teaching example; the figure is not banking data or a claim about any country's banking system.

### Scene 3 — What this does, and does not, mean

**Visual:** Keep the balance-sheet graphic on screen. Highlight “new deposit” and “new debt” together. Avoid money appearing from a magic box or imagery suggesting fraud.

**Narration:**

This is the key distinction: in this kind of transaction, the bank creates deposit money when it makes the loan. The bank is not simply moving another customer's existing deposit into your account. In the standard case we're describing, it records a new loan and a matching deposit.

But don't take that to mean a bank can lend any amount it wants.

Banks cannot lend without limit. They must judge whether borrowers are likely to repay, manage risks and costs, maintain sufficient capital, follow the rules that apply to them, and be able to make payments when due. Customer demand, profitability, funding conditions and monetary policy also influence lending.

If you use the deposit to pay someone who banks elsewhere, the banks may also need to settle the payment between themselves. Creating a deposit when a loan begins does not make those payment and liquidity responsibilities disappear.

### Scene 4 — Money is not the same as wealth

**Visual:** Move from the balance-sheet graphic to ordinary real-world activity: a shop receiving payment, a worker repairing equipment, or a household making a purchase. Use these as illustrative possibilities, not as claims about what every loan finances.

**Narration:**

There is one more important distinction. Creating a bank deposit is not the same as creating a new physical resource or guaranteeing that anyone becomes wealthier.

A loan gives the borrower access to funds, but it also creates a debt. What happens next depends on how the money is used, what it costs, whether the activity produces value, and whether the debt can be repaid.

Credit can finance useful activity. It can also finance spending or investments that do not work out. The accounting mechanism alone does not tell us which outcome will follow.

### Scene 5 — The answer

**Visual:** Return to the simple bank-and-borrower balance sheets. Show “deposit created” next to “debt incurred,” then close on the central question.

**Narration:**

So, when a commercial bank grants a loan and credits the borrower's account, it normally creates a matching bank deposit.

That is one way money is created in the modern economy. It is not unlimited, and it is not the same as creating real wealth. Banks still face risks, costs, regulation, funding and liquidity needs, and the practical question of whether borrowers can repay.

Understanding where bank money comes from is useful. Understanding the debt attached to it, the constraints on lending, and what the borrowing is used for is just as important.

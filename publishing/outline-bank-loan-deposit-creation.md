## 1. Decision question

Does this topic provide a clear, useful explanation for a broad audience, with enough room to show both the mechanism and its limits without turning into a general documentary about the entire monetary system?

**Working audience:** Curious non-specialists who use bank accounts and encounter loans, but may not know how commercial-bank deposits are created. This is a provisional audience assumption to revisit before scripting.

**One-sentence promise:** Explain what changes on a bank's and borrower's balance sheets when a commercial bank grants a loan, and why this does not mean banks can create unlimited money.

## 2. Core takeaway

In the standard case where a commercial bank grants a loan and credits a customer's deposit account, the bank records a loan asset and a matching deposit liability. The borrower records a deposit asset and a matching debt liability. This creates a bank deposit, but not a free gift, unlimited lending capacity, or automatic creation of real wealth.

The public explanation should make the mechanism understandable rather than portray it as a secret or conspiracy.

## 3. Proposed narrative sequence

### Beat 1 — Start with the everyday puzzle

**Question on screen:** When you borrow money from a bank, where did the money come from?

Briefly acknowledge the familiar idea that banks collect savings and lend them to borrowers. State that this is incomplete as a description of how modern commercial-bank lending creates deposit money.

**Evidence:** Bank of England (2014), overview and section on misconceptions; Bank of England public explainer.

**Visual:** A simple question and a neutral illustration of a bank account balance changing. Do not use fake news headlines, alarmist imagery, or claims that the banking system is a scam.

### Beat 2 — Show the loan being made

Use a hypothetical loan of $100, explicitly labelled as an illustration rather than real-world data.

**Bank entries:**
- Loan asset: +$100
- Customer deposit liability: +$100

**Borrower entries:**
- Deposit asset: +$100
- Loan liability: +$100

**Evidence:** Bank of England (2014), balance-sheet example; IMF Working Paper 19/285, balance-sheet mechanics.

**Visual:** Two side-by-side balance sheets, with matching entries highlighted. The graphic should make clear that the bank's asset is the borrower's obligation and the bank's liability is the borrower's deposit.

**Plain-language explanation:** The bank has promised to honour the $100 deposit and has acquired a claim on the borrower for $100. The borrower can use the deposit to make payments, but also owes the $100 loan. The entries balance; the borrower has not received a debt-free windfall.

### Beat 3 — Correct the first misconception

**Claim to communicate:** In this standard case, the bank does not need to transfer a pre-existing customer's deposit into the borrower's account at the instant the loan is originated. The loan transaction creates a matching deposit.

**Evidence:** Bank of England (2014); Bank of England explainer.

**Caveat:** This does not mean funding, deposits, or other liabilities are irrelevant to a bank's business. Distinguish the accounting at loan origination from how the bank manages its balance sheet and settles later payments.

### Beat 4 — Show why the system still has constraints

Explain briefly that a borrower may spend the deposit at a business whose account is at another bank. The payment can cause a transfer of central-bank reserves between banks for settlement. Banks must manage liquidity and funding as well as credit risk, capital and regulation, profitability, customer demand, and monetary-policy conditions.

**Evidence:** Bank of England (2014), sections on limits and settlement; IMF Working Paper 19/285 for the coexistence of deposit creation and interbank liquid-funding needs.

**Visual:** Borrower pays a seller at another bank; the customer deposit appears at the receiving bank and interbank settlement is represented separately. Do not depict the transfer as the borrower directly moving central-bank reserves.

**Plain-language explanation:** Creating a deposit when a loan begins is not the same as being able to ignore payments leaving the bank. If money is paid to someone banking elsewhere, the banks must settle with one another and manage their available liquidity. The bank also needs loans likely to be repaid and must operate within economic and regulatory constraints.

### Beat 5 — Correct the second misconception: money is not wealth

A loan creates a financial asset and a matching liability. It does not, by itself, create a new physical resource or guarantee a productive outcome. The borrower may use it to purchase something, invest, refinance another obligation, or consume; consequences depend on what happens next.

**Evidence:** Bank of England public explainer; balance-sheet logic from the source audit.

**Visual:** Separate “financial entries” from “real-world outcome.” Use a neutral example rather than claiming that every loan finances productive investment or that debt is always harmful.

### Beat 6 — Close with the limits and a concise answer

Restate the answer in careful terms:

“When a commercial bank grants a loan and credits the borrower's account, it normally creates a matching bank deposit. That deposit is accompanied by a debt, and the bank remains constrained by risk, capital, profitability, funding and liquidity, regulation, demand, and monetary policy.”

End with a question that encourages understanding rather than fear: “When evaluating credit, what matters alongside where the money comes from is what the borrowing finances, its cost, and whether it can be repaid.”

## 5. What this outline intentionally leaves out

- Current Nigerian money-supply statistics or claims about specific Nigerian banking regulations.
- Detailed reserve-management procedures, payment-system architecture, central-bank facilities, and bank liquidity ratios.
- The entire history of money, monetary schools of thought, fractional-reserve banking debates, quantitative easing, and central-bank digital currencies.
- Claims that banks create money “out of nothing” without immediately explaining the balance-sheet entries and constraints.
- Claims that bank money creation is secret, fraudulent, or unlimited.
- An exhaustive explanation of bank profitability, credit risk models, or the macroeconomic effects of credit cycles.

These topics can be explored separately if they become necessary. They should not be appended simply because they are related.

## 6. Visual and editorial guardrails

- Use diagrams to explain mechanisms, not to decorate unsupported claims.
- Any numbers must be explicitly labelled as hypothetical unless sourced as actual data.
- Keep the bank, borrower, seller, deposits, loans, and reserves visually distinct.
- Every arrow or transaction chain should be followed by plain-language explanation.
- Distinguish a balance-sheet identity from a prediction about behaviour or economic outcomes.
- Avoid sensational claims and cinematic visuals that imply wrongdoing not established by evidence.
- Verify generated or stock visuals do not contain misleading labels, fake financial data, or invented documents.
- Add citations on screen or in the description for material factual claims, and preserve a full source list in the project.
- Recheck source passages against the final script, because the final wording may change the evidence needed.

## 9. Editorial scope review (2026-10-31)

### Assessment

**Decision: Retain the candidate, narrow the first explainer, and do not start production yet.**

The outline has a strong central question and a visible mechanism. Its main risk is not lack of material, but trying to explain too much at once. The detailed payment-settlement example could turn a first explanation of deposit creation into a lesson on the full banking system.

### Revised scope for a first explanation

Keep the main narrative to four moves:

1. **The question:** Where does the money come from when a commercial bank grants a loan?
2. **The mechanism:** Show the matching loan asset and deposit liability for the bank, and deposit asset and loan liability for the borrower.
3. **The limit:** Explain in plain language that creating a deposit is not unlimited lending. Briefly name the main constraints without explaining every regulatory or operational mechanism.
4. **The meaning:** Explain that the borrower has a deposit and a debt, and that financial entries alone do not create real resources or guarantee a productive result.

The interbank payment/settlement point should be a brief supporting sentence or a small optional visual, not a full narrative beat in the first version. Explain only that a payment to a customer at another bank may require the banks to settle between themselves and manage liquidity. Avoid detailed reserve mechanics unless they prove essential to prevent a misconception.

Loan repayment can be omitted from the main sequence if it makes the explanation crowded. It can be mentioned in a short closing note or saved for a separate future question.

### Audience and value

The working audience remains curious non-specialists who use bank accounts and encounter loans. That is specific enough for an outline, but before scripting we should decide whether to speak primarily to everyday bank customers, students learning economics, or people trying to understand the financial system. Do not attempt to serve all three equally in one short explanation.

The topic has a plausible audience value because it replaces an incomplete everyday mental model with a transparent balance-sheet example. However, audience demand has not been tested. We should describe the candidate as educationally promising, not proven popular.

### Evidence and visual review

- The Bank of England's 2014 article directly supports the core mechanism and cautions against both the “banks simply lend out savers' deposits” account and a mechanical reserve-multiplier explanation. Its discussion of constraints is also useful. [Source](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy)
- The IMF working paper supports the distinction between bank lending/deposit creation and nonbank lending, and explains why liquid funding needs in a multi-bank system can coexist with deposit creation. It is a working paper whose views do not necessarily represent the IMF. [Source](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2019/12/20/money-creation-in-fiat-and-digital-currency-systems-48843)
- The $100 loan example must be clearly labelled hypothetical. Dollars are the chosen teaching denomination, not a claim about any country's banking data or rules. Show the asset/liability relationship accurately; do not label the loan as “free money.”
- The phrase “money creation” should be explained immediately in ordinary language. Avoid sensational framing such as “banks create money from nothing” unless the precise accounting meaning and limits are explained in the same breath.
- No Nigeria-specific regulatory or statistical claims are needed for this first version. Leave them out rather than imply the UK source describes Nigeria.

### Format decision

A narrated faceless video is a plausible format because the balance-sheet mechanism benefits from a simple animated diagram. A written explainer or single diagram could still communicate the core idea more efficiently. The eventual script should be short enough to answer the question without padding; exact duration is deliberately not fixed at this stage.

### Gate result

**Suitable to proceed to a short draft script only if the user wants to continue.** The topic remains provisional, audience demand is untested, and the script must retain claim-level source checks. This review does not authorize recording, visual generation, paid-tool use, or publication.

## 10. Audience and explanatory style decision (2026-10-31)

**Decision recorded:** The user selected:
- **Primary audience:** Everyday bank customers — people who save, borrow, and use bank accounts.
- **Explanatory style:** Balanced documentary — clear narration, diagrams, and restrained cinematic visuals.

### Implications for the outline

- Begin with a familiar question about a bank loan and an account balance, not technical terminology.
- Explain “deposit,” “loan asset,” and “liability” when first introduced, using everyday language before formal labels.
- Keep the hypothetical balance-sheet example visible long enough for viewers to follow it.
- Use narration to explain what the diagram means; never rely on arrows or labels alone.
- Use cinematic visuals only to establish context or transitions. Use clear diagrams, not dramatic imagery, to communicate financial mechanics.
- Keep technical details such as interbank settlement brief and secondary.
- Avoid assuming the viewer knows monetary aggregates, reserves, or balance-sheet accounting.
- Do not oversimplify the constraints: name them plainly, then explain only those needed for the central point.

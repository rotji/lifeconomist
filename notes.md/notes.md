1. The first principle: debt is simultaneously an asset and a liability
Suppose the U.S. Treasury owes a pension fund $1 billion.

Treasury: $1B liability
Pension fund: $1B asset
Nothing mysterious has happened yet.

But the crucial question becomes:

Can the borrower continue servicing the liability without increasingly damaging the rest of the system?

That's where Dalio's framework becomes useful.

A country's debt doesn't become problematic merely because the absolute number is large.

The more important relationships are:

Debt / income

New borrowing / available savings

Interest rate / economic growth

Asset values / liabilities

Government spending / government revenue

2. The really important concept: debt service
Imagine a business:

Revenue = $100
Expenses excluding debt = $70
Debt service = $10
Free cash flow = $20
Now interest rates rise and debt service becomes $20.

Nothing happened to revenue.

But the business suddenly has only:

$100 − $70 − $20 = $10

available.

The debt hasn't necessarily increased.

The cash-flow burden increased.

That's the mechanical point Dalio keeps returning to.

At sufficiently high debt-service levels, something else has to give.

housing

government programs

corporate expansion
asset purchases

The existing debt doesn't need to triple for the interest burden to triple.

The price of refinancing does it.

Interest is the price.

There doesn't have to be a single magic number.

Economic Machine — Mechanisms and Feedback Loops V1
We have established the structure of the Economic Machine. Now we need to understand how its parts interact, how one change spreads through the system, and how those interactions can reinforce or weaken the original change.

This is an important step before building an observation or monitoring system. Otherwise, we might collect economic data without understanding what the data actually tells us.

1. First, understand what a feedback loop is
A feedback loop occurs when an initial change travels through a system and eventually affects the conditions that produced the original change.

There are two major types.

A. Reinforcing loop

Amplifies change
An initial change produces effects that strengthen the original change. This can create rapid growth, a boom, a downward spiral, or a crisis.

B. Balancing loop

Resists or stabilizes change
An initial change produces a response that counteracts it. This can limit overheating, stabilize prices, or prevent a problem from becoming larger.

For example, rising prices can cause a central bank to raise interest rates. Higher rates can reduce borrowing and spending, which may ease demand pressure on prices. That is a balancing mechanism, although it takes time and does not always work as intended.

Important distinction: an economic chain describes how effects may travel; a feedback loop shows how those effects can eventually influence their own starting conditions.

2. The ten major mechanisms and feedback loops
Loop 1 — The real-economy income loop
Spending

Production and demand for workers
Household and business income
More capacity to spend
Spending can increase again

Plain-language translation: When households and businesses spend more, businesses may sell more, produce more, and hire more workers. Those workers and businesses earn income, some of which is spent again. This can support further economic activity.

Mechanism: One person's spending becomes another person's revenue or income. The effect depends on whether businesses can meet the extra demand and whether the income is spent domestically, saved, used to repay debt, or spent on imports.

Qualification: More spending does not automatically create more real wealth. If production cannot keep up, the result may be higher prices rather than proportionately higher output.

Example: If more people buy goods from local retailers, retailers may restock more frequently, wholesalers may receive more orders, and distributors may hire additional workers. But if the goods are imported, some of the extra spending flows abroad.

Loop 2 — The credit boom loop
Credit becomes easier to obtain
Borrowing and spending increase
Business revenue and/or asset prices rise
Borrowers appear more creditworthy; collateral may be worth more
Lenders may extend more credit

Plain-language translation: When loans are easy to get, people and businesses can spend money they have not yet earned. This can increase sales and push up asset prices. If lenders believe borrowers are safer because their incomes or collateral values have risen, they may offer even more loans.

Mechanism: Credit can support both economic activity and asset purchases. Rising collateral values can make additional borrowing possible, creating a reinforcing loop.

Qualification: The loop is not guaranteed. Banks can tighten lending, incomes may fail to grow, and asset prices can rise much faster than the cash flows needed to support them. Credit-funded demand can also create inflation.

Example: A property boom can make buildings more expensive. Owners can then borrow against higher property valuations, and buyers may borrow larger sums. If property prices subsequently fall, the same mechanism can operate in reverse.

Loop 3 — The credit contraction loop
Interest rates rise or lending standards tighten
Borrowing and interest-sensitive spending weaken
Sales, incomes and asset prices may fall
Debt repayment becomes harder; collateral may lose value
Lending may contract further

Plain-language translation: If loans become more expensive or harder to obtain, households may delay purchases and businesses may postpone investment. Lower sales can reduce income, while falling asset prices can weaken the collateral supporting existing loans. Banks may then become even more cautious.

Mechanism: The key is the interaction between borrowing costs, cash flow, collateral and lenders' willingness to take risk.

Qualification: Higher interest rates do not always produce a recession. The impact depends on existing debt levels, fixed versus variable interest rates, savings, government policy, and how strong underlying income and productivity are.

Example: A business that borrowed heavily to expand may face higher repayments. If customers also reduce spending, the business can suffer a double pressure: less revenue and more expensive debt.

Loop 4 — Inflation, central banks and interest rates
Demand grows faster than available supply
Prices face upward pressure
Central bank may raise interest rates
Credit and interest-sensitive spending may weaken
Demand pressure may ease

Plain-language translation: When people and businesses want to buy more than the economy can supply, prices can rise. A central bank may respond by making borrowing more expensive. That can slow demand and reduce inflationary pressure.

Mechanism: Monetary policy influences financial conditions, borrowing, spending, investment and expectations. These effects travel through the economy with delays.

Qualification: Inflation can also come from supply disruptions, energy prices, exchange-rate depreciation or rising production costs. Higher interest rates cannot directly produce more oil, repair a port or remove a supply bottleneck. They may reduce overall demand while the original supply problem persists.

Example: If food prices rise because of a poor harvest, interest-rate increases might restrain general demand, but they do not restore the missing harvest.

Loop 5 — The asset-price and collateral loop
Asset prices rise
Owners' reported wealth and collateral values increase
Borrowing capacity and willingness to spend may increase
Demand for assets may rise further

Plain-language translation: When a house, stock portfolio or other asset becomes more valuable, its owner may feel wealthier and may be able to borrow more against it. That can encourage more spending or further asset purchases, which may push prices higher again.

Mechanism: This loop connects financial-market valuations to the real economy through wealth, collateral and credit.

Qualification: A higher market valuation is not the same as cash in the bank. Owners may be unable to sell at the quoted price, and using an asset as collateral creates repayment obligations. When prices fall, the process can reverse.

Example: If shares rise sharply, an investor may feel wealthier. But if the investor needs cash during a market decline, selling shares can realize a loss. This is why Dalio distinguishes wealth from money available to pay obligations.

Loop 6 — Debt service and the cash-flow squeeze
Debt and interest obligations grow
More income goes to debt service
Less money is available for consumption and investment
Economic income growth may weaken
Debt becomes harder to service relative to income

Plain-language translation: The problem with debt is not simply how much is owed. It is also how much cash must be paid to service that debt compared with how much income is available. If repayments consume an increasing share of income, less remains for other activities.

Mechanism: Debt creates contractual claims on future cash flow. When those claims grow faster than income, the borrower has less room to absorb shocks.

Qualification: Debt can be productive when it finances investments that generate sufficient future income. A government, company or household with manageable fixed-rate obligations is not in the same position as one facing large near-term repayments or rapidly repricing interest rates.

Example: A government may continue funding essential services, but higher interest payments can leave less budget space for infrastructure or education. If the government borrows to cover the gap, its financing needs may increase further.

Loop 7 — Productivity and productive investment
Investment in skills, equipment, technology and infrastructure
Workers and businesses can produce more efficiently
Output or quality may improve; unit costs may fall
Higher real income and returns may support further investment
Productive capacity can expand further

Plain-language translation: An economy becomes more capable of generating real wealth when people can produce more useful output with the same resources, or produce better output with fewer resources. Successful investment can improve this capacity and make further investment worthwhile.

Mechanism: Productivity growth can expand the economy's sustainable supply, supporting real income and living standards over time.

Qualification: Not every investment improves productivity. Projects can fail, equipment can sit idle, debt can be excessive, and productivity gains may be unevenly distributed. Benefits may also take years to appear.

Example: Reliable electricity can enable a Nigerian manufacturer to reduce generator costs, increase operating hours and produce more goods. But the gain depends on equipment, skills, demand, maintenance and the reliability of the electricity supply.

This loop is especially important because it differs from a credit boom. Credit can increase spending today; productivity can increase the amount the economy is capable of producing over time. They can reinforce each other, but they are not interchangeable.

Loop 8 — Government deficits and capital demand
Government spending exceeds revenue
Government needs financing
Government borrows from available pools of capital
Financing costs and private-sector funding conditions may change
Growth, revenue and future borrowing needs may change

Plain-language translation: When a government spends more than it collects, it generally needs to finance the difference. If borrowing needs are large relative to the savings and capital available at acceptable prices, interest rates may face upward pressure. That can make borrowing more expensive for companies and households too.

Mechanism: Government financing interacts with central-bank policy, domestic savings, foreign investment, bank balance sheets and private-sector demand for capital.

Qualification: Government borrowing does not mechanically push interest rates up in every situation. Rates also depend on inflation expectations, central-bank purchases or policy, demand for safe assets, foreign capital flows and the economic outlook. Borrowing can also support productive infrastructure that improves future growth and revenue.

Example: If a government and major technology companies are both seeking large amounts of financing, they may compete for some of the same investors' capital. Whether this raises borrowing costs depends on the scale of financing needs and the willingness of investors to supply funds.

This is the core of Dalio's question about the U.S. Treasury market: not simply whether a particular yield is “too high,” but how the demand for financing compares with the capital available, and at what price investors are willing to provide it.

Loop 9 — The international trade and currency loop
Domestic demand and production change
Imports, exports and foreign-currency needs change
Exchange rates and capital flows may respond
Import costs, inflation and financing conditions may change
Domestic demand and production may change again

Plain-language translation: An economy is connected to the rest of the world. Changes in local demand affect how much it imports; changes in production affect what it can export. These flows influence demand for foreign currencies and can affect exchange rates, prices and financing conditions. Those effects can then feed back into the domestic economy.

Mechanism: Trade, exchange rates and capital flows connect the domestic economy to global economic and financial conditions.

Qualification: A trade deficit does not automatically cause a currency to fall. Capital inflows, interest rates, commodity exports, foreign reserves, expectations and government policy can all influence the outcome.

Example: If Nigeria's oil-export earnings fall while demand for imported fuel, machinery and other goods remains high, foreign-currency availability may come under pressure. If the naira depreciates, imported goods and production inputs can become more expensive, potentially affecting inflation and business activity.

This is one reason we cannot understand a country's economy by examining only its internal transactions.

Loop 10 — Financial stress and the policy response
Borrowers, banks or markets come under stress
Credit tightens; asset sales or demands for cash may increase
Authorities may respond with liquidity support, rate changes or other measures
Financing conditions and confidence may change
Stress may ease—or continue to spread

Plain-language translation: When financial institutions or borrowers struggle to meet obligations, authorities may intervene to prevent a local problem from spreading. The intervention can restore confidence and liquidity, but it may not fix the underlying debt or solvency problem.

Mechanism: Banks and markets are connected through lending, collateral, payment obligations and shared exposures. A failure in one area can therefore affect other areas. Policy responses then alter the conditions under which the rest of the system operates.

Qualification: Providing liquidity can help a solvent institution that cannot obtain cash quickly. It may not rescue an institution whose assets are fundamentally worth less than its liabilities. Different problems require different responses.

Example: If a sharp market decline triggers collateral calls, some investors may have to sell assets to raise cash. Those sales can put further downward pressure on prices. A central bank may supply liquidity to functioning markets, but that does not guarantee that asset prices will recover or every borrower will survive.

This loop also connects to the derivatives discussion we had earlier: derivative losses can generate margin calls, which create immediate cash demands and can force asset sales. The risk can spread beyond the original market movement.

3. What the ten loops reveal about the Economic Machine
The loops are not ten independent systems. They interact.

For instance, a rise in interest rates can operate through several mechanisms simultaneously:

Interest rates rise

Borrowing

New loans and refinancing may become more expensive.

Bonds

Existing fixed-rate bonds generally lose market value.

Asset valuations

Future cash flows may be discounted at a higher rate.

Currency and capital flows

Investor incentives and exchange rates may change.

Combined result: These effects can influence spending, inflation, government financing, company profits, collateral and financial stability.

In normal words, an interest-rate change is not just a central-bank event. It can change the cost of borrowing, the value of existing financial assets, the attractiveness of different investments and the ability of borrowers to meet obligations. These effects can reinforce one another or partially offset one another.

The result depends on the starting conditions. A highly indebted economy with large refinancing needs may respond very differently from an economy with low debt, strong productivity and abundant savings.

An absorber is a person, institution, market or resource that initially bears the effect of a change.

5. Our working template for investigating any economic event
We can now establish a common template that we will reuse across countries, industries, companies and financial markets.

Initial condition: What was happening before the change?

Trigger: What changed first?

Mechanism: Through what economic process does the change matter?

Transmission: Through which markets, institutions, contracts or supply chains does it travel?

Absorbers: Who initially bears the costs, benefits or risks?

Responses: How do households, businesses, banks, investors and governments react?

Feedback: Do those reactions amplify the initial change or counteract it?

New state: What conditions exist after the effects have travelled through the system?

For example, if a government announces a large increase in borrowing, we would not immediately conclude that interest rates must rise or that equities must fall. We would investigate the financing requirement, the investors likely to fund it, the supply of available capital, central-bank policy, private-sector borrowing demand, the currency and the resulting changes in yields, credit conditions and cash flows.

That is how we move from a headline to a defensible explanation.

We have mapped the major mechanisms and feedback loops. Now we need to answer a different question:

Given all those mechanisms, what condition is the economy currently in, and how might that condition evolve?

This is where we introduce the Economic State Model. It is not a system for predicting the future with certainty. It is a way to organize evidence, recognize economic conditions, and understand which mechanisms are likely to matter most.

For example:

State: An economy is experiencing monetary tightening, slowing credit growth and weakening demand.

The mechanism helps explain why the state may have developed and what could happen next.

5. How to classify a state without guessing
We should not say that an economy is overheating just because inflation increased in one month, or that it is in a credit crisis because one bank failed.

For every proposed state, we need four elements:

1. Evidence

What observable data supports the classification? For example, credit growth, employment, inflation, interest expense or lending standards.

2. Mechanism

Why does that evidence matter? Which feedback loop connects it to the wider economy?

3. Counterevidence

What evidence suggests the classification might be wrong or incomplete?

4. Direction of change

Is the condition strengthening, weakening or remaining broadly stable? One data point rarely establishes a trend.

This also requires distinguishing leading indicators, which may signal future changes; coincident indicators, which describe current activity; and lagging indicators, which often confirm changes after they have begun.

For instance, new business orders may weaken before official unemployment rises. If we wait only for unemployment data, we may identify a slowdown later than we otherwise could.

Productivity growth
Long-term

Medium-term

Long-term debt cycle
Structural

Current state → new evidence or shock → activated mechanism → transmission → response → possible next state.

This project is a small macro-asset simulation prototype designed to explore how broad economic conditions influence portfolio performance.

## What it is about

The backend models a simplified economy in which three macro variables drive asset behavior:

- inflation
- interest rate
- economic growth

A macro state is updated with a shock vector using a basic equation:

- next state = current state + shock

The project then maps these macro changes to asset returns using exposure vectors. In the code, the default assets are:

- Bonds
- Stocks

Each asset has a sensitivity to the macro variables, and its expected return is calculated by taking the dot product of its exposure vector and the macro shock vector. This makes the model behave like a compact factor-based asset engine.

## Main components

The backend is organized around a few modules:

- `macro_engine.py`: updates macro variables and converts macro state/shocks into numeric vectors
- `asset_engine.py`: defines asset exposures, expected return calculations, and risk scoring
- `allocation.py`: allocates portfolio weights based on risk-adjusted expected returns
- `simulation.py`: runs a hand-calculation example and a multi-step simulation

The logic is deliberately simple and educational rather than production-grade. It demonstrates a reusable pattern:

1. Macro conditions change over time.
2. Asset exposures determine sensitivity to those changes.
3. Expected returns are computed from the exposure model.
4. Risk scores are estimated from exposure magnitude.
5. Weighting logic assigns a portfolio allocation based on the best risk-adjusted opportunities.
6. Portfolio return is aggregated over time.

## Example behavior

The included simulation starts from a base macro state and applies a sequence of shocks. It then prints:

- the updated macro state
- the shock vector
- expected returns for each asset
- risk scores
- the derived asset weights
- the portfolio return estimate

This gives a simple scenario-based view of how inflation, rates, and growth changes can affect asset allocation decisions.

This repository is best understood as a prototype macro-finance / asset-allocation toy model: a practical educational exercise for showing how macroeconomic variables can be translated into asset-level returns and portfolio allocation decisions. I would look for:

Decision-making evidence: records showing how particular banks or groups influenced a policy decision.

Coordination evidence: communications or agreements demonstrating prohibited coordination, rather than merely parallel interests.

Benefit evidence: analysis identifying which institutions benefited, by how much and compared with plausible alternatives.

Governance evidence: documentation of actual control, conflicts of interest, or improper influence over decision-makers.

Legal analysis: relevant statutes, court decisions and expert analysis establishing whether the conduct violates applicable law.

## 1. Concept: what are we measuring?

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the value of final goods and services produced within a country's economic territory over a defined period, usually a quarter or a year.

Real GDP growth measures how much that production has increased or decreased after accounting for changes in prices.

Why is the adjustment necessary?

Imagine that Nigerian businesses produce the same quantity of goods and services this year as last year, but prices increase by 20%. The money value of production may rise substantially even though the economy has not produced more.

Real GDP attempts to separate changes in production from changes in prices. Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) compiles GDP using national-accounts methods and publishes quarterly and annual economic measures.&#x20;

### Why this indicator matters to our model

Real GDP growth gives us an initial measure of the direction and pace of aggregate economic activity.

- Positive growth indicates that measured real output increased over the comparison period.
- Negative growth indicates that measured real output decreased.
- Slowing positive growth indicates that output is still increasing, but at a slower rate.
- Strong growth does not automatically mean that households are becoming wealthier or that the economy is financially healthy.

The key distinction: GDP growth measures aggregate production, not the complete condition of an economy. Credit, debt-service burdens, productivity, productive investment, inflation and other indicators will help us interpret it.

## 2. Operational definition

For the Economic State Transition Model, I propose the following initial definition:

> Real GDP growth is the percentage change in Nigeria's inflation-adjusted GDP between two specified periods, measured using the latest available official national-accounts series, with the comparison period, data vintage, methodology and revision status recorded.

This definition deliberately requires us to identify the comparison period. “GDP growth” without that information is incomplete.

We will maintain two separate measures:

| Measure                       | Calculation                                                      | Primary use                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Annual growth                 | This year's real GDP versus the previous year's real GDP         | Longer-term economic direction                              |
| Quarterly year-on-year growth | This quarter's real GDP versus the same quarter one year earlier | More frequent monitoring with seasonality partly controlled |

Quarter-on-quarter growth can also be recorded, but we must distinguish the raw quarterly rate from a seasonally adjusted and annualized rate. They are not interchangeable.

## 3. Formula and numerical example

The general formula is:

\\[ g_t=\left(\frac{Y_t^{R}}{Y\_{t-1}^{R}}-1\right)\times100 \\]

Where:

- \\(g_t\\) = real GDP growth in percent.
- \\(Y_t^{R}\\) = real GDP in the current period.
- \\(Y\_{t-1}^{R}\\) = real GDP in the specified comparison period.

For year-on-year quarterly growth, the comparison period is the same quarter in the previous year.

### An important distinction: reference year and constant-price series

Nigeria's 2019 rebasing is not merely a cosmetic change to a spreadsheet. Rebasing and rebenchmarking can incorporate new surveys, previously underrepresented activities, revised weights and updated statistical methods. NBS documentation describes the use of a 2019 reference year and a broader production and product framework.&#x20;

For our measurement protocol, we should therefore:

- Prefer the latest internally consistent, rebased official series.
- Avoid joining older and newer series until their compatibility has been checked.
- Preserve historical releases when available so we can later reconstruct what an analyst knew at a particular date.
- Record methodological breaks instead of treating them as economic shocks.

The last point matters greatly. If measured GDP changes because the statistical agency improves its coverage, we must not mistake the entire revision for a sudden change in actual economic activity.

## 6. Revisions and uncertainty

GDP figures are estimates based on surveys, administrative records, national-accounting procedures and other information. They can be revised as better information becomes available.

The IMF also documents that historical series may change, estimates can be used where information is incomplete, and its figures can differ from national sources.&#x20;

Our protocol should distinguish two questions:

1. What is our best current estimate of historical growth? Use the latest appropriate series.
2. What did we know at the time? Use the historical release or data vintage available on that date, when retrievable.

Both matter. The first helps us understand economic history; the second will eventually help us test whether our model could have identified a transition in real time, without relying on information published later.

## 7. Limitations and interpretation cautions

Real GDP growth is necessary, but insufficient, for identifying economic states.

- Aggregate versus per-person output: Total GDP can grow while real GDP per capita stagnates or falls if population grows faster.
- Output versus prosperity: GDP growth does not directly establish how income is distributed or whether household purchasing power improved.
- Output versus financial health: An economy can grow while businesses, households or government face heavy debt-service burdens.
- Sector differences: Aggregate growth can conceal contraction in important sectors or regions.
- Measurement uncertainty: Informal activity, incomplete reporting and methodological changes can affect estimates.
- Causation: A change in GDP does not, by itself, identify whether credit, productivity, government spending, exports or another mechanism caused it.

These are reasons to combine the indicator with our other five foundational indicators and later evidence about economic mechanisms—not reasons to discard GDP growth.

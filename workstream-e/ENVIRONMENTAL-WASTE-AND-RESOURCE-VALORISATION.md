# Waste, By-products, Reuse and Resource Valorisation

**Parent map:** [Environmental Opportunity Intelligence](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-INTELLIGENCE.md)  
**Territory:** 3.2 — Waste, by-products, reuse, and resource valorisation  
**Status:** Initial horizontal map completed on 2026-11-06. This is a broad navigation map, not an exhaustive taxonomy, validated market assessment, or selection of approved opportunities.

## 1. Purpose and boundary

Map the systems through which discarded materials, residuals, surplus products, and by-products are prevented, collected, handled, reused, repaired, processed, recovered, exchanged, converted, treated, or disposed of.

The territory includes both the material streams and the services, infrastructure, technologies, markets, and information systems around them. It is not limited to recycling or waste-to-energy. Prevention and product-life extension matter alongside end-of-pipe treatment.

Waste is not automatically a resource. Its potential value depends on composition, quantity, consistency, contamination, location, collection and handling costs, processing requirements, safety, regulation, and the existence of a user or buyer.

## 2. Initial broad subcategory map

### 3.2.1 Municipal and household waste
Household refuse, food scraps, packaging, plastics, paper, cardboard, glass, metals, textiles, bulky waste, and other municipal solid waste.

### 3.2.2 Commercial and institutional waste
Waste from shops, markets, offices, schools, hospitals, hotels, restaurants, public institutions, and other service organizations. Streams may be more concentrated or predictable than household waste, but must be assessed by site and activity.

### 3.2.3 Agricultural and forestry residues
Crop residues, husks, shells, stalks, leaves, cobs, bagasse, pruning residues, sawdust, offcuts, bark, and other biomass left after agricultural or forestry activities.

### 3.2.4 Food waste and food-processing by-products
Rejected produce, peels, seeds, shells, pulp, whey, spent grain, processing water, surplus food, and residues from slaughtering, milling, brewing, and other food operations. Food safety and contamination determine which pathways are permissible.

### 3.2.5 Industrial waste and by-products
Manufacturing residues, process waste, off-specification products, slag, ash, scrap, spent process materials, and recoverable co-products. The composition and process history can be decisive for safety and reuse.

### 3.2.6 Construction and demolition waste
Concrete, masonry, bricks, timber, metals, glass, gypsum, asphalt, excavated soil, and mixed demolition materials. Sorting, contamination, grading, transport distance, and local construction specifications affect recovery options.

### 3.2.7 Mining and extractive-industry residues
Tailings, waste rock, overburden, mineral-processing residues, quarry fines, and other extraction-related materials. These may contain recoverable minerals but can also present geochemical, dust, drainage, and long-term liability risks.

### 3.2.8 Electronic and electrical waste
Discarded phones, computers, appliances, electrical equipment, circuit boards, cables, components, and batteries. Reuse and material recovery coexist with hazardous-substance, fire, worker-safety, and regulated handling concerns.

### 3.2.9 Waste collection and reverse logistics
Collection, aggregation, routing, transport, transfer, storage, take-back, deposit-return, and return flows from consumers or businesses to repair, reuse, recovery, or treatment destinations.

### 3.2.10 Sorting, separation, and preprocessing
Manual and automated sorting, grading, screening, shredding, crushing, washing, dewatering, baling, densification, and preparation of materials to meet downstream specifications.

### 3.2.11 Reuse, repair, and product-life extension
Direct reuse, resale, repair, refurbishment, remanufacturing, component harvesting, parts recovery, and maintenance models that prevent products or materials from becoming waste prematurely.

### 3.2.12 Recycling and material recovery
Mechanical, chemical, biological, and other material-recovery pathways for metals, plastics, paper, glass, textiles, construction materials, batteries, and other streams. Recovered output quality and actual substitution of virgin materials matter.

### 3.2.13 Waste-to-energy and resource conversion
Anaerobic digestion, landfill-gas recovery, combustion, gasification, pyrolysis, carbonisation, and other conversion routes that may produce biogas, heat, electricity, fuels, oils, chars, or other outputs. Each pathway requires feedstock-specific technical, environmental, safety, regulatory, and economic assessment.

### 3.2.14 Safe treatment and final disposal
Treatment and controlled management of hazardous and non-hazardous waste, residuals that cannot be recovered, contaminated materials, and disposal facilities. This includes containment, treatment, landfill operations, and long-term monitoring where required.

### 3.2.15 By-product exchange and industrial symbiosis
Finding and qualifying opportunities for one organization's residual material, heat, water, or by-product to become another organization's input. Matching requires compatible specifications, reliable volumes, logistics, legal status, quality assurance, and continuity of supply.

### 3.2.16 Waste intelligence and traceability
Waste audits, material-flow inventories, stream characterisation, facility and operator mapping, traceability, compliance records, pricing information, supplier-buyer discovery, and data products supporting operational or procurement decisions.

## 3. Cross-cutting questions for later vertical exploration

The following questions are retained for the later vertical phase; they are not being answered exhaustively now:

- What are the material's composition, quantity, variability, moisture, contamination, and seasonal pattern?
- Where and by whom is it generated, controlled, stored, and currently handled?
- What does the current disposal or handling route cost, and who pays?
- What collection radius and transport method are realistic?
- What preparation or processing is required, and what are its yields, energy, water, labour, and maintenance needs?
- What outputs and residuals result, and what specifications must they meet?
- Who uses or buys the recovered output, what does it replace, and what evidence demonstrates repeat demand?
- What permits, standards, safety controls, environmental obligations, and liability apply?
- Could value be captured through prevention, collection, aggregation, logistics, repair, testing, data, coordination, processing, distribution, or final treatment rather than owning a processing plant?

## 4. Opportunity forms visible across this territory

Potential positions include waste audits and stream mapping; collection and aggregation coordination; sorting and preprocessing; repair and resale; equipment supply and maintenance; contract processing; material brokerage and offtake coordination; testing and quality assurance; traceability and compliance support; facility operations; and treatment or conversion.

These are opportunity positions to investigate, not evidence of demand, profitability, permission, or accessibility. Existing operators and infrastructure may be relevant capabilities, but their availability and commercial terms must be verified.

## 5. Safeguards and evidence boundaries

- Do not assume a material is safe because it is described as waste, natural, recyclable, or reusable.
- Do not recommend handling, processing, or conversion of hazardous, contaminated, electronic, battery, medical, chemical, or mixed waste without appropriate expertise, controls, permits, and lawful routes.
- Do not infer environmental benefit from a recycling or conversion label alone; assess actual lifecycle effects, residuals, emissions, energy, water, transport, and displacement of virgin inputs.
- Distinguish vendor claims, laboratory tests, pilot results, operating performance, customer contracts, and audited economics.
- Verify the material's legal status, ownership, access rights, transport rules, product standards, and liability before proposing a transaction.
- Keep hypotheses and leads separate from tested findings and decisions.

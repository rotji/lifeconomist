## Recommended next stage when the user chooses to resume

Build a **broad, cross-sector catalogue of real-world non-traditional business-model examples**. This was the proposed next stage, but it is not authorized to begin automatically. Wait for the user's explicit instruction.

For each real-world example, record:
1. Business/model name and source links.
2. Sector, geography, and relevant market context.
3. The actual customer problem or economic friction.
4. Participants and the roles each performs.
5. What the focal business owns, controls, accesses, coordinates, or supplies.
6. What it deliberately does not own or perform.
7. How the operating arrangement works in practice.
8. Who pays, what triggers payment, and the revenue mechanism.
9. What value is created for each relevant participant.
10. Required assets, capital, skills, relationships, permissions, contracts, and regulatory status.
11. Main operating costs, risks, dependencies, and failure modes.
12. Evidence quality: company claims, independent reporting, filings, customer evidence, observed transactions, or other sources.
13. What is known, inferred, disputed, and unknown.
14. Whether the pattern appears transferable elsewhere, and what local validation would be required.

## Research discipline for the next stage

- Prefer actual operating businesses and documented arrangements over motivational stories, generic lists, or hypothetical examples.
- Use primary sources where available: company disclosures, pricing and terms, regulatory records, filings, contracts or service descriptions. Use independent reporting and credible research to cross-check company claims.
- Distinguish a company's marketing description from verified operating facts and demonstrated commercial performance.
- Do not treat funding, publicity, user counts, gross transaction value, or a large addressable market as proof of profitability.
- Explain the business mechanism in plain language, including the money flow and responsibilities.
- Look for examples across different sectors and geographies; do not let the first few familiar examples define the catalogue.
- Preserve examples that are capital-intensive as part of the full landscape. Assess low-resource accessibility separately rather than filtering the whole catalogue prematurely.
- Do not assume every intermediary, marketplace, aggregator, or platform creates defensible value. Examine bypass risk, customer benefit, alternatives, and the reason participants continue paying.
- Do not start a sector deep dive, choose a business for the user, or narrow to a shortlist until the catalogue gives a defensible basis and the user chooses the next move.

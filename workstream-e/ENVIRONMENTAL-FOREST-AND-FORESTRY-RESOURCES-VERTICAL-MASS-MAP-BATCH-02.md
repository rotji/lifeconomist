## 1. Purpose and boundary

This batch maps **3.1.2 Forest and Forestry Resources** as a resource family. It covers forest ecosystems as managed and used landscapes, forest products, forestry operations, management, monitoring, protection, restoration, and related value chains.

The map distinguishes:
- **Forest and forestry resources (3.1.2):** forest resource assessment, forest management, timber and non-timber forest products, forestry operations, forest protection, and forest-sector value chains.
- **Biodiversity and ecological integrity (3.8):** species, habitats, ecological processes, conservation outcomes, and biodiversity assessment. Cross-reference where forestry affects these.
- **Agriculture and biological production (3.9):** agricultural crops, livestock, and managed production systems that are primarily agricultural.
- **Waste and resource recovery (3.2):** residues, sawdust, wood waste, reuse, recycling, and recovery after a material becomes a waste/by-product stream.
- **Energy (3.4):** biomass energy conversion and energy markets; forest biomass feedstock may originate here but energy conversion belongs primarily to 3.4.
- **Climate and carbon (3.5):** greenhouse-gas accounting, carbon removal, and carbon markets; forest carbon is cross-referenced, not treated as the whole forest economy.
- **Environmental intelligence (3.12):** remote sensing, data platforms, measurement, monitoring, and verification methods across forestry.
- **Environmental economics and governance (3.15):** tenure, concessions, incentives, finance, trade rules, benefit-sharing, and institutional governance.

A forest is not merely a stock of harvestable timber. It is also an ecosystem, a source of livelihoods and products, a landscape that influences water and climate, and a place where rights and public interests may overlap. Sustainable use requires attention to ecological limits, tenure, community rights, regeneration, and long-term management.

## 2. Branch 3.1.2.1 — Forest Types, Extent, and Resource Inventories

### Scope and components
- Natural forests, secondary/regrowth forests, planted forests, community forests, production forests, protected forests, and degraded forest landscapes.
- Forest classification, forest definition, canopy/cover thresholds, and national inventory conventions.
- Forest area, type, age/structure, stocking, species composition, biomass, growing stock, and regeneration.
- National forest inventories, sample plots, permanent sample plots, field measurements, aerial surveys, and remote-sensing estimates.
- Forest boundaries, management units, compartments, blocks, and inventory cycles.
- Data quality, sampling design, uncertainty, temporal comparability, and inventory updates.

### System mechanism
**Define forest classes and inventory design → collect field and remote observations → estimate extent, structure and resources → assess trends and uncertainty → inform management, conservation, planning and investment.**

An inventory is an estimate built from definitions, samples, measurements, and models. Different definitions or survey years can produce different forest-area figures. Remote sensing can map canopy and change, but field measurements are often needed to estimate species, timber volume, condition, and other attributes.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Forest inventory services; field plot data collection under supervision; geospatial data processing; inventory database management; forest resource reporting; independent quality checks; data integration for public agencies, concession holders, community managers, researchers, and project operators.

### Constraints and questions
Access and permissions; difficult terrain; seasonal access; sampling bias; outdated inventories; inconsistent definitions; measurement competence; safety; community protocols. Which forest inventories exist for Nigeria and each state? What years, methods, and definitions were used? Who uses them and where are the data gaps?

## 3. Branch 3.1.2.2 — Natural Forest Management and Silviculture

### Scope and components
- Management objectives and forest management plans.
- Natural regeneration, assisted natural regeneration, enrichment planting, and regeneration protection.
- Silvicultural systems, stand tending, thinning, pruning, and selective harvesting.
- Species/site matching and management of mixed stands.
- Forest structure, age classes, growing stock, and harvest cycles.
- Sustainable yield, allowable cut, rotation, and management prescriptions.
- Monitoring of regeneration, mortality, growth, and stand condition.

### System mechanism
**Assess forest condition and objectives → choose a silvicultural system → manage regeneration and stand development → monitor growth and ecological condition → adjust harvest and management over time.**

Silviculture is the practice of managing forest establishment, composition, growth, and renewal. It is not simply tree planting. The correct intervention depends on forest type, site conditions, ecological objectives, local use, and management capacity.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Management-plan preparation and implementation by qualified teams; nursery and seed supply; supervised field operations; regeneration monitoring; work scheduling and records; contractor coordination; inventory and growth-data support.

### Constraints and questions
Unsuitable species, fire, grazing, pests, illegal cutting, tenure conflict, insufficient maintenance, and short funding horizons can undermine regeneration. Which management systems are permitted and practised locally? Who holds management rights? Are there funded management contracts and measurable regeneration obligations?

## 4. Branch 3.1.2.3 — Timber Resources and Wood Supply

### Scope and components
- Standing timber and merchantable growing stock.
- Commercial and locally used tree species.
- Tree selection, felling, bucking, extraction, and log grading.
- Sawmilling, veneer, plywood, poles, boards, joinery, and other primary wood products.
- Timber measurement, species identification, grading, moisture, quality, and traceability.
- Domestic supply, imports, exports, transport, storage, and market channels.
- Legal harvest documentation and chain of custody.

### System mechanism
**Forest resource and legal harvest rights → tree selection and felling → log extraction and transport → primary processing → product grading and distribution → end use and market feedback.**

Timber supply depends on the resource, lawful access, harvest rules, skilled operators, equipment, transport, processing capacity, and buyer specifications. A forest with standing trees does not automatically provide an accessible or sustainable commercial supply.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Timber inventory and market intelligence; supplier/buyer directories; logistics and order coordination; quality documentation; traceability support; sawmill operations and maintenance; timber processing and distribution; professional forestry and harvesting services.

### Constraints and questions
Illegal logging, unsustainable harvest, species restrictions, poor measurement, informal markets, transport cost, low processing efficiency, and weak traceability. Which species and product categories have verified legal supply? What are actual delivered prices and quality requirements? Which authorities issue and enforce harvest and transport permissions?

## 5. Branch 3.1.2.4 — Plantation Forestry and Planted Forests

### Scope and components
- Commercial timber plantations and industrial woodlots.
- Smallholder and community woodlots.
- Protective, restoration, shelterbelt, and multipurpose plantations.
- Site selection, land preparation, species selection, nursery stock, planting density, and establishment.
- Weeding, tending, thinning, pruning, fire protection, and harvest planning.
- Rotation length, yield modelling, survival, growth, and financial returns.
- Plantation certification, safeguards, and post-harvest replanting.

### System mechanism
**Secure land and rights → assess site and market → establish planting material and plantation → maintain and protect → monitor survival and growth → harvest and regenerate or replant.**

Plantations are long-cycle assets. Returns may be delayed for years, and depend on land tenure, species, survival, maintenance, fire risk, market access, processing capacity, and the timing of harvest. Planted forests are not automatically equivalent to natural forests in biodiversity or ecosystem function.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Nursery operations; seedling supply; planting and maintenance contracts; survival audits; plantation inventory; firebreak and access maintenance; growth monitoring; timber marketing and offtake coordination; plantation finance and management.

### Constraints and questions
Land competition, insecure tenure, unsuitable species, water demand, pests, fire, market volatility, and long payback periods. What planted-forest programmes and private plantations operate in Nigeria? What species, rotation, buyer contracts, and land arrangements are actually used?

## 6. Branch 3.1.2.5 — Non-Timber Forest Products (NTFPs)

### Scope and components
- Edible fruits, nuts, seeds, leaves, mushrooms, spices, and other foods.
- Medicinal and aromatic plants, gums, resins, latex, fibres, dyes, and natural extracts.
- Rattan, bamboo where forest-linked, thatching materials, craft materials, and other plant products.
- Wild honey and other forest-associated products where collection is lawful and sustainable.
- Harvest protocols, seasonal availability, regeneration, quality, processing, storage, and market access.
- Traditional knowledge, intellectual property, access and benefit-sharing, and community rights.

### System mechanism
**Resource and rights identification → sustainable collection or cultivation → sorting and primary processing → quality control and storage → aggregation and market access → resource monitoring and benefit-sharing.**

NTFPs can support livelihoods and diversify forest value, but they are not automatically abundant, renewable under any harvest intensity, or commercially standardised. Some products are seasonal, perishable, difficult to identify, or subject to safety and quality requirements.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Product and supplier mapping; seasonal availability intelligence; aggregation and logistics coordination; quality documentation; packaging and processing by competent operators; traceability; market access support; community enterprise services.

### Constraints and questions
Overharvesting, species misidentification, contamination, food/drug regulation, unstable supply, traditional knowledge rights, and unequal benefit distribution. Which products have reliable local supply and identifiable buyers? What permits and quality standards apply? Who owns the knowledge and resource rights?

## 7. Branch 3.1.2.6 — Forest Nursery, Seed, and Genetic Material Supply

### Scope and components
- Seed sourcing, collection, identification, storage, testing, and certification.
- Provenance, seed zones, genetic diversity, and seed transfer considerations.
- Tree nurseries, propagation, seedlings, cuttings, tissue culture where relevant, and hardening.
- Seedling quality, health, survival, and dispatch.
- Native species, commercial species, restoration species, and agroforestry planting stock.
- Nursery planning, production forecasting, inventory, and buyer coordination.

### System mechanism
**Identify planting objective and species → source appropriate seed/propagules → propagate and raise stock → quality-check and harden → distribute and plant → monitor survival and source performance.**

Planting material quality and provenance influence survival and long-term outcomes. Cheap seedlings may be unsuitable for the site or objective. Nursery supply must be connected to actual planting plans, timing, species requirements, and aftercare.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Seed and nursery directories; demand forecasting; seedling inventory systems; nursery production; quality documentation; delivery coordination; seed collection under permission; technical nursery services.

### Constraints and questions
Seed adulteration, poor provenance, low germination, pests, seasonal demand, unsold stock, and inadequate aftercare. Which species are in demand? Who sets seed/seedling standards? Are public procurement and restoration programmes transparent and accessible to small suppliers?

## 8. Branch 3.1.2.7 — Forest Harvesting, Extraction, and Operations

### Scope and components
- Harvest planning and operational prescriptions.
- Felling, bucking, skidding, forwarding, loading, and log transport.
- Low-impact harvesting and reduced-impact logging practices.
- Road, landing, and access planning.
- Equipment, fuel, maintenance, operator training, and safety.
- Work measurement, productivity, damage control, and operational records.
- Contractor management and post-harvest site assessment.

### System mechanism
**Authorised harvest plan → operational layout and safety preparation → harvesting and extraction → transport and delivery → post-harvest assessment and regeneration protection.**

Harvesting is a high-risk operational activity. Poorly planned access roads, felling, and extraction can damage soils, watercourses, residual trees, habitats, and worker safety. A lower-impact method requires trained operators, suitable equipment, supervision, and monitoring.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Operational scheduling and documentation; contractor and equipment coordination; maintenance support; log tracking; safety records; supervised inventory and field support; specialist harvesting services through qualified operators.

### Constraints and questions
Worker injury, illegal harvest, equipment capital, fuel, road access, environmental damage, and payment risk. What operators are licensed or authorised? Which tasks can be delegated? What safety, insurance, and environmental requirements apply?

## 9. Branch 3.1.2.8 — Forest Roads, Access, and Infrastructure

### Scope and components
- Forest access roads, tracks, bridges, culverts, and crossings.
- Road alignment, drainage, slope, erosion, and sediment control.
- Log landings, storage yards, depots, and loading points.
- Fire access, patrol routes, and emergency access.
- Maintenance, seasonal passability, and closure/decommissioning.
- Connectivity to mills, markets, communities, and public transport networks.

### System mechanism
**Forest management and operational requirements → route and site planning → construction or improvement → use and maintenance → erosion, safety, and access monitoring → rehabilitation or closure.**

Access infrastructure enables management and economic activity but can also fragment habitats, increase erosion, facilitate illegal entry, and change land-use pressure. Roads should be planned against both operational benefits and environmental/social impacts.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Road condition inventories; route and logistics analysis; maintenance scheduling; contractor coordination; site monitoring; drainage inspection documentation; engineering and construction by qualified providers.

### Constraints and questions
Capital cost, seasonal failure, erosion, bridge safety, protected-area restrictions, land access, and unintended access impacts. Who owns and maintains forest access infrastructure? Which operators pay for road-condition information or maintenance support?

## 10. Branch 3.1.2.9 — Forest Protection, Fire, Pests, and Illegal Activities

### Scope and components
- Forest fire prevention, detection, suppression, and post-fire assessment.
- Pest, disease, invasive species, and forest-health surveillance.
- Illegal logging, encroachment, unauthorised clearing, and resource theft.
- Patrol planning, incident reporting, and enforcement coordination.
- Firebreaks, fuel management, early warning, and community preparedness.
- Forest health indicators, disturbance severity, and recovery monitoring.

### System mechanism
**Threat or pressure → observation and detection → verification and response → containment or enforcement → recovery and monitoring.**

Remote sensing and reports can identify possible fire, clearing, or disturbance. They do not independently establish who caused it, whether an activity was authorised, or what enforcement action is justified. Reliable response needs local verification, institutional authority, and safe procedures.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Incident data management; fire and disturbance alert triage; patrol and maintenance scheduling; field reporting support; forest-health monitoring; equipment and service coordination; post-event mapping.

### Constraints and questions
False alarms, remote access, response capacity, security, community trust, unclear jurisdiction, and dangerous fire or enforcement situations. Which agencies and community structures respond? What monitoring and reporting systems exist? Who funds prevention and response?

## 11. Branch 3.1.2.10 — Community Forestry and Participatory Forest Management

### Scope and components
- Community-managed forests and locally governed resources.
- Community forestry institutions, cooperatives, and user groups.
- Participatory mapping, management plans, local rules, and monitoring.
- Customary tenure, access, use rights, and conflict resolution.
- Benefit-sharing, livelihood needs, inclusion, and representation.
- Community-based enterprises and local processing.
- Consent, grievance mechanisms, and accountability.

### System mechanism
**Recognise rights and stakeholders → agree objectives and rules → establish representative governance → manage and use resources → distribute benefits and responsibilities → monitor outcomes and resolve disputes.**

Community forestry is not simply asking residents to participate in an externally designed project. Rights, authority, representation, consent, benefit distribution, and local knowledge determine whether management is legitimate and durable.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Participatory data collection; community enterprise support; transparent records and reporting; market and buyer discovery; logistics coordination; training and administrative support; independent monitoring where communities agree.

### Constraints and questions
Elite capture, unequal representation, unclear rights, conflict, inadequate benefit-sharing, and short project funding. Which communities hold recognised or customary rights? Who speaks for different user groups? What governance and consent processes are legitimate?

## 12. Branch 3.1.2.11 — Forest Restoration, Rehabilitation, and Reforestation

### Scope and components
- Natural regeneration and assisted natural regeneration.
- Enrichment planting and restoration of degraded forest.
- Reforestation and afforestation, with ecological suitability assessed.
- Restoration of riparian forest, mangroves, and other forest-linked systems where appropriate.
- Fire, grazing, invasive species, and degradation-pressure management.
- Long-term maintenance, survival, biodiversity, and ecosystem-function monitoring.
- Restoration standards, baselines, permanence, and verification.

### System mechanism
**Diagnose degradation and rights → set ecosystem-specific objectives → select intervention → implement and maintain → monitor ecological and social outcomes → adapt and verify over time.**

Restoration is not measured only by the number of seedlings planted. Survival, native composition, ecosystem function, land rights, local benefits, and persistence matter. Planting trees in naturally non-forest ecosystems can cause harm; the historical ecosystem and site context must guide interventions.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Restoration baseline mapping; nursery and seed supply; field logistics; maintenance contracts; survival monitoring; community coordination; reporting and verification support; specialist ecological restoration.

### Constraints and questions
Land tenure, wrong species, fire, grazing, drought, weak maintenance, short grants, and inflated impact claims. Which restoration programmes have multi-year budgets? What outcomes are contracted? Who verifies them and how are communities compensated?

## 13. Branch 3.1.2.12 — Forest Carbon, Climate Services, and Ecosystem Payments

### Scope and components
- Forest biomass and carbon stock estimation.
- Avoided deforestation, improved forest management, afforestation/reforestation, and other land-sector climate activities.
- Measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV).
- Baselines, additionality, leakage, permanence, uncertainty, and reversal risk.
- Carbon standards, registries, project validation and verification.
- Benefit-sharing, land/community rights, safeguards, and claims.
- Payments for ecosystem services and other conservation finance.

### System mechanism
**Define rights and project boundary → establish baseline and methodology → implement eligible activity → measure and report outcomes → independent validation/verification and registry processes → issuance/payment where requirements are met → long-term monitoring and benefit distribution.**

Forest carbon is a specialised climate-finance mechanism, not a guaranteed income stream from owning or planting trees. Projects require recognised rights, defensible baselines, accepted methods, monitoring, validation/verification, safeguards, market access, and long-term obligations. Carbon accounting belongs primarily to 3.5, with forest resource and rights dependencies here.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Project data support; forest inventory and monitoring services; community engagement and benefit-sharing administration; MRV data processing under competent supervision; project development and finance; independent validation/verification by authorised bodies.

### Constraints and questions
Methodology changes, credit-price volatility, transaction costs, permanence obligations, land/carbon rights, community consent, double counting, and reputational risk. Which projects are registered and operational in Nigeria? What standards, buyers, prices, costs, and benefit-sharing terms are evidenced?

## 14. Branch 3.1.2.13 — Forest Products Processing and Value Addition

### Scope and components
- Primary timber conversion: sawmilling, peeling, chipping, and basic sizing.
- Secondary processing: furniture, joinery, flooring, panels, doors, packaging, and engineered wood.
- NTFP cleaning, drying, extraction, grading, packaging, and formulation where lawful.
- Wood drying, preservation, treatment, and quality testing.
- By-product handling, residues, sawdust, bark, and offcuts.
- Product standards, certification, traceability, design, and market channels.

### System mechanism
**Lawful and traceable forest feedstock → primary conversion → secondary processing/value addition → product quality and standards → distribution and end markets → residue recovery and resource-efficiency feedback.**

Value addition can increase revenue per unit of raw material, but it depends on consistent legal feedstock, machinery, energy, skills, quality control, working capital, market demand, and waste management. Processing residues become part of the waste/resource-recovery system when treated as by-products or waste.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Processor and buyer directories; market intelligence; production and inventory records; quality documentation; equipment maintenance; logistics coordination; small-scale processing; product design and distribution.

### Constraints and questions
Unreliable feedstock, power costs, low recovery rates, equipment downtime, product defects, finance, and competition from substitute materials. What products have verified demand? Which processors have capacity gaps? What is the delivered cost and quality of legal raw material?

## 15. Branch 3.1.2.14 — Forest Product Traceability, Certification, and Responsible Sourcing

### Scope and components
- Legal origin and harvest documentation.
- Chain-of-custody systems from forest to processor and buyer.
- Species identification and product verification.
- Forest management and product certification schemes.
- Due diligence, supplier audits, risk screening, and transaction records.
- Digital traceability, batch identity, geolocation, and document retention.
- Responsible procurement, buyer requirements, and market access.

### System mechanism
**Identify source and rights → document harvest and custody events → maintain product/batch identity through processing and transport → verify claims and supplier controls → buyer due diligence and market acceptance.**

Traceability is a chain of evidence, not merely a QR code or database. Each transfer and transformation must be recorded, and claims must be supported by credible documents and checks. Certification schemes have specific scopes and rules; a certificate for one operation does not automatically certify every product or supplier.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Document and data workflow support; supplier mapping; traceability system implementation; chain-of-custody record management; audit preparation; buyer requirement research; independent certification by competent bodies.

### Constraints and questions
Informal supply chains, document fraud, data gaps, cost of certification, weak enforcement, and buyer-specific requirements. Which buyers require traceability? What proof do they accept? Who pays for compliance and how are small producers included?

## 16. Branch 3.1.2.15 — Forest Policy, Tenure, Concessions, and Sector Governance

### Scope and components
- Forest laws, policies, strategies, and institutional mandates.
- State and federal roles, forest reserves, protected areas, and public forest estates.
- Community and customary rights, concessions, licences, permits, and harvest allocations.
- Forest revenue, fees, royalties, taxes, and benefit-sharing.
- Enforcement, reporting, transparency, and dispute resolution.
- Public procurement, forest programmes, grants, and investment.
- International trade, biodiversity, climate, and sustainable forest commitments.

### System mechanism
**Law and policy allocate authority and rights → institutions issue plans, permits, and controls → operators and communities act within the rules → monitoring and enforcement assess compliance → policy and allocation may change.**

Forest governance in Nigeria is institutionally and jurisdictionally complex. Do not assume a single national permit or uniform state-level process. The applicable authority, land status, rights, species, activity, and location must be checked before any operational or commercial claim.

### Economic activity and possible roles
Policy and procurement intelligence; public programme monitoring; compliance document support; institutional data systems; transparent reporting; stakeholder and provider directories; legal and professional services through qualified practitioners.

### Constraints and questions
Overlapping mandates, weak records, enforcement gaps, corruption risks, policy uncertainty, and contested rights. Which authority controls the specific forest and activity? What current rules and fees apply in the relevant state? Are concessions and procurement processes transparent and accessible?

## 17. Cross-branch forest value chains

### A. Timber value chain
**Forest inventory and management → lawful harvest → extraction and transport → primary processing → secondary manufacturing → buyer/end use → residue reuse or recovery.**

In plain language, timber becomes a commercial product only after the resource is measured, rights and permissions are established, trees are harvested responsibly, logs reach processors, and products meet buyer specifications. At every stage, traceability, quality, logistics, and environmental controls affect cost and legitimacy.

### B. Non-timber product value chain
**Resource/rights identification → sustainable collection or cultivation → sorting and primary processing → quality and safety checks → aggregation and storage → market access → resource regeneration and benefit-sharing.**

In plain language, a forest product can have local value but still fail to reach a reliable market if supply is seasonal, quality varies, storage is poor, or the collector lacks access to buyers. Sustainable harvest and community rights must remain part of the economics.

### C. Restoration and ecosystem-finance chain
**Rights and baseline → ecological diagnosis → intervention and maintenance → monitoring and verification → funding/payment mechanism → long-term stewardship and benefit distribution.**

In plain language, restoration finance depends on more than planting activity. The project must have clear rights, a suitable ecological plan, measurable outcomes, long-term maintenance, credible verification, and a fair arrangement for those who manage and depend on the land.

## 18. Opportunity and capability positions

Potential roles across the forest economy include:
- Forest resource and supplier information services.
- Inventory data collection and GIS processing under appropriate supervision.
- Nursery and seed supply, inventory, and distribution.
- Timber and NTFP aggregation, market discovery, and logistics coordination.
- Processor directories, quality records, and production workflow support.
- Forest incident and change monitoring.
- Restoration monitoring, field logistics, and community coordination.
- Traceability and compliance documentation.
- Specialist forestry, ecological, engineering, and certification services.
- Plantation, processing, infrastructure, and restoration project development.

These are system positions, not a shortlist or proof of demand. The full landscape includes capital-intensive forestry, processing, plantation, infrastructure, and restoration projects. More accessible entry hypotheses may involve information, documentation, data processing, coordination, or supervised field support—but only where a real user, lawful access, competence, and payment route are demonstrated.

## 19. Risks and safeguards

1. **Forest loss and unsustainable harvest:** do not encourage extraction without resource, rights, and regeneration evidence.
2. **Land and community rights:** identify rights-holders and customary users; obtain legitimate consent and agree benefit-sharing.
3. **Ecological mismatch:** do not treat plantations as equivalent to natural forest or plant trees in naturally non-forest ecosystems.
4. **Illegal supply:** do not facilitate trade in illegally harvested timber or restricted species.
5. **Fire and worker safety:** avoid unsupervised field activity and hazardous operations.
6. **Pesticides and chemicals:** do not recommend or handle hazardous chemicals outside lawful professional controls.
7. **Carbon and biodiversity claims:** no credit, offset, restoration, or biodiversity-gain claims without accepted methodology and independent verification.
8. **Data and privacy:** protect sensitive community, tenure, and location information.
9. **Certification claims:** verify scope, validity, chain-of-custody coverage, and the certifying body.
10. **Financial exposure:** long rotations, uncertain harvest rights, market cycles, fire, and maintenance can create substantial capital and timing risks.

## 20. Nigeria-specific evidence and research agenda

This batch is a taxonomy map. It does not assert current forest area, deforestation rates, timber output, NTFP market size, plantation returns, carbon prices, or the number of active forestry enterprises. Those claims require current, source-specific investigation.

Priority source families for subsequent evidence work:
- Federal Ministry of Environment and the relevant forestry/landscape departments.
- National Council on Climate Change for forest-carbon and climate-policy interfaces.
- National Bureau of Statistics for available forestry, agriculture, trade, and industrial data.
- State forestry departments and forest-reserve authorities; the relevant state is essential.
- National Park Service and protected-area authorities where applicable.
- FAO Global Forest Resources Assessment, FAOSTAT forestry products, and country forestry profiles.
- UN-REDD, UNFCCC, and recognised carbon registries for project-specific climate claims.
- Research institutions, universities, forest communities, producer groups, timber processors, and buyers.
- Customs/trade data and relevant standards/certification bodies for product and market requirements.

### Questions for a Nigeria-focused vertical evidence phase
1. What is the latest nationally reported forest inventory and how current are its measurements?
2. How do forest definitions and estimates differ across official sources?
3. Which forest types and resource products are present in each state or ecological zone?
4. Which authorities manage each forest category and issue relevant permissions?
5. What is the scale and structure of formal and informal timber and NTFP value chains?
6. Which products have traceable legal supply and identifiable buyers?
7. What forestry programmes, concessions, restoration projects, or procurement opportunities are active?
8. What is the real status of forest monitoring, fire detection, and enforcement systems?
9. Which communities hold customary use or management rights, and how are benefits distributed?
10. Which service gaps are reported by actual operators, communities, processors, agencies, or buyers?

## 22. Later vertical investigation questions

When this family is selected for deeper research, investigate:
- One defined forest type, geography, and management regime at a time.
- The current legal authority, rights-holders, permissions, and applicable state rules.
- Actual inventory data, field condition, resource availability, and ecological constraints.
- A specific product or service value chain, including all intermediaries and delivered costs.
- Existing operators, public programmes, buyers, providers, and procurement channels.
- Customer pain, budget ownership, payment timing, and alternatives.
- Technical, safety, environmental, and professional requirements.
- A small reversible test using permitted data or supervised activity.

Do not start with a generic claim that Nigeria needs reforestation, timber processing, or forest monitoring. Identify a particular user, location, problem, and decision first.

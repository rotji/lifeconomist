# Environmental Opportunity Intelligence — Remaining Territories Vertical Mass Documentation

**Status:** Consolidated first-pass vertical mass map, 2026-11-06.  
**Purpose:** Accelerate documentation across the remaining environmental domain so the user can move to the next Lifeconomist phase.  
**Coverage:** Remaining resource families in 3.1 (after land and forestry), plus vertical branch maps for 3.2–3.15.  
**Evidence status:** Taxonomy and system mapping only. This document is not a scientific inventory, Nigeria market study, legal review, technical feasibility study, or validation of any business opportunity.

## 1. How to use this document

This is a portfolio-level vertical map. It deliberately groups many branches into one document rather than requiring one GitHub file and one user confirmation per small branch. It provides:
- major sub-branches and their components;
- the operating mechanism and value-chain stages;
- possible economic activities and participation positions;
- principal dependencies, constraints, and safeguards;
- cross-references to the primary territory for overlapping topics;
- a clear distinction between mapped concepts, hypotheses, and validated opportunities.

The map is intentionally broad. It does not attempt to list every species, material, machine, regulation, product, or local market. A later investigation should descend only where evidence, an identified user, a concrete dependency, or the user's choice justifies it.

### Status language
- **Mapped:** a branch and its system components have been identified.
- **Lead/hypothesis:** a possible problem, service, or economic position worth testing.
- **Evidence required:** local conditions, technical performance, legal permissions, customer demand, or economics remain unknown.
- **Validated:** reserved for a finding supported by appropriate independent evidence. No opportunity in this document is marked validated.

## 2. Remaining natural resource families — 3.1

The earlier batches cover **3.1.1 Land and Terrestrial Resources** and **3.1.2 Forest and Forestry Resources**. The following resource families complete a first-pass resource inventory map. Resource occurrence, stock, quality, location, access, and rights belong here; downstream service delivery and industrial transformation belong in their relevant territories.

### 3.1.3 Freshwater resources

**Branches and components**
1. Rivers, streams, channels, and seasonal waterways: catchments, flow regimes, river morphology, sediment, seasonal variation.
2. Lakes, natural ponds, and inland water bodies: extent, depth, storage, quality, ecological status, use.
3. Reservoirs and impoundments as resource stocks: storage, inflows, sedimentation, operating levels, competing uses.
4. Groundwater and aquifers: aquifer geometry, recharge, storage, water table, yield, connectivity, vulnerability.
5. Springs, seeps, and wetlands as freshwater-linked resources: source locations, hydrology, seasonality, ecological function.
6. Rainfall and atmospheric freshwater inputs: precipitation patterns, intensity, seasonality, catchment contribution.
7. Freshwater quality and resource condition: physical, chemical, and biological characteristics; natural and anthropogenic pressures.
8. Freshwater resource inventories and allocation: basin boundaries, withdrawals, users, rights, transboundary flows, resource accounting.

**Mechanism:** precipitation and catchment processes → surface runoff/infiltration → river, lake, wetland and aquifer storage/flows → abstraction and ecological use → replenishment, discharge and quality change. This means freshwater availability is not just the visible volume in a river or reservoir; it depends on timing, recharge, quality, upstream use, and ecological requirements.

**Economic activity/roles:** hydrological and groundwater surveys; resource mapping; field measurements; water-resource data services; catchment intelligence; resource accounting; monitoring support; lawful exploration and assessment by qualified operators.

**Dependencies and cautions:** hydrological seasons, data gaps, shared basins, abstraction rights, contamination, drought, ecological flows, and the distinction between resource assessment (3.1.3) and water supply/treatment/distribution services (3.3). No local yield or availability should be inferred without site-specific evidence.

### 3.1.4 Marine and coastal resources

**Branches and components**
1. Coastal waters and nearshore zones: bathymetry, water quality, currents, salinity, seasonal dynamics.
2. Estuaries, lagoons, deltas, and tidal systems: mixing, sediment, freshwater influence, habitats, navigation.
3. Marine water and offshore resource conditions: depth, currents, temperature, salinity, ecological zones.
4. Coastal landforms and sediment systems: beaches, dunes, mudflats, shorelines, erosion and accretion.
5. Marine biological resources: wild fish, shellfish, seaweeds, and other biological stocks (cross-reference 3.8 and 3.9).
6. Seabed and geological resources: sediments, aggregates, and mineral occurrence (cross-reference 3.7).
7. Marine renewable resource conditions: wind, wave, tidal, and current regimes (energy conversion under 3.4).
8. Coastal access, use, and rights: fishing grounds, ports, conservation areas, customary use, and competing uses.

**Mechanism:** ocean-atmosphere and river-coast processes → water movement, sediment transport and habitat conditions → resource distribution and coastal change → human use, infrastructure exposure and ecosystem response. Coastal resource value and risk are shaped by dynamic interactions, not fixed boundaries alone.

**Economic activity/roles:** coastal mapping; shoreline and habitat observation; marine data services; lawful resource surveys; fisheries information; sediment and erosion monitoring; navigation and site information; specialist environmental assessment.

**Dependencies and cautions:** maritime jurisdiction, protected areas, navigational safety, community rights, coastal erosion, storms, pollution, and expensive field operations. Marine energy belongs primarily to 3.4; fisheries production to 3.9; ecosystem integrity to 3.8; coastal hazard management to 3.14.

### 3.1.5 Wild fisheries and aquatic biological stocks

**Branches and components**
1. Inland capture fisheries: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, floodplains, species and seasonal stocks.
2. Coastal and marine capture fisheries: nearshore and offshore stocks, fishing grounds, fleet and gear.
3. Shellfish and other aquatic invertebrates: stock, habitat, collection and handling.
4. Fish stock assessment: catch, effort, size composition, recruitment, mortality and population trends.
5. Fishing grounds and resource access: customary access, licences, protected zones and conflict.
6. Landing, first sale and raw-resource handling: landing sites, species identification, freshness, ice and traceability.
7. Aquatic habitat condition: spawning grounds, nursery areas, water quality, flow, mangroves and wetlands.
8. Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing risks: monitoring, compliance and reporting.

**Mechanism:** aquatic habitat and recruitment → fish population dynamics → fishing effort and catch → landing and first sale → stock response and management feedback. A high catch in one period does not prove a healthy fish stock; catch must be interpreted with effort, season, size, and ecological conditions.

**Economic activity/roles:** catch and landing data systems; stock-monitoring support; traceability; market and landing intelligence; cold-chain coordination; lawful fisher services; data collection under approved protocols.

**Dependencies and cautions:** stock depletion, closed seasons, gear restrictions, food safety, licensing, community access, and uncertainty in catch data. Aquaculture is managed production under 3.9; processing and distribution are downstream food value chains.

### 3.1.6 Mineral and geological resources

**Branches and components**
1. Geological mapping and regional geology: formations, structures, lithology and geological history.
2. Mineral occurrences and deposits: metallic, industrial, construction and energy-related minerals.
3. Geochemical and geophysical evidence: sampling, assays, anomalies, surveys and interpretation.
4. Resource classification and estimation: grade, tonnage, confidence, geometry and uncertainty.
5. Dimension stone, aggregates, clays, sands and construction minerals.
6. Groundwater-linked geology and subsurface structure: cross-reference 3.1.3 and 3.6.
7. Geological hazards and constraints: faults, subsidence, instability and seismic considerations.
8. Mineral rights, licences, access, community interests and land-use constraints.

**Mechanism:** geological processes create and concentrate materials → exploration gathers evidence → resource models estimate location and quantity → rights and technical/economic conditions determine whether extraction is possible. A mineral occurrence is not automatically a reserve or commercially recoverable deposit.

**Economic activity/roles:** geological data compilation; GIS and geoscience data processing; exploration logistics; sampling support by qualified teams; mineral intelligence; supplier/buyer intelligence; compliance and documentation support.

**Dependencies and cautions:** mineral rights, permits, environmental assessment, assay quality, resource classification standards, community consent, rehabilitation liabilities, commodity prices and infrastructure. Extraction, beneficiation, refining, metallurgy and materials production belong primarily to 3.7.

### 3.1.7 Biomass and wild biological feedstocks

**Branches and components**
1. Naturally occurring plant biomass and wild harvestable materials.
2. Woody biomass and forest-derived feedstocks.
3. Grasses, reeds, aquatic plants and other non-forest biomass.
4. Algae and aquatic biomass stocks.
5. Biological residues at source before becoming a waste/by-product stream.
6. Seasonal biomass availability, moisture, composition and spatial distribution.
7. Sustainable yield, regeneration, harvest intensity and competing ecological uses.
8. Biomass access, customary rights, collection and transport constraints.

**Mechanism:** biological growth and ecosystem productivity → harvest or collection → feedstock preparation and logistics → conversion or direct use → residuals and ecosystem response. The same biomass may have food, feed, material, ecological, or energy uses, so the highest-value use cannot be assumed without comparing alternatives and ecological effects.

**Economic activity/roles:** feedstock mapping; seasonal supply intelligence; sustainable sourcing; aggregation; quality/moisture measurement; logistics; supplier coordination; biomass market data.

**Dependencies and cautions:** food security, biodiversity, land and water use, invasive species, seasonality, moisture, transport radius, land tenure, and sustainability. Energy conversion belongs to 3.4; biological manufacturing to 3.13; agricultural residues after generation to 3.2 or 3.9 depending on the question.

### 3.1.8 Other natural resources and resource flows

**Branches and components**
1. Geothermal resource conditions: temperature gradients, heat flow and subsurface conditions.
2. Wind resource: speed distributions, turbulence, seasonal and site variation.
3. Solar resource: irradiance, shading, seasonality and site conditions.
4. Wave, tidal and current resource regimes.
5. Natural heat, cold and other locally relevant physical resource gradients.
6. Natural salts and evaporite resources where present.
7. Resource co-location and competing-use analysis.
8. Resource data, access, rights, and long-term change.

**Mechanism:** natural physical processes create a spatially and temporally variable resource → measurement characterises its quality and availability → technology converts it into a service or product. Resource potential alone does not establish a viable project; conversion technology, grid or customer access, permits, finance, and operating conditions matter.

**Economic activity/roles:** resource assessment data; site screening; measurement campaigns; GIS and planning; project support; monitoring and forecasting. Energy conversion and project operation belong under 3.4.

**System/value chain:** generation/prevention → segregation → collection → aggregation → sorting/preprocessing → reuse/recycling/treatment/conversion → product quality and offtake → residual management. In plain language, the waste stream has to be reliably identified, separated, delivered, processed into a specification buyers accept, and the remaining fraction managed safely. A machine cannot fix inconsistent feedstock or missing buyers by itself.

**Economic positions:** waste audits; route and supplier coordination; material aggregation; buyer discovery; quality data; equipment rental/maintenance; collection contracts; processing; product distribution; traceability and compliance support.

**Constraints:** variable composition, contamination, collection density, transport cost, working capital, informal-sector livelihoods, hazardous fractions, permits, emissions, product standards and offtake. Do not assume all waste is a valuable feedstock or that recycling is always environmentally superior.

5. Extreme heat and health/productivity exposure.
6. Storms, coastal hazards and severe weather.
7. Industrial accidents, pollution incidents and hazardous releases.
8. Exposure mapping: people, assets, infrastructure and ecosystems.
9. Vulnerability and adaptive capacity assessment.
10. Early warning, communication and last-mile alert systems.
11. Preparedness, emergency logistics and continuity planning.
12. Response coordination, damage assessment and recovery.
13. Resilient infrastructure and adaptation measures.
14. Risk transfer, insurance data and financial protection.
15. Disaster loss data, post-event verification and learning.

**System/value chain:** hazard identification → exposure and vulnerability assessment → warning/preparedness → event and response → loss/damage assessment → recovery and adaptation. A hazard is not the same as risk: consequences depend on who or what is exposed, how vulnerable they are, and their capacity to respond.

**Economic positions:** risk mapping; asset exposure data; alert distribution; continuity planning; emergency supply coordination; damage assessment support; insurance data services; resilience project support.

**Constraints:** forecast uncertainty, alert fatigue, last-mile connectivity, public trust, emergency authority, safety, insurance data quality and unequal vulnerability. Do not issue unqualified warnings or imply that a model predicts a specific event with certainty.

## 4. Cross-territory value-chain patterns

The following recurring chains help reveal opportunities that a sector-by-sector list can miss:

1. **Resource-to-product:** resource occurrence → access and rights → extraction/collection → preparation → transformation → quality specification → buyer and distribution.
2. **Waste-to-input:** waste generation → separation → collection → grading → preprocessing → recovered input → industrial user → residual handling.
3. **Environmental problem-to-remedy:** detection → assessment → diagnosis → intervention → verification → monitoring and liability.
4. **Asset-to-service:** asset inventory → design/procurement → installation → operation → maintenance → performance monitoring → renewal.
5. **Measurement-to-decision:** user question → data specification → acquisition → quality control → analysis → decision product → action and feedback.
6. **Biological production-to-market:** inputs → cultivation/rearing → health and yield management → harvest → storage → processing → distribution → residual use.
7. **Risk-to-resilience:** hazard → exposure → vulnerability → warning/preparedness → response → recovery → adaptation.
8. **Policy-to-market:** legal framework → rights and standards → incentives/procurement → operator response → monitoring/enforcement → revised policy.

These chains are frameworks for investigation, not universal process templates. The plain-language point is that value can be captured between stages: coordinating supply, ensuring quality, maintaining equipment, verifying claims, or making data usable may matter as much as owning the primary asset.

## 5. Opportunity positions across the entire environmental economy

The following roles recur across multiple territories. They should be evaluated against a specific customer, job, permission, and payment route.

### Asset-heavy positions
- Resource ownership or concession operation.
- Farms, plantations, mines, quarries and industrial production.
- Collection fleets, processing plants, treatment facilities and laboratories.
- Energy, water, transport and environmental infrastructure.
- Remediation, restoration and large project contracting.
- Equipment manufacturing and industrial technology development.

### Asset-light or capability-leveraged positions
- Resource, supplier, buyer, facility and project intelligence.
- GIS, data processing, monitoring, reporting and workflow services.
- Customer discovery, demand aggregation and procurement support.
- Brokerage, sourcing and coordination where lawful and transparent.
- Maintenance scheduling and field-service coordination.
- Traceability, compliance documentation and audit preparation.
- Distribution, installation coordination, training and after-sales support.
- Sampling and laboratory coordination through qualified providers.
- Contract processing or shared-facility access rather than owning a plant.
- Project packaging, finance coordination and partner identification.
- Independent verification only where competence, methods and independence requirements are met.

An asset-light position is not automatically low-risk or profitable. It still needs a buyer, trust, competence, data access, liability boundaries, repeatable delivery and an economic reason for the customer to pay. Existing providers are capabilities to investigate, not assumed partners.

## 6. Cross-cutting safeguards

1. Establish lawful rights and permissions before accessing land, water, forests, minerals, biological resources, facilities or restricted data.
2. Respect customary tenure, community consent, local livelihoods and benefit-sharing.
3. Do not infer environmental improvement from an activity count, equipment purchase or marketing claim.
4. Account for pollution transfers, residuals, energy and water use, toxicity and lifecycle impacts.
5. Use qualified professionals for hazardous sampling, engineering, chemical handling, remediation, wildlife work and regulated services.
6. Protect sensitive species, community, infrastructure and personal data.
7. Verify standards, permits, licences and responsible authorities for the exact jurisdiction and activity.
8. Distinguish forecast, estimate, observation, measurement, verified result and legal determination.
9. Avoid unsupported carbon, biodiversity, offset, recycling, restoration and sustainability claims.
10. Consider who bears environmental costs and who receives economic benefits.

## 7. Nigeria and global research agenda

This consolidated map does not establish current Nigerian resource quantities, environmental conditions, production volumes, market sizes, prices, customer demand, regulatory status or project pipelines. Each must be established from dated, source-specific evidence.

For any selected branch, identify:
- the responsible federal, state, local, basin, sectoral or community authority;
- the relevant law, regulation, permit, standard and professional requirement;
- actual resource, facility, operator and infrastructure evidence;
- local users, customers, suppliers, competitors and substitute services;
- the physical and digital data available, its date, method and uncertainty;
- actual input/output specifications, delivered costs, logistics and operating constraints;
- environmental and social safeguards and who bears the risks;
- the decision-maker, budget holder, procurement route and payment timing.

Global technologies, suppliers and markets may be relevant, but transferability to Nigeria must be tested against local feedstock, infrastructure, skills, finance, regulation, logistics and customer economics. Do not infer a Nigerian opportunity from a foreign demonstration or vendor case study.

## 9. What this mass documentation accomplishes — and what it does not

This document completes a broad first-pass vertical decomposition of the remaining environmental territories at the branch-map level. It reduces the need to continue one small branch at a time and gives Lifeconomist a navigable landscape of the environmental economy.

It does **not** mean:
- every branch has been deeply researched;
- all technologies or economic activities have been listed;
- Nigeria's resource base or environmental conditions have been measured;
- markets, customer demand, suppliers, margins or investment returns have been validated;
- all laws, permits, standards or rights have been verified;
- an opportunity has been selected or recommended.

The next phase should not automatically continue down the taxonomy. It should use the completed map, existing candidate register and portfolio comparison to decide what matters for the user's final direction. A bounded, evidence-backed opportunity may justify deeper research; otherwise the project can move to another domain or phase.

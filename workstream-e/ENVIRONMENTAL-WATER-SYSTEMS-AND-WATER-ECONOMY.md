# Water Systems and the Water Economy

**Parent map:** [Environmental Opportunity Intelligence](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-INTELLIGENCE.md)  
**Territory:** 3.3 — Water systems and the water economy  
**Status:** Initial horizontal map completed on 2026-11-06. This is a broad navigation map, not an exhaustive technical taxonomy, validated market assessment, or selection of approved opportunities.

## 1. Purpose and boundary

Map the natural, engineered, commercial, and institutional systems through which water is sourced, stored, treated, moved, used, measured, recovered, discharged, and governed.

Water is not one uniform commodity. Surface water, groundwater, rainwater, wastewater, seawater, and industrial process water differ in quality, location, reliability, legal status, treatment requirements, and possible uses. Water quantity and water quality are inseparable: water that exists physically may not be accessible, safe, affordable, or suitable for a particular use.

This territory includes the water cycle as it intersects with human activity, water infrastructure and services, water-dependent industries, water quality and monitoring, and the economic relationships around supply, use, reuse, and discharge. It overlaps with agriculture, energy, land, infrastructure, environmental intelligence, and remediation.

## 2. Initial broad subcategory map

### 3.3.1 Surface-water systems
Rivers, streams, lakes, reservoirs, wetlands, catchments, and other inland surface-water bodies. Relevant dimensions include flow, seasonality, storage, quality, competing uses, ecological requirements, and upstream-downstream relationships.

### 3.3.2 Groundwater systems
Aquifers, wells, boreholes, springs, recharge zones, groundwater storage, and groundwater-surface-water interactions. Investigation includes availability, depth, recharge, abstraction, quality, salinity, contamination, depletion, and access rights.

### 3.3.3 Rainwater and stormwater systems
Rainfall capture, rooftop harvesting, urban runoff, drainage networks, retention, detention, infiltration, and stormwater storage or treatment. The same rainfall can be a supply opportunity, a flood hazard, or both depending on timing, location, infrastructure, and quality.

### 3.3.4 Water sourcing, abstraction, and intake
Intakes, borehole drilling and development, pumping, river abstraction, reservoir withdrawals, desalination intakes, and other ways of obtaining raw water. Access permissions, source sustainability, energy requirements, and source protection are central.

### 3.3.5 Water storage and reservoirs
Dams, reservoirs, tanks, cisterns, elevated storage, balancing reservoirs, industrial storage, and distributed storage. This includes construction, operation, inspection, leakage, sedimentation, water-age and quality management, and storage optimisation.

### 3.3.6 Drinking-water treatment and purification
Coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection, membrane treatment, adsorption, and other processes used to make water suitable for drinking. Treatment trains depend on source-water characteristics and applicable drinking-water standards; no single treatment method is universally sufficient.

### 3.3.7 Industrial and commercial water treatment
Water conditioning for manufacturing, food and beverage, pharmaceuticals, mining, power generation, cooling, boilers, laboratories, and other industrial or commercial uses. Specifications vary by process and may include hardness, dissolved solids, silica, microbes, metals, and other parameters.

### 3.3.8 Desalination and brackish-water treatment
Seawater and brackish-water treatment through reverse osmosis, thermal processes, electrodialysis, and other separation technologies. Energy use, pretreatment, membrane or equipment performance, concentrate/brine management, intake impacts, and delivered-water cost are important.

### 3.3.9 Water distribution and network systems
Pipelines, pumping stations, valves, pressure management, service connections, public standpipes, bulk supply, and network operations. Issues include non-revenue water, intermittent supply, leakage, bursts, pressure, asset condition, metering, and equitable access.

### 3.3.10 Wastewater collection and sewerage
Household, municipal, commercial, and industrial wastewater collection; sewer networks; septic systems; onsite sanitation; pumping; conveyance; and sludge collection. Service coverage, safe containment, infiltration, overflows, and connection economics are relevant.

### 3.3.11 Wastewater treatment and effluent management
Primary, secondary, and tertiary treatment; biological processes; nutrient removal; disinfection; industrial effluent treatment; sludge treatment; and safe discharge. Requirements depend on influent composition, receiving environment, reuse goals, and discharge standards.

### 3.3.12 Water reuse and recycling
Greywater reuse, treated municipal wastewater, industrial process-water recycling, cooling-water recirculation, agricultural reuse, and fit-for-purpose non-potable supply. Reuse requires appropriate treatment, monitoring, separation, user acceptance, and controls against exposure or cross-connection.

### 3.3.13 Irrigation, drainage, and agricultural water management
Surface irrigation, sprinkler systems, drip irrigation, fertigation, drainage, water scheduling, soil-water monitoring, and farm water storage. Water productivity, energy, salinity, soil conditions, crop needs, and basin-level sustainability interact.

### 3.3.14 Water quality, testing, and laboratory services
Sampling, field testing, laboratory analysis, microbial and chemical testing, contamination detection, quality assurance, certification, and compliance support. Sampling design, chain of custody, detection limits, test methods, and interpretation determine whether results can support decisions.

### 3.3.15 Water measurement, metering, and digital intelligence
Bulk and customer meters, smart meters, sensors, telemetry, SCADA, remote monitoring, leak detection, pressure analytics, water accounting, forecasting, digital twins, and decision-support systems. Data value depends on accuracy, coverage, maintenance, integration, and the decision it improves.

### 3.3.16 Flood, drought, and watershed management
Catchment management, flood forecasting, drainage, flood protection, drought planning, recharge protection, erosion control, water allocation, environmental flows, and nature-based watershed interventions. Risks and interventions cross administrative boundaries and affect multiple users.

### 3.3.17 Marine and coastal water systems
Coastal waters, estuaries, lagoons, marine water quality, seawater intake, coastal groundwater, saltwater intrusion, and marine discharge. This branch connects to fisheries, ports, coastal infrastructure, desalination, ecosystems, and pollution management.

### 3.3.18 Water governance, rights, finance, and service markets
Water allocation, abstraction permits, tariffs, utility regulation, public-private service arrangements, bulk-water contracts, water-user associations, basin institutions, infrastructure finance, affordability, and enforcement. Legal and institutional arrangements differ by jurisdiction and must be checked locally.

## 3. Cross-cutting dimensions for later vertical exploration

- **Quantity and reliability:** How much water is available, when, where, and with what seasonal or operational reliability?
- **Quality and suitability:** What is in the water, what uses can it support, and what treatment or monitoring is necessary?
- **Source and basin context:** What is the source, recharge or flow regime, catchment condition, and interaction with other users and ecosystems?
- **Access and rights:** Who controls or may abstract, store, distribute, use, discharge, or sell the water, under what permissions and obligations?
- **Infrastructure:** What assets, networks, energy, chemicals, operators, maintenance, and spare parts are required?
- **Economics:** Who pays for connection, abstraction, treatment, storage, distribution, testing, wastewater handling, and reliability? What determines cost per delivered or treated unit?
- **Customers and uses:** Which households, farms, businesses, industries, institutions, utilities, or public agencies have a defined water problem?
- **Environmental effects:** What are the impacts of abstraction, energy use, brine, sludge, effluent, land take, ecological flows, and downstream users?
- **Service performance:** What do continuity, pressure, quality, response time, losses, compliance, and customer experience look like?
- **Opportunity position:** Could value be created in equipment, installation, operations, maintenance, testing, monitoring, finance, coordination, software, or complete service delivery?

## 4. Opportunity forms visible across this territory

Potential positions include borehole and water-system assessment; water testing coordination; treatment equipment supply and maintenance; leak detection and network monitoring; tank and pump maintenance; industrial water audits; irrigation scheduling and efficiency support; wastewater and sanitation services; reuse-system design and operation; water-quality data and compliance reporting; bulk-water logistics; and project or service coordination.

These are opportunity forms for investigation, not claims that demand, technical competence, permissions, customer access, or profitability have been established. Water services can carry significant public-health, environmental, engineering, and liability responsibilities. Technical work must be delivered by appropriately qualified and authorised operators.

## 5. Important interfaces with other territories

- **Energy:** pumping, treatment, desalination, wastewater processing, and water supply depend on energy; hydropower and cooling systems depend on water.
- **Agriculture and food:** irrigation, livestock, aquaculture, processing, and food safety depend on water quantity and quality.
- **Land and soil:** catchments, recharge, drainage, erosion, salinity, and land-use change affect water systems.
- **Waste and remediation:** wastewater, sludge, leachate, industrial effluent, and contaminated groundwater connect directly to waste treatment and pollution control.
- **Materials and chemicals:** pipes, pumps, membranes, filters, treatment chemicals, sensors, and corrosion-resistant materials support water infrastructure.
- **Biodiversity and ecosystems:** wetlands, rivers, aquifers, estuaries, and coastal systems provide ecological functions and require environmental protection.
- **Environmental intelligence:** hydrological data, water-quality observations, asset mapping, remote sensing, and change detection support monitoring and decisions.

These interfaces identify relationships for future investigation; they do not imply that every linked system belongs inside this territory.

## 6. Safeguards and evidence boundaries

- Do not equate the presence of water with sustainable availability or legal access.
- Do not make drinking-water safety claims from appearance, smell, a single field test, or an unverified treatment device.
- Do not recommend abstraction or borehole development without checking source sustainability, local hydrogeology, permissions, and effects on other users.
- Do not treat wastewater or greywater as safe for reuse without fit-for-purpose treatment, monitoring, and exposure controls.
- Account for treatment residuals, sludge, brine, chemicals, energy, and final discharge or disposal.
- Distinguish equipment specifications, laboratory results, pilot performance, continuous operating performance, regulatory compliance, and customer outcomes.
- Verify the applicable jurisdiction's water rights, abstraction and discharge rules, drinking-water requirements, utility arrangements, and professional obligations.

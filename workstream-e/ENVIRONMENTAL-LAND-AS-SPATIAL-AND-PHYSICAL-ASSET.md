## 1. Why this is the first vertical unit

The horizontal survey has covered all 15 environmental territories. We are now descending into the first mapped branch in the existing taxonomy, not adopting the previously proposed candidate sequence. The first unit under 3.1.1 is **3.1.1.1 Land as a Spatial and Physical Asset**.

This unit asks a foundational question: what is land as a locatable, measurable, bounded, connected physical asset, before considering its soil properties, ecological contents, legal rights, development, or market value?

The investigation is deliberately foundational. It should clarify the object being described and the systems that identify, measure, record, manage, and use it. It does not assume that parcel mapping or land administration is the user's eventual opportunity.

## 2. Scope and conceptual boundary

Land as a spatial and physical asset concerns:
- **Location:** where a parcel or site is in relation to a recognised coordinate reference system and surrounding features.
- **Extent:** its area, dimensions, shape, and spatial footprint.
- **Boundaries:** the lines or features used to distinguish one parcel or site from another, and the evidence supporting those boundaries.
- **Identity:** the identifiers used to refer to a parcel across surveys, plans, registries, planning systems, taxation, utilities, and transactions.
- **Configuration:** parcel shape, frontage, depth, contiguity, fragmentation, adjacency, access corridors, and relationship to neighbouring parcels.
- **Physical setting:** terrain, surface features, drainage context, nearby roads and infrastructure, and surrounding land uses, without turning this unit into a soil or engineering assessment.
- **Spatial change:** subdivision, consolidation, boundary adjustment, encroachment, road acquisition, erosion, construction, and other changes that may alter the physical or recorded configuration.

### Important distinctions

A land parcel is not simply a polygon on a map. It is a spatial object that may be represented by several records and observations. Those representations can disagree.

Keep these concepts separate:

1. **Physical occupation or use:** what is visibly on or happening at a site.
2. **Surveyed geometry:** coordinates, bearings, distances, monuments/beacons, and survey plan representation established using appropriate methods.
3. **Cadastral parcel record:** the parcel representation maintained in a land administration/cadastre system.
4. **Legal interest or title:** the rights, interests, restrictions, and instruments recognised by the applicable legal and registry framework.
5. **Planning status:** zoning, permitted use, setbacks, reservations, easements, and development controls.
6. **Market description/value:** how a property is marketed, compared, or valued for a specific purpose.

These layers can be related, but one does not prove the others. A visible fence does not by itself prove a legal boundary. A coordinate pin does not establish title. A registered document does not remove the need to check whether the described parcel matches the site and current official records. A planning designation does not establish ownership.

## 3. Decomposition of the unit

### 3.1.1.1.1 Parcel and site identification
How a particular parcel or site is uniquely referred to across systems. Potential identifiers include plot or parcel number, survey plan number, land file number, title instrument reference, estate/layout reference, administrative area, and geographic coordinates.

**Investigation questions:** Are identifiers unique? Can they be matched across agencies and records? What happens when numbering changes, parcels split, or records are migrated? Are duplicate or conflicting identities detectable?

### 3.1.1.1.2 Spatial reference and coordinate framework
The coordinate reference systems, geodetic control, datum, projection, accuracy, and transformation procedures used to locate land. Spatial data from different sources may not align if reference systems, epochs, units, or transformation methods differ.

**Investigation questions:** Which coordinate reference systems are officially used? How are survey controls maintained? What accuracy is required for a given purpose? How are legacy plans reconciled with modern GNSS/GIS data?

### 3.1.1.1.3 Parcel geometry and measurement
The geometry of a parcel: points, lines, bearings, distances, area, perimeter, shape, and measurement uncertainty. Geometry can originate from field survey, digitised plans, photogrammetry, satellite imagery, or legacy maps, but these sources are not interchangeable in accuracy or legal standing.

**Investigation questions:** What method produced the geometry? What is its accuracy and date? Is the source authoritative for the intended use? Has a qualified surveyor checked the boundary?

### 3.1.1.1.4 Boundary evidence and monumentation
Evidence used to identify and re-establish parcel boundaries, including approved survey plans, beacon coordinates, physical monuments, historical records, adjoining parcel records, and official cadastral references.

**Investigation questions:** Are beacons present and undisturbed? Do physical markers agree with the approved survey record? How are missing, moved, disputed, or overlapping boundaries handled? Who is authorised to resolve discrepancies?

### 3.1.1.1.5 Parcel topology and spatial relationships
How parcels connect or relate: adjacency, shared boundaries, gaps, overlaps, enclaves, access routes, rights-of-way, road frontage, and subdivision patterns.

**Investigation questions:** Can the data detect gaps and overlaps? Are shared boundaries consistent? Is access physically present and legally established? Are there parcels with no clear access or inconsistent topology?

### 3.1.1.1.6 Parcel subdivision, consolidation, and change history
How a parcel's geometry and identity change through subdivision, merger, excision, acquisition, boundary adjustment, or correction. A change should preserve traceability to prior records and the authority for the change.

**Investigation questions:** Is there a parent-child history? Are superseded plans retained? Can a user determine which parcel geometry is current? Are changes reflected consistently across registry, cadastre, planning, and billing systems?

### 3.1.1.1.7 Site access and physical connectivity
The site's physical connection to public roads, paths, transport corridors, and service networks. Physical access and legal access must be investigated separately.

**Investigation questions:** Is access passable year-round? Is the route public, private, customary, or an easement? Does the mapped road reach the actual site? Are access constraints seasonal or disputed?

### 3.1.1.1.8 Parcel context and surrounding spatial fabric
The neighbouring parcels, settlements, roads, waterways, public facilities, infrastructure corridors, and surrounding land-use pattern that affect the site's spatial context.

**Investigation questions:** Which contextual layers are authoritative and current? Are nearby changes visible? Could a proposed use conflict with reservations, rights-of-way, protected areas, or surrounding development?

### 3.1.1.1.9 Spatial records, plans, and document linkage
The relationship between survey plans, title documents, allocation records, deeds, cadastral geometry, site photographs, planning records, and digital identifiers.

**Investigation questions:** Can a document be reliably linked to the correct parcel? Are scanned documents searchable? Are metadata, dates, version history, and provenance retained? Can records be authenticated by the issuing authority?

### 3.1.1.1.10 Physical change and parcel condition observation
Observation of visible changes affecting parcel configuration or physical identity: new structures, changed access, encroachment indicators, demolition, road expansion, earthworks, erosion, or boundary-marker disturbance.

**Investigation questions:** What change can be observed remotely, and what requires field verification? What imagery date and resolution are suitable? How are false positives and seasonal changes handled? Who has authority to confirm an encroachment or boundary violation?

## 4. How the land-object system works end to end

A simplified representation:

**Land/site exists → it is identified and surveyed → geometry and supporting evidence are recorded → parcel identifiers link records → authorised institutions maintain cadastral and legal records → users rely on those records for allocation, transactions, planning, taxation, infrastructure, and dispute resolution → physical and legal changes require updates.**

In plain language: the land exists independently of the database. Institutions and professionals create measurements and records to describe it. Those records become useful only when the location, geometry, identity, documentary evidence, and relevant legal or planning status can be matched reliably. If a parcel is divided, transferred, acquired, or physically altered, the relevant records must be updated and their history preserved. A map alone cannot perform all of these functions.

### Main system actors
- Landholders, occupiers, customary rights-holders, communities, and adjoining owners.
- Licensed/registered land surveyors and relevant professional bodies.
- State and FCT land administration departments and land registries.
- Surveyor-General offices and cadastral/mapping departments.
- Planning authorities and development-control bodies.
- Courts and dispute-resolution institutions.
- Utilities, infrastructure agencies, lenders, insurers, developers, valuers, estate professionals, and property users.
- GIS, document-management, imagery, and land-information-system providers.

The roles and authorities differ by jurisdiction. A national-level description must not erase state and FCT differences.

## 5. Nigeria institutional and evidence scan

### 5.1 Land governance and registration context

A Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientation report dated 20 March 2025 described the Federal Government's National Land Registration, Documentation and Titling Programme (NLRDP), developed with state governments and World Bank collaboration. The report quoted the Minister of Housing and Urban Development as describing land registration as below 10% nationally and set out an ambition to increase it to above 50% over ten years, alongside a National Digital Land Information System and more standardised, interoperable registration systems.

**Interpretation:** This is a government-reported baseline and programme ambition, not an independently verified current registration rate or proof that the target has been achieved. It signals a nationally recognised land-information and administration challenge. The programme's implementation, state participation, coverage, budgets, procurement, and operational progress need separate verification.

### 5.2 Federal Capital Territory example: AGIS and FCTA

The FCTA describes Abuja Geographic Information Systems (AGIS) as the FCT's spatial data integration and computerisation centre and says it computerises cadastral and land registry operations. Its stated functions include geospatial infrastructure, cadastral and registry records, title production, title deed plans, street naming and house numbering, geospatial data, property searches, and verification of land records.

The current AGIS/FCT Lands portal describes account-based access to land-file information, ground-rent payment, certified map downloads, application tracking, and receipts. The services page lists title searches, map production, survey data and site plans, deeds registration, certified true copies, and parcel subdivision/merger/extension.

The FCTA Survey and Mapping Department describes statutory surveying and mapping responsibilities in the FCT, including parcel surveys, layout designs, title deed plans, farm and quarry demarcation, mapping, control networks, charting and plot documentation, and supervision of registered surveyors.

**Interpretation:** The FCT provides an example of an institutional system in which parcel geometry, survey controls, land records, legal documents, map production, and customer services are connected. It is not evidence that every Nigerian state has equivalent systems, service levels, open data, or digital access. Nor does the public portal imply that bulk cadastral records are freely available for commercial reuse.

### 5.3 National technical capabilities

The Office of the Surveyor-General of the Federation describes cadastral and legal survey functions for government properties, cadastral GIS database development, survey control, and expert support in property litigation. The National Centre for Remote Sensing lists cadastral mapping as a service using high-resolution imagery and mapping techniques for land administration and property management.

**Interpretation:** Public-sector and technical-provider capabilities already exist. A potential entrant should first understand what these institutions and qualified providers already deliver. The gap, if any, may be in access, workflow, record quality, interoperability, user support, update frequency, or a specific underserved customer—not necessarily the absence of mapping capability.

## 6. Value chain and economic positions

### 6.1 Upstream: creating a reliable spatial description
- Establishing geodetic control and coordinate frameworks.
- Field reconnaissance and boundary evidence collection.
- Cadastral and topographic surveying by qualified professionals.
- Aerial, satellite, drone, and photogrammetric data acquisition.
- Digitisation and georeferencing of legacy maps and plans.
- Quality control, topology checks, and coordinate transformation.

### 6.2 Middle: maintaining parcel identity and records
- Cadastral database and land-information-system operation.
- Parcel identifier management and parent-child history.
- Survey plan/document scanning, indexing, metadata, and retrieval.
- Matching geometry to registry and planning records.
- Versioning, audit trails, backups, cybersecurity, and data governance.
- Change intake, validation, approval, and publication.

### 6.3 Downstream: using spatially reliable land information
- Property search and record verification by authorised channels.
- Site identification and due diligence support.
- Planning, infrastructure, utilities, taxation, and public asset management.
- Parcel subdivision/merger workflows.
- Development monitoring and change detection.
- Lender, insurer, developer, valuation, and transaction workflows.

### 6.4 Possible participation positions (hypotheses only)

| Position | What the role might deliver | Important boundary or dependency |
|---|---|---|
| Document preparation and indexing support | Organise, scan, classify, and link records under an institution's or client's process | Data permission, confidentiality, quality control; cannot certify title |
| GIS data cleaning and topology support | Identify duplicate identifiers, gaps, overlaps, invalid geometry, and inconsistent metadata | Must not alter authoritative records or represent output as a legal survey |
| Field-data collection assistant | Capture permitted site observations, photos, and reference information for a qualified team | Consent, safety, supervision, equipment and professional boundaries |
| Parcel information workflow support | Help users assemble records and navigate official application requirements | Must not guarantee ownership, interpret disputed rights, or act as an unauthorised legal intermediary |
| Change-monitoring support | Flag visible changes around defined assets or sites for review | Imagery limitations; observation is not proof of encroachment or title violation |
| GIS/LIS implementation and integration | Configure systems, migrate records, connect maps and document workflows | Procurement, technical competence, security, data governance, institutional approval |
| Professional survey and cadastral services | Survey, set out, certify, and produce authoritative plans | Requires appropriately registered/licensed professionals and statutory compliance |
| Land administration infrastructure | Build and operate cadastral, registry, geodetic, and document systems | Large institutional procurement, capital, security, legal and governance dependencies |

These positions are not evidence of demand, accessible contracts, or immediate income. They are possible locations in the system where value could be created if a defined user problem and lawful access are established.

## 7. Constraints, failure modes, and safeguards

1. **False certainty from maps:** imagery and approximate coordinates can be mistaken for authoritative boundaries. Label source, date, scale, resolution, accuracy, and intended use.
2. **Title/geometry mismatch:** a document may describe a parcel that does not match current ground conditions or cadastral records. Refer discrepancies to the competent registry and qualified professionals.
3. **Duplicate or conflicting identifiers:** legacy and modern systems may refer to the same site differently, or the same identifier may be reused. Preserve provenance and version history.
4. **Unauthorised practice:** boundary determination, cadastral certification, legal title opinions, and other regulated functions must remain with authorised professionals and institutions.
5. **Data rights and privacy:** land records may contain personal, financial, security-sensitive, or restricted information. Public visibility does not automatically grant permission for bulk extraction or resale.
6. **Community and customary rights:** formal parcel records may not fully capture customary interests, communal use, occupancy, or competing claims. Do not treat unregistered or poorly documented interests as nonexistent.
7. **Physical versus legal access:** a visible track or road does not prove a right-of-way; a recorded easement may not be physically passable.
8. **Stale records:** physical subdivision, acquisition, construction, erosion, or encroachment may not be reflected promptly in official records.
9. **Institutional fragmentation:** survey, registry, planning, taxation, and utilities may maintain different datasets and update cycles.
10. **Conflict and safety:** fieldwork may encounter disputes, security risks, or unsafe sites. Do not enter land or collect personal/property data without appropriate permission and safeguards.

## 8. Initial opportunity hypotheses and what would validate them

No opportunity is selected. The following are testable hypotheses:

### H1 — Record discoverability and document-to-parcel matching
A defined user group may spend time and money locating, organising, and reconciling parcel documents with official parcel identifiers and maps.

**Evidence needed:** user interviews; examples of permitted workflows; time/cost of current process; error frequency; existing registry or professional services; willingness to pay; data handling permissions.

### H2 — GIS data quality and change-history support
An institution, professional firm, developer, or asset owner may need support identifying inconsistencies in non-authoritative working datasets before qualified review and official update.

**Evidence needed:** a real dataset with permission; documented data-quality issues; a responsible technical owner; accepted validation criteria; procurement or service budget; liability boundary.

### H3 — Site/asset change observation for a defined decision
A land or asset manager may need periodic evidence of visible physical change around a site or parcel, with flagged observations sent to an authorised reviewer.

**Evidence needed:** defined decision and user; baseline and repeat imagery; adequate resolution and update frequency; ground truth; false-positive rate; existing alternatives; payment rationale.

### H4 — Field support to existing survey/GIS providers
Qualified providers may need trained assistance for non-certifying data capture, document preparation, or GIS processing under their supervision.

**Evidence needed:** actual provider workload; required skills and equipment; supervision and insurance; professional rules; payment terms; proof that this is a legitimate delegated task.

Do not convert these hypotheses into a service offer until the evidence is gathered. A technically possible task is not necessarily a market.

## 9. Smallest responsible investigation tests

A desk-based first test can be conducted without attempting a land survey or accessing private records:

1. Map the official public-facing land-information services in one jurisdiction.
2. Record which tasks can be completed online, which require physical visits, and which require original documents or professional services.
3. Use only public demonstration material or data explicitly authorised for use.
4. Trace one fictional or public demonstration parcel through the workflow without submitting an application or claiming a title result.
5. Record where identity, geometry, document, and legal-status layers are joined or remain separate.
6. Interview a small number of willing users or professionals only with clear consent and without asking them to disclose sensitive title documents.
7. Ask which step is slow, confusing, costly, error-prone, or unavailable, and who is responsible for fixing it.
8. Stop if the only apparent value requires unauthorised access to records, unlicensed surveying, legal advice, or guarantees of ownership.

The test should produce a process map and evidence log, not a prototype or business launch.

# Environmental Vertical Exploration and Selection Method

**Status:** Adopted working procedure, 2026-11-06. This method governs the transition from the completed horizontal survey to selective vertical exploration. It is revisable when practical evidence reveals a better method.

**Parent:** [Environmental Opportunity Intelligence](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-INTELLIGENCE.md)

## 1. Purpose

The horizontal survey has provided broad maps for territories 3.1–3.15. Vertical exploration now needs a disciplined way to choose branches without simply following the order in which they were first mapped, over-focusing on a familiar technology, or prematurely narrowing the opportunity landscape.

This method is designed to:
- compare candidates across the whole environmental economy;
- preserve the complete opportunity landscape and accessible participation routes;
- investigate dependencies before making unsupported opportunity claims;
- distinguish a promising research topic from a viable customer proposition;
- maintain traceability from discovery to evidence, decisions, and any eventual experiment.

## 2. Governing rules

1. **No automatic first-in-line priority.** Territory order does not determine research priority.
2. **Evidence before ranking.** Unknowns remain unknown; do not manufacture scores.
3. **Problem before product.** Identify the affected system, user, operational problem, and consequence before choosing a machine, app, or service.
4. **Whole system before isolated stage.** Map inputs, processes, outputs, residuals, users, infrastructure, value chain, economics, and safeguards.
5. **Two opportunity lenses remain active.** Keep large-scale/capital-intensive possibilities visible alongside accessible roles that may use existing assets and providers.
6. **End-to-end without assuming ownership.** Examine complete customer outcomes, but test whether the entrant can provide one specialist component, coordinate existing providers, or integrate capabilities with permission.
7. **Local context must be evidenced.** Global examples and market estimates are hypotheses for local investigation, not proof of demand or suitability.
8. **No false validation.** A public announcement, technology video, market forecast, or provider website is not proof of operational performance or willingness to pay.
9. **Surface credible opportunities immediately.** The user decides whether to pause the planned sequence.
10. **Preserve stopping points.** Do not repeat completed work. Revisit only for new evidence, a dependency, contradiction, explicit request, or justified correction.

## 3. The vertical investigation sequence

For a selected candidate, work through these gates. A gate can send the investigation back to an earlier question when evidence exposes a gap; this is not repetition if the reason is documented.

### Gate 1 — Define the real-world system and boundary
- Specify the physical, biological, industrial, institutional, or commercial system.
- Identify location, scale, time horizon, and actors.
- Define what is inside and outside the investigation.
- Cross-reference other territories without duplicating their subject matter.

### Gate 2 — Establish the problem and affected parties
- Describe the real-world situation in plain language.
- Identify who experiences the problem, who bears the cost, who controls the process, and who could pay to change it.
- Separate observed problems from assumptions and general statements.

### Gate 3 — Map the end-to-end system
- Trace inputs, access, collection, preparation, processes, outputs, co-products, residuals, users, distribution, maintenance, and end-of-life.
- Identify infrastructure, suppliers, operators, intermediaries, regulators, and affected communities.
- Consider alternative pathways and existing solutions.

### Gate 4 — Establish evidence and measurement
- Define the claims that matter and the evidence needed to support them.
- Identify authoritative sources, primary records, datasets, field observations, experts, and independent tests.
- Record provenance, dates, definitions, uncertainty, missingness, and contradictory evidence.
- Separate direct observation, reported fact, inference, hypothesis, and unknown.

### Gate 5 — Map dependencies and constraints
- Technical: process performance, quality, capacity, reliability, maintenance, scale-up.
- Resource: feedstock, water, energy, land, labour, data, skills, and infrastructure.
- Commercial: buyer, user, budget owner, pricing, procurement, payment, working capital.
- Legal/institutional: rights, permits, licences, standards, insurance, professional duties, liability.
- Environmental/social: lifecycle impacts, pollution, safety, community rights, distribution of benefits and burdens.
- Timing: seasonality, lead times, policy changes, infrastructure readiness, and customer cycles.

### Gate 6 — Identify value-capture positions
Assess multiple possible roles, not only manufacturing or owning assets:
- operator or producer;
- equipment maker, importer, distributor, renter, or maintainer;
- feedstock/resource supplier, collector, aggregator, or logistics provider;
- specialist service provider;
- data, intelligence, measurement, or monitoring provider;
- broker, marketplace, or transaction coordinator;
- implementation partner or systems integrator;
- documentation, compliance workflow, or reporting support;
- finance, insurance, verification, or assurance partner where authorised;
- full end-to-end service provider.

For each role, identify the customer outcome, responsibilities, dependencies, required competence, capital, permissions, and accountability.

### Gate 7 — Test the economic case
- Identify who pays and why.
- Estimate costs from evidence, not generic assumptions.
- Include acquisition, labour, equipment, transport, energy, water, inventory, maintenance, compliance, insurance, financing, waste handling, and working capital.
- Distinguish revenue, gross margin, cash flow, avoided costs, public benefits, and environmental benefits.
- Test alternatives and sensitivity to volume, price, utilisation, yield, seasonality, and failure.
- Do not assume environmental benefit creates a paying market.

### Gate 8 — Define the smallest responsible test
- State the uncertainty the test is meant to reduce.
- Choose a reversible, lawful, safe, low-cost test proportionate to the risk.
- Define access, consent, professional supervision, baseline, success/failure criteria, and stop conditions.
- Avoid experiments involving hazardous substances, environmental release, unqualified sampling, unsafe machinery, or regulated activities without appropriate controls and expertise.
- A test may be a customer interview, public-data analysis, provider quotation, site observation with permission, desk-based reconstruction, or supervised field activity; it need not be a prototype.

### Gate 9 — Decision and record
Classify the candidate as one of:
- **Advance:** evidence supports the next defined investigation or test.
- **Hold:** potentially important, but a named dependency or evidence gap blocks progress.
- **Merge:** substantially overlaps another candidate; retain both source references.
- **Reject for now:** evidence indicates poor fit, unacceptable risk, no credible user, or disproportionate barriers.
- **Monitor:** dependent on a future event, policy, technology, or market change.
- **Validated for a defined scope:** only when the specific customer, technical, legal, environmental, and economic claims required for that scope are adequately supported.

Record the decision, evidence, confidence, unresolved risks, date, and reason. Reversal requires a new entry; do not erase the earlier decision.

## 4. Candidate comparison rubric

Use the screening dimensions in [Environmental Opportunity Portfolio Screening](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-PORTFOLIO-SCREENING.md). For each candidate, create a compact evidence card containing:
- candidate ID and name;
- primary and secondary territories;
- problem and affected user;
- payer/budget owner hypothesis;
- current alternatives;
- environmental significance;
- evidence available and confidence;
- key dependencies and constraints;
- complete capital-intensive opportunity view;
- accessible participation routes;
- smallest responsible next test;
- status and decision rationale.

Do not use a weighted aggregate score until the evidence is sufficiently comparable and the user has approved the weighting. A shortlist is a research sequence, not a business commitment.

## 5. Dependencies and investigation order

Some investigations require prerequisites:
- Resource availability and quality may need 3.1 evidence before downstream processing can be assessed.
- Waste recovery requires stream quantity, composition, contamination, ownership, logistics, and buyer specifications before machinery economics.
- Water services may require water-quality or hydrological evidence, operator access, and qualified laboratories or technicians.
- Geospatial intelligence requires suitable imagery or records, ground truth, licensing, update frequency, and a decision-maker.
- Agricultural opportunities require a defined production segment and at least one production or market cycle where seasonality matters.
- Energy services require load and cost data, safe technical competence, and reliable equipment/service providers.
- Remediation requires site access, baseline assessment, competent specialists, safe handling, and long-term verification.
- Nature-related claims require ecological baselines, rights-holder engagement, measurable outcomes, and safeguards against exaggerated claims.
- Environmental finance or credit markets require the underlying environmental outcome, legal rights, methodology, verification, and real demand to be established first.

A dependency can be a reason to investigate a supporting branch first, but do not expand into every adjacent territory automatically. Investigate only what is needed to answer the selected question.

## 6. Documentation and continuity protocol

Every completed exploration must be documented in the Lifeconomist GitHub repository before moving to the next major task. Each record should state:
- scope and question;
- method and sources;
- findings and evidence status;
- assumptions and unknowns;
- dependencies and cross-links;
- opportunity positions, clearly labelled as hypotheses or leads;
- decisions and stopping point;
- next action.

Before resuming, inspect the existing record and continue from its stopping point. Do not recreate completed material. Use a new dated evidence or decision entry when a discovery changes a prior conclusion.

## 7. User control

The assistant may propose a research sequence and surface opportunities, but the user chooses whether to:
- continue the comparison;
- pause for a deeper investigation;
- test a specific opportunity;
- defer or reject a candidate;
- change the selection criteria.

The assistant must explain why a candidate is being proposed and what remains unknown. Do not present a provisional shortlist as a final direction for Lifeconomist.

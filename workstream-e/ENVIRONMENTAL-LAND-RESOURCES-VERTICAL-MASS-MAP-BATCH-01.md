## 2. Branch 3.1.1.2 — Terrain, Topography, and Landforms

### Scope
The physical form and elevation of the land surface: elevation, relief, slope, aspect, curvature, ridges, valleys, plains, plateaus, hills, mountains, escarpments, depressions, ravines, gullies, and landform transitions.

### Main components
1. Elevation and height reference systems.
2. Digital elevation models (DEMs) and digital terrain models (DTMs).
3. Slope, aspect, curvature, roughness, and local relief.
4. Landform classification and geomorphological mapping.
5. Watershed divides, flow direction, accumulation, and drainage pathways.
6. Terrain constraints for roads, buildings, agriculture, utilities, and other infrastructure.
7. Terrain change from excavation, earthworks, mining, construction, erosion, or mass movement.
8. Terrain data validation, resolution, vertical accuracy, and uncertainty.

### How the system works
**Surface observations and elevation measurements → elevation model → terrain derivatives and landform interpretation → suitability, design, routing, drainage, and hazard decisions → field verification and updated models.**

In plain language, terrain analysis turns measurements of the land's shape into information that helps people decide where and how to build, farm, route infrastructure, manage water, or assess hazards. The result depends on the elevation source, resolution, vertical datum, vegetation/building removal, processing method, and field checks. A coarse global elevation model may be adequate for regional screening but unsuitable for engineering design or parcel-level decisions.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- DEM/DTM acquisition, processing, correction, and quality assessment.
- Terrain and slope mapping for defined sites or infrastructure corridors.
- Watershed and drainage analysis for planning and agricultural use.
- Earthworks quantity estimation and progress monitoring, when validated against survey control.
- Terrain-risk screening for landslides, erosion, flooding pathways, and unstable slopes.
- Data preparation and GIS support for engineering, planning, and environmental consultancies.
- Field survey and engineering interpretation by appropriately qualified professionals.

### Constraints and safeguards
- DEM resolution and vertical accuracy may not support detailed engineering.
- Tree canopy, buildings, water, and radar artefacts can distort elevation.
- Vertical datums and coordinate systems may differ.
- Terrain-derived flow paths are models, not complete hydrological studies.
- Engineering design, setting-out, and certification require qualified professionals.
- Do not infer land ownership, legal boundaries, or title from terrain data.

### Later investigation questions
Which elevation datasets cover the chosen jurisdiction? What are their source dates, spatial resolution, vertical accuracy, licence and known artefacts? Which decisions actually require better terrain data than existing sources? Who pays for terrain information, and what professional review is required?

## 3. Branch 3.1.1.3 — Surface Cover and Terrestrial Land Cover

### Scope
What physically covers the land surface at a given time: trees, shrubs, grass, crops, bare soil, exposed rock, built surfaces, wetlands, water, snow/ice where relevant, and mixed or transitional cover.

### Main components
1. Land-cover classification systems and class definitions.
2. Satellite, aerial, drone, and field observations.
3. Image preprocessing, atmospheric correction, mosaicking, and compositing.
4. Supervised, unsupervised, object-based, and machine-learning classification.
5. Training/validation samples and reference data.
6. Accuracy assessment, confusion matrices, class-specific error, and uncertainty.
7. Seasonal and multi-year cover change.
8. Fragmentation, canopy density, vegetation indices, burn scars, and exposed-surface indicators.
9. National, regional, and local land-cover inventories.

### How the system works
**Imagery and field reference data → classification → accuracy assessment → cover maps and change layers → decisions about land management, conservation, agriculture, infrastructure, and risk.**

A land-cover map estimates what is physically present on the ground. It is not the same as a land-use map: a field may be covered by crops while its use is agriculture; a forest may be protected, commercially managed, or used by local communities. Classification errors are inevitable and must be reported rather than hidden.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Land-cover mapping for a defined geography or project.
- Classification validation and ground-reference collection.
- Change detection for deforestation, vegetation loss, urban expansion, fire, or wetland conversion.
- Monitoring support for restoration, infrastructure, agriculture, and conservation projects.
- Historical imagery analysis and map updates.
- Data quality review and preparation for planners, researchers, public agencies, and project operators.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Cloud cover, seasonal differences, sensor resolution, shadows, mixed pixels, and class confusion.
- Accuracy can vary substantially between classes and locations.
- Public imagery does not guarantee unrestricted commercial reuse of every derived product.
- Field reference collection requires lawful access and safe practice.
- A cover change signal does not by itself establish cause, illegality, ownership, or ecological impact.

### Later investigation questions
Which land-cover classifications are already available for Nigeria and for which years/states? How accurate are they locally? Which classes matter to a specific decision? Is the gap coverage, resolution, timeliness, validation, or interpretation?

## 4. Branch 3.1.1.4 — Land Use and Land-Use Systems

### Scope
How people, organisations, and institutions use, allocate, designate, or manage land: residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, conservation, recreational, transport, mining, infrastructure, public, institutional, and mixed uses.

### Main components
1. Existing and historical land-use inventories.
2. Statutory zoning and planning designations.
3. Actual observed use versus permitted/designated use.
4. Land-use transitions and urban/peri-urban expansion.
5. Competing uses and allocation trade-offs.
6. Land-use intensity, density, and functional patterns.
7. Public, customary, private, communal, and institutional land-use systems.
8. Land-use planning, development control, and monitoring.
9. Land-use scenarios and spatial planning.

### How the system works
**Human activities and institutional designations → observed and recorded land-use patterns → planning and allocation decisions → land-use change → monitoring of outcomes and conflicts.**

Land use describes the purpose or activity associated with land, not simply its visible cover. A parcel may be designated industrial but remain vacant; a location may be visibly cultivated without the observer knowing the rights or legal permissions involved. Reliable analysis distinguishes what is recorded, what is observed, and what is legally permitted.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Land-use inventories and spatial planning support.
- Monitoring urban expansion and land-use transition.
- Site and corridor screening for defined projects.
- Development-control and public-asset monitoring support.
- Research on competing land demands and infrastructure implications.
- Data integration for planning, utilities, logistics, and environmental assessment.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Planning rules and land-use authority vary by jurisdiction.
- Observed activity is not proof of permission or legal compliance.
- Informal, customary, seasonal, and mixed uses may be missed by simplistic categories.
- Land-use classifications can encode political and social choices; document definitions and limitations.
- Do not label occupation or use as unlawful without authoritative evidence.

### Later investigation questions
Which authority holds current land-use plans? Are plans digitised and accessible? How frequently are they updated? Where do observed use and official designation diverge? Which decision-maker needs this information and what action follows?

## 5. Branch 3.1.1.5 — Terrestrial Ecosystems and Ecological Landscapes

### Scope
The arrangement and condition of connected land-based ecological areas and landscape mosaics, including habitat patches, ecological corridors, forest/grassland mosaics, riparian zones, and ecological transition areas. Detailed species and ecosystem-function analysis remains primarily in 3.8.

### Main components
1. Landscape units and ecological regions.
2. Habitat patches and patch configuration.
3. Ecological corridors and connectivity.
4. Edge effects, fragmentation, and isolation.
5. Riparian and terrestrial transition zones.
6. Landscape-scale disturbance and recovery.
7. Ecosystem-service relationships at landscape scale.
8. Human settlement, infrastructure, and ecological interface.
9. Ecological landscape planning and monitoring.

### How the system works
**Habitat and landscape patterns → connectivity and ecological condition → movement, ecological processes, and ecosystem services → land-use and conservation decisions → long-term monitoring.**

Ecological landscapes function across boundaries that may not match parcel or administrative boundaries. A road, settlement, farm expansion, or other land conversion can divide habitat and alter connectivity. A map can identify possible fragmentation, but ecological consequences need appropriate biological evidence and field validation.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Landscape connectivity and fragmentation analysis.
- Ecological baseline and monitoring support.
- Conservation and restoration project mapping.
- Screening of infrastructure or land-use projects for ecological sensitivity.
- Nature-based solution planning and monitoring support.
- Data services for conservation organisations, project developers, researchers, and public bodies.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Ecological boundaries are not always visible from imagery.
- Species movement and ecological function require specialist evidence.
- Community and customary rights must be respected.
- Do not claim biodiversity gain or ecosystem-service value from land-cover change alone.
- Conservation and restoration claims require baselines, duration, permanence, and independent checks.

### Later investigation questions
Which landscape units are already mapped? Where are connectivity gaps? Which projects have funded ecological monitoring needs? What indicators and field protocols are accepted? Who holds rights and who receives benefits?

## 6. Branch 3.1.1.6 — Land Condition, Disturbance, and Physical Constraints

### Scope
Observable physical condition and disturbances affecting land capability or stability: erosion features, gullying, desertification indicators, subsidence, landslide signs, compaction, excavation, dumping, surface sealing, degradation, abandoned disturbed sites, and other constraints. Soil diagnosis belongs primarily in 3.6; contamination/remediation in 3.10.

### Main components
1. Surface disturbance and exposed ground.
2. Erosion features and sediment movement.
3. Gully development and slope instability indicators.
4. Subsidence, settlement, and ground deformation.
5. Desertification and land degradation indicators.
6. Compaction, sealing, and altered infiltration.
7. Excavation, quarrying, earthworks, and abandoned disturbed land.
8. Fire, vegetation removal, and disturbance recovery.
9. Condition baselines, severity classification, and repeat monitoring.

### How the system works
**Natural or human pressure → physical change/disturbance → observation and diagnosis → risk or condition assessment → prevention, repair, rehabilitation, or monitoring.**

A visible symptom does not always reveal its cause. For example, exposed soil may reflect construction, seasonal farming, drought, fire, or erosion. A landslide scar is not enough to establish future slope stability. Screening can flag a concern, but diagnosis and engineering or soil treatment require qualified assessment.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Repeat observation and disturbance mapping.
- Land-condition inventories for asset owners and project operators.
- Erosion/gully monitoring and reporting support.
- Site inspection documentation under qualified supervision.
- Rehabilitation progress monitoring.
- Data support for environmental impact, compliance, and risk workflows.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Remote indicators can be ambiguous and seasonal.
- Ground investigation may be required for diagnosis.
- Engineering, geotechnical, and soil-treatment work requires qualified competence.
- Contaminated or unstable sites may pose health and safety risks.
- Do not make remediation or safety claims from imagery alone.

### Later investigation questions
Which degradation types are locally material? Which public monitoring datasets exist? Who is responsible for prevention or rehabilitation? Are there recurring inspection/reporting needs and funded programmes?

## 7. Branch 3.1.1.7 — Land Availability, Suitability, and Competing Uses

### Scope
Whether a particular area is appropriate for a specified use, considering physical, ecological, legal, infrastructure, social, economic, and operational constraints. Suitability is always purpose-specific.

### Main components
1. Define the proposed use and required conditions.
2. Physical criteria: terrain, drainage, surface condition, and access.
3. Environmental criteria: protected areas, habitat, water, pollution, and hazard exposure.
4. Infrastructure criteria: roads, electricity, water, communications, and services.
5. Legal/planning criteria: permitted use, restrictions, tenure, easements, and approvals.
6. Social criteria: communities, livelihoods, customary interests, and displacement risk.
7. Economic criteria: land cost, access cost, servicing, logistics, and operating costs.
8. Multi-criteria analysis, weights, exclusions, and uncertainty.
9. Scenario comparison and suitability updates.

### How the system works
**Define use requirements → identify criteria and exclusions → assemble spatial and non-spatial evidence → evaluate trade-offs → shortlist sites → verify with authorities, rights-holders, and field professionals.**

A suitability model does not decide that land is available, legally usable, socially acceptable, or commercially viable. It helps screen alternatives. Criteria and weights can change the result, so assumptions must be visible and the decision-maker must own the trade-offs.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Site-screening and comparative analysis for a defined user.
- Data assembly and due-diligence coordination.
- Constraint mapping for infrastructure and project planning.
- Scenario analysis and suitability model maintenance.
- Specialist referrals for legal, environmental, engineering, and survey verification.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Do not present a weighted suitability score as an objective truth.
- Missing data can create false confidence.
- Land rights, consent, protected status, and community impacts cannot be reduced to a map score.
- Site recommendations require current data and professional/legal review.
- Suitability for one use does not imply suitability for another.

### Later investigation questions
Which users make recurring site decisions? What criteria are mandatory versus negotiable? What evidence do they currently use? What errors are costly? Who pays for screening, and what verification do they require?

## 8. Branch 3.1.1.8 — Land Access, Tenure, Rights, and Restrictions

### Scope
The legal, customary, administrative, and practical relationships that determine who may occupy, access, use, transfer, manage, or restrict land. This includes ownership interests, occupancy, leases, licences, easements, rights-of-way, community and customary interests, public land, protected status, concessions, restrictions, and disputes.

### Main components
1. Statutory land tenure and applicable land laws.
2. Customary and communal tenure systems.
3. Rights of occupancy, leases, licences, concessions, and other interests.
4. Easements, rights-of-way, access rights, and servitudes.
5. Public land, protected land, reserves, and statutory restrictions.
6. Community consent, consultation, benefit-sharing, and safeguards.
7. Allocation, transfer, registration, renewal, and revocation processes.
8. Competing claims, boundary/tenure disputes, and dispute resolution.
9. Due diligence, records, and rights-holder identification.

### How the system works
**Rights and interests arise under law, custom, allocation, agreement, or other recognised processes → evidence and records are created → access/use decisions are made → transfers, restrictions, disputes, and changes require lawful procedures.**

A physical site and its legal rights are different layers. A person occupying land may have interests not obvious from a registry extract; a registered interest may be subject to restrictions or competing claims. Rights are jurisdiction-specific and can be complex. No map, informal assurance, or single document should be treated as conclusive title verification.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Administrative document organisation and workflow support under authorised supervision.
- Public-record navigation and information preparation, without legal conclusions.
- Rights-holder and stakeholder mapping for projects using appropriate consent.
- Land-access risk screening for project teams.
- Coordination with qualified surveyors, lawyers, valuers, and relevant authorities.
- Monitoring of official policy, allocation, and land administration changes.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Legal practice and title opinions must be left to authorised legal professionals.
- Survey certification and boundary determination require qualified surveyors.
- Land disputes can create serious personal, legal, and safety risks.
- Customary and communal rights must not be erased by incomplete formal records.
- Consent, privacy, and benefit-sharing are essential for sensitive community data.
- Never promise title, guarantee access, or facilitate unlawful acquisition or dispossession.

### Later investigation questions
Which jurisdiction and land regime apply? Which records are authoritative and accessible? How are customary interests recognised? What are the actual user pain points in official processes? Which administrative tasks may lawfully be delegated?

## 9. Branch 3.1.1.9 — Land Development, Preparation, and Site Servicing

### Scope
Activities that convert land into a site capable of supporting a defined use, and the services that keep the site functional: lawful clearing, grading, earthworks, drainage, access roads, utilities, site preparation, erosion control, rehabilitation obligations, and ongoing site management.

### Main components
1. Feasibility and pre-development site investigation.
2. Site clearance and vegetation management under approvals.
3. Survey control, setting-out, and construction layout.
4. Cut/fill, grading, compaction, and earthworks.
5. Stormwater drainage and erosion/sediment control.
6. Access roads and internal circulation.
7. Water, sanitation, electricity, and communications servicing.
8. Environmental and social management during construction.
9. Construction monitoring, inspection, and quality records.
10. Site maintenance, rehabilitation, and decommissioning.

### How the system works
**Land rights and approvals → site investigation and design → contractors and infrastructure provision → construction and environmental controls → handover, operation, maintenance, and eventual rehabilitation.**

Site preparation is not just moving earth. It depends on lawful access, planning permission, survey control, geotechnical design, drainage, environmental safeguards, contractor competence, and utility coordination. Poor sequencing can cause erosion, flooding, unstable ground, cost overruns, and damage to neighbouring land.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Project documentation, progress reporting, and contractor coordination.
- Procurement and supplier intelligence for site works.
- Environmental inspection and compliance record support.
- GIS-based site inventory and change monitoring.
- Equipment rental/coordination through existing operators.
- Specialist earthworks, drainage, surveying, and engineering services by qualified providers.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Construction and engineering work require permits and competent professionals.
- Earthworks can trigger erosion, sediment, flooding, and habitat impacts.
- Land access and rights must be established before work.
- Equipment, fuel, labour, insurance, and payment risk can be substantial.
- Do not specify designs or certify works outside professional competence.

### Later investigation questions
Which site-development tasks are routinely outsourced? Who owns the budget? What failures or delays occur? What work can be performed as documentation/coordination support? Which providers and licences are required?

## 10. Branch 3.1.1.10 — Land Observation, Mapping, and Change Intelligence

### Scope
The information and observation layer used to describe land and detect change: surveys, GIS, satellite imagery, aerial/drone imagery, field observations, land inventories, spatial databases, map products, and repeat monitoring.

### Main components
1. Data acquisition: satellite, aerial, drone, field, survey, public records.
2. Georeferencing, coordinate systems, and spatial alignment.
3. Feature extraction, digitisation, classification, and interpretation.
4. GIS database design, metadata, provenance, and versioning.
5. Change detection and event monitoring.
6. Ground truth, accuracy assessment, and uncertainty reporting.
7. Field verification and issue escalation.
8. Dashboards, reports, alerts, APIs, and decision-support outputs.
9. Data access, licensing, privacy, security, and retention.

### How the system works
**Acquire observations → align and process data → interpret features and change → validate against independent evidence → communicate findings to a decision-maker → update the record when authorised.**

The commercial value is not the map alone. It comes from answering a decision-relevant question with adequate accuracy, timeliness, traceability, and trust. Existing government and private services must be mapped before proposing a new product.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- GIS data preparation, cleaning, and documentation.
- Historical land-cover or site-change analysis.
- Asset/site inventory maintenance.
- Field-reference collection under permission.
- Repeat monitoring and exception reporting.
- Data integration and workflow automation.
- Specialist mapping and surveying delivered by qualified providers.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Different sensors and datasets have different licences, resolutions, dates, and accuracy.
- Change detection produces candidate signals, not definitive legal findings.
- Drones, surveys, and fieldwork may require permissions and competent operators.
- Sensitive location/property data require safeguards.
- Avoid duplicating free/official services without identifying a specific unmet decision.

### Later investigation questions
Which users need recurring updates? What decision changes because of the information? What existing platform already serves them? What accuracy and update interval are required? Can a small repeatable workflow be tested with permitted data?

## 11. Branch 3.1.1.11 — Land Stewardship, Conservation, and Restoration

### Scope
Long-term protection, responsible management, rehabilitation, and ecological restoration of land, including erosion control, sustainable land management, conservation agreements, landscape recovery, and monitoring.

### Main components
1. Land stewardship plans and management objectives.
2. Conservation areas, buffers, and protected landscapes.
3. Sustainable land-management practices.
4. Erosion and degradation prevention.
5. Revegetation, reforestation, and assisted natural regeneration.
6. Restoration of degraded sites and landscapes.
7. Invasive-species control and ecological threat management.
8. Community stewardship and customary management.
9. Baselines, indicators, monitoring, and adaptive management.
10. Restoration finance, contracts, and benefit-sharing.

### How the system works
**Baseline and rights-holder engagement → define ecological/social objectives → choose interventions → implement and maintain → monitor outcomes over time → adapt management and verify claims.**

Restoration is not simply planting trees or completing a one-off activity. The intervention must match the ecosystem, site history, causes of degradation, rights and livelihoods, and long-term maintenance capacity. Outcomes may take years and can fail if funding, tenure, monitoring, or local participation is weak.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Baseline mapping and monitoring support.
- Restoration project documentation and progress tracking.
- Nursery, seed, planting-material, and maintenance supply chains.
- Community coordination and field logistics.
- Erosion-control and land-management services by competent providers.
- Verification and reporting support with accepted protocols.
- Project development and finance coordination where a real payer exists.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Ecological outcomes need measurable baselines and long-term follow-up.
- Land tenure, community consent, livelihoods, and benefit-sharing are central.
- Avoid monoculture or unsuitable planting prescriptions.
- Do not claim carbon/biodiversity credits or ecological gains without the relevant standards and verification.
- Funding and maintenance obligations must be explicit.

### Later investigation questions
Which degraded landscapes have funded programmes? Who owns/uses the land? Which intervention is ecologically appropriate? Who pays for implementation and maintenance? What indicators and independent verification are accepted?

## 12. Branch 3.1.1.12 — Land Markets, Valuation, and Allocation

### Scope
The economic systems through which land is valued, rented, bought, sold, allocated, assembled, leased, concessioned, taxed, or otherwise assigned to uses and users.

### Main components
1. Land and property market structure.
2. Sale, lease, rent, concession, and allocation mechanisms.
3. Valuation methods and comparable evidence.
4. Location, access, infrastructure, amenities, and externalities.
5. Development potential and highest-and-best-use analysis.
6. Restrictions, title, tenure, easements, and legal risk.
7. Public land allocation, compulsory acquisition, and compensation.
8. Land taxation, ground rent, fees, and property administration.
9. Market data, transaction records, price discovery, and information asymmetry.
10. Land assembly, fragmentation, and development economics.

### How the system works
**Rights and restrictions + location and physical characteristics + infrastructure and demand → market evidence and valuation → allocation or transaction → investment/use outcomes → updated market and public records.**

Land value is not a single inherent number. It depends on the interest being valued, date, purpose, market conditions, rights, restrictions, comparable transactions, and assumptions. Asking prices are not the same as completed transaction prices. A map or automated model cannot replace a qualified valuation where one is required.

### Economic activity and opportunity surfaces
- Land-market research and price/transaction data organisation.
- Site and location intelligence for defined buyers.
- Comparable evidence collection and market reporting.
- Property-record workflow support under authorised channels.
- Public allocation, procurement, and policy monitoring.
- Valuation by appropriately qualified professionals.
- Data services for developers, lenders, insurers, investors, and public institutions.

### Constraints and safeguards
- Transaction data may be incomplete, private, stale, or unreliable.
- Asking prices may not represent realised prices.
- Legal interests and restrictions materially affect value.
- Professional valuation and legal advice require appropriate qualifications.
- Land speculation, displacement, corruption, and inequitable allocation are material risks.
- Do not publish sensitive personal/property data without lawful basis.

### Later investigation questions
Who needs reliable land-market information and for what decision? What transaction evidence is accessible and verifiable? How are asking and completed prices distinguished? Which professional standards apply? Is there a recurring payer for better information?

## 13. Cross-branch dependencies

The 12 branches describe different dimensions of a shared land system. Typical dependency chains include:

- **Site selection:** parcel identity → terrain → land cover/ecology → land condition → suitability → rights and planning → infrastructure/site servicing → market and investment decision.
- **Land degradation response:** observation → condition diagnosis → soil/geotechnical or ecological expertise → intervention → stewardship/restoration → repeat measurement and verification.
- **Land information service:** data rights and provenance → acquisition → processing → quality assessment → decision-specific interpretation → user workflow → feedback and update.
- **Land transaction:** parcel identity and survey evidence → legal rights and restrictions → planning status and access → market evidence → qualified valuation and legal due diligence → transaction/registration.

In plain language, no single map or dataset can answer every land question. A site decision often combines physical shape, visible cover, ecological sensitivity, legal rights, infrastructure, and economic evidence. The order of checks depends on the decision and the risks involved.

- **E1 — Initial signal:** one or more sources indicate a programme, problem, dataset, market, or capability, but important facts remain unverified.
- **E2 — Corroborated context:** multiple credible sources support the relevant contextual claim; operational conditions may still be unknown.
- **E3 — Operationally verified:** actual process, data, provider, user, or asset has been examined with sufficient evidence for the stated claim.
- **E4 — Commercially tested:** a defined user/payer has demonstrated demand through a paid pilot, contract, or similarly strong behavioural evidence.

These labels are not a mathematical score. A candidate can have E3 technical evidence and E0 commercial evidence. Always state what the evidence level applies to.

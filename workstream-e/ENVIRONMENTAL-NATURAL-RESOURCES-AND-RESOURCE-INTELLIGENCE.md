# Natural Resources and Resource Intelligence — Remaining Resource Families

**Status:** Initial horizontal mapping supplement documented 2026-11-06. Read together with [Land and Terrestrial Resources](ENVIRONMENTAL-LAND-RESOURCES-AND-LOW-RESOURCE-ENTRY.md), which already provides the initial Level 3 map for land. This supplement broadens territory **3.1** across its other principal natural-resource families. It is a navigation map, not a geological, ecological, hydrological, legal, or market assessment. Detailed vertical exploration remains deferred.

## 1. Purpose and boundary

Natural resources are the naturally occurring stocks, flows, and systems that people and economies depend on, use, manage, conserve, or transform. Resource intelligence concerns understanding their location, quantity, quality, condition, accessibility, rights, constraints, competing uses, and change over time.

This territory maps the resource itself and the intelligence needed to understand it. It does not absorb every downstream industry that uses a resource. For example, forest ecology connects to 3.8, commercial crop production to 3.9, mineral processing to 3.7, water utilities to 3.3, and environmental observation methods to 3.12.

## 2. Principal resource families

### 3.1.2 Forest and forestry resources

- Natural forests, woodland, plantations, community forests, and managed forest landscapes.
- Timber, poles, pulpwood, fuelwood, and other wood resources.
- Non-timber forest products such as fibres, gums, resins, oils, nuts, fruits, medicinal and aromatic plants, and other lawful biological products.
- Forest biomass, residues, harvesting by-products, and resource availability.
- Forest condition, growth, regeneration, degradation, fire exposure, pests, access, tenure, concessions, and management constraints.
- Resource inventories, sustainable-yield assessment, traceability, stewardship, and forest-resource intelligence.

**Boundary:** Ecosystem integrity, habitats, species, conservation, and ecological services are primarily cross-referenced to 3.8. Plantation and agroforestry production may connect to 3.9. Wood processing and engineered timber products connect to 3.7; wood residues and waste streams to 3.2.

### 3.1.3 Biomass and naturally occurring biological feedstocks

- Woody biomass, grasses, agricultural and forestry residues, aquatic biomass, and other naturally sourced organic matter.
- Biomass availability, moisture, composition, density, seasonality, location, collection constraints, and competing uses.
- Wild-harvested biological feedstocks and sustainably managed sources, where lawful.
- Biomass inventories, supply mapping, quality classification, traceability, and sourcing intelligence.
- Resource competition among food, feed, soil protection, ecosystem functions, energy, materials, and industrial uses.

**Boundary:** Cultivated crops, livestock products, and farm-generated production streams primarily connect to 3.9; algae and biological resources to 3.8; waste-derived feedstocks and recovery systems to 3.2; conversion into fuels, materials, or chemicals to 3.4, 3.7, and 3.13. Biomass should not automatically be treated as unused waste: ecological functions, soil cover, animal feed, local livelihoods, and existing uses may have higher value.

### 3.1.4 Freshwater and inland-water resources

- Rivers, streams, lakes, reservoirs, wetlands as water stores, and other inland surface-water bodies.
- Water quantity, flow, seasonal variability, catchment characteristics, storage, recharge interactions, and availability.
- Water quality and natural characteristics relevant to potential uses, including sediment, salinity, nutrients, and contamination pressures.
- Watersheds, catchments, river basins, transboundary flows, and upstream/downstream dependencies.
- Resource access, abstraction rights, allocation, competing uses, ecological flow requirements, and local governance.
- Surface-water resource inventories, basin intelligence, seasonal outlooks, and change monitoring.

**Boundary:** Water abstraction, treatment, distribution, wastewater, and water-service economics belong primarily to 3.3. Aquatic habitats and ecosystem functions connect to 3.8. Flood and drought hazards connect to 3.14. Hydrological measurement and modelling connect to 3.12.

### 3.1.5 Groundwater and subsurface water resources

- Aquifers, shallow and deep groundwater, confined and unconfined systems, springs, and groundwater-dependent ecosystems.
- Aquifer extent, depth, recharge, storage, flow, well yields, seasonal variation, and abstraction sustainability.
- Groundwater quality, natural geochemistry, salinity, mineralisation, and contamination vulnerability.
- Borehole and well inventories, hydrogeological mapping, recharge zones, groundwater-surface-water connections, and monitoring.
- Access rights, drilling constraints, competing abstraction, depletion, and long-term resource security.

**Boundary:** Borehole construction, pumping, treatment, supply networks, and water services connect to 3.3. Groundwater contamination and remediation connect to 3.10. Geological formations and subsurface materials connect to 3.7; geophysical and hydrogeological measurement connects to 3.12.

### 3.1.6 Marine, coastal, and ocean resources

- Ocean water, coastal zones, estuaries, lagoons, tidal systems, and marine environments as resource settings.
- Marine and coastal resource locations, physical conditions, access, seasonal patterns, and competing uses.
- Salt and other naturally occurring marine-derived resources, subject to environmental and legal constraints.
- Marine biomass, seaweed, and other biological resources, cross-referenced to 3.8.
- Offshore and coastal resource areas, seabed information, coastal access, spatial claims, and use conflicts.
- Marine resource intelligence, coastal change information, and resource-use mapping.

**Boundary:** Marine water systems and desalination connect to 3.3; fisheries and aquaculture production to 3.9; marine ecosystems and biodiversity to 3.8; coastal hazards to 3.14; offshore energy to 3.4; seabed minerals to 3.7. Maritime jurisdiction, navigation, and resource rights require careful legal analysis.

### 3.1.7 Fisheries and wild aquatic resources

- Inland capture fisheries, riverine and lake fisheries, coastal fisheries, and marine capture fisheries.
- Wild fish stocks, shellfish, crustaceans, and other aquatic organisms harvested from natural systems.
- Species and stock distribution, abundance, seasonality, migration, habitat dependency, and regeneration.
- Fishing grounds, landing sites, access rights, licences, gear constraints, effort, and traceability.
- Resource sustainability, stock assessment, illegal or unreported extraction, and competing uses of aquatic ecosystems.
- Fisheries resource intelligence, catch and landing data, supply visibility, and stock-change monitoring.

**Boundary:** Farmed fish and aquaculture systems belong primarily to 3.9. Species conservation and aquatic habitat condition connect to 3.8. Seafood processing and trade connect to 3.9 and relevant value-chain territories. Fisheries governance and access rights connect to 3.15.

### 3.1.8 Mineral, geological, and earth-material resources

- Metallic minerals, industrial minerals, gemstones, construction minerals, aggregates, clays, sands, gravels, rocks, and dimension stone.
- Geological formations, mineral occurrences, deposits, ore bodies, reserves and resource classifications, with distinctions between these terms preserved.
- Resource grade, quality, depth, geometry, mineral associations, impurities, overburden, and geological uncertainty.
- Geological mapping, exploration evidence, geophysical and geochemical surveys, sampling, drilling information, and resource estimation.
- Mineral rights, licences, land access, community interests, environmental constraints, infrastructure, and extraction economics.
- Resource databases, prospectivity analysis, deposit intelligence, and supply-risk mapping.

**Boundary:** Extraction, beneficiation, refining, metallurgy, chemical processing, materials design, and product manufacture connect primarily to 3.7. Mine waste and tailings connect to 3.2 and 3.10. Geological hazards and ground engineering connect to 3.6 and 3.14. Resource claims and classifications require competent technical reporting; a mapped occurrence is not automatically an economically recoverable deposit.

### 3.1.9 Other natural resource flows and stocks

- Naturally occurring salts, geothermal resources, and other location-specific resource systems.
- Natural heat, subsurface fluids, and other geophysical resource flows where technically and legally relevant.
- Resource stocks and flows that may not fit neatly into land, water, biomass, or mineral categories.
- Resource interdependencies, replenishment rates, extraction limits, and cumulative effects.

**Boundary:** Energy conversion and supply systems belong primarily to 3.4; materials and industrial applications to 3.7. Include a resource here when the central question concerns its natural occurrence, location, condition, accessibility, and sustainable availability.

## 3. Cross-cutting resource-intelligence dimensions

For each resource family, the later investigation should distinguish:

1. **Occurrence and location:** where the resource exists, its spatial distribution, and the confidence of the evidence.
2. **Quantity and classification:** estimated stock, flow, yield, grade, volume, or abundance; distinguish measured, inferred, modelled, and speculative quantities.
3. **Quality and characteristics:** composition, grade, condition, purity, biological characteristics, physical properties, and suitability for particular uses.
4. **Availability and replenishment:** seasonality, regeneration, recharge, growth, replenishment rates, depletion, and sustainable yield.
5. **Accessibility and infrastructure:** terrain, depth, distance, roads, ports, power, water, storage, processing, and practical access.
6. **Rights and governance:** ownership, tenure, customary rights, licences, concessions, community interests, protected status, and lawful access.
7. **Competing uses and dependencies:** ecological functions, livelihoods, food, water, energy, industrial demand, and downstream users.
8. **Environmental and social constraints:** ecological thresholds, cumulative impacts, contamination, health and safety, community consent, and benefit-sharing.
9. **Economic relevance:** potential uses, specifications, logistics, extraction or collection costs, substitutes, market access, and price exposure.
10. **Change and uncertainty:** climate effects, land-use change, overuse, new discoveries, data gaps, uncertainty, and update frequency.

## 4. Cross-cutting opportunity positions

Potential positions to investigate include resource directories and inventories; public geological, hydrological, forestry, and fisheries data integration; permitted field-data collection; resource-location and access mapping; supplier and rights-holder discovery; quality and availability information; traceability support; change monitoring; and coordination with qualified surveyors, laboratories, ecologists, hydrogeologists, geologists, forestry professionals, and resource operators.

These are possible roles, not validated services. Technical resource estimation, geological reporting, hydrogeological assessment, ecological surveys, fisheries stock assessment, resource certification, and legal rights opinions may require accredited methods, specialist qualifications, statutory authority, or professional sign-off.

The full landscape must retain large-scale opportunities such as forestry management, mineral exploration and extraction, water-resource infrastructure, marine resource development, sustainable fisheries, and industrial biomass supply. In parallel, investigate lower-resource support roles based on public information, data organisation, field assistance with permission, monitoring administration, and coordination with existing qualified providers.

## 5. Questions for later vertical exploration

- Which resource family and geographic system are involved, and what is the actual evidence of occurrence?
- Is the resource a finite stock, renewable stock, replenishing flow, or biological population?
- What is known about quantity and quality, and what classification or uncertainty applies?
- What uses already depend on it, including ecological and community uses that may not appear in formal markets?
- Who holds rights, authority, customary interests, or stewardship responsibilities?
- What infrastructure, permissions, technical work, and capital are needed to access it?
- What are the sustainable-use limits and consequences of extraction or diversion?
- What is the difference between physical occurrence, technically recoverable quantity, legally accessible quantity, and economically viable quantity?
- Who uses or buys the resource, what specifications do they require, and what substitutes exist?
- What new information would materially change a decision, and who would pay for that information?

# Environmental Basic Opportunities — First-Pass Prioritisation and Experiments

**Status:** Working screening plan; hypotheses only, 2026-11-06.  
**Parent:** [Environmental Opportunity Entry Ladder](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITIES-ENTRY-LADDER.md)  
**Project:** Lifeconomist / Workstream E.

## 1. Purpose and limits

The entry ladder contains fifteen Basic opportunities. This document turns that broad list into a practical first-pass screening plan. It does not claim that any opportunity has proven demand, customers, revenue or profitability.

The screening is based on the ability to make a small first attempt without buying equipment or inventory, the possibility of producing a tangible sample, and the ability to test a real customer's willingness to use or pay for the result. The ratings below are provisional judgements, not measured market data.

## 2. First-pass screening of all Basic candidates

Legend:
- **Strong:** relatively straightforward to test with desk research or a small sample.
- **Conditional:** testable, but customer access, trust, specialist review or field permissions may be a meaningful obstacle.
- **Weak first test:** possible, but likely harder to monetise quickly or more exposed to safety, reputational or operational risk.

### Initial test order

**Priority A — make samples and seek customer feedback:** B8, B6, B1, B2, B3 and B14.

**Priority B — explore through interviews or combine with Priority A:** B7, B13, B12, B5 and B9.

**Priority C — do not begin with unsupervised fieldwork or public claims:** B4 and B15. B10 is a method used to test the other candidates; B11 can support credibility and distribution but should not be mistaken for validated service demand.

This is an order for cheap experiments, not a permanent ranking of business potential.

## 3. Five practical experiments

Run these as small tests. Do not build a platform, buy equipment, collect materials or commit to a long project before learning whether a real user has the problem and values the output.

### Experiment 1 — Operational records and weekly reporting for a small operator (B8, B6, B14)

**Hypothesis:** Some small farms or facilities have recurring operational information but lack a simple, dependable way to turn daily records into a useful weekly view.

**Potential user:** A small poultry/farm operator, retailer, water-service operator or facility manager. Start with one type, not all of them.

**First deliverable:** A one-page sample weekly report backed by a simple daily register. For a farm, possible fields include inputs used, production/output, losses, stock balance, water/energy interruptions and maintenance actions. Include only fields the operator can reliably record and use.

**Test sequence:**
1. Ask one operator to describe the last time missing or late information caused a problem.
2. Inspect the current recordkeeping method only with permission.
3. Build a sample using synthetic data first; then adapt it to the operator's actual workflow if invited.
4. Run a short, bounded trial with clear responsibilities for entering data and checking errors.
5. Ask whether the report changed a decision, saved time or prevented a missed action.
6. Ask what the operator would pay for continued maintenance and reporting; do not treat compliments as payment evidence.

**Evidence of progress:** The operator supplies data consistently, uses the report, asks for continuation, refers another operator or pays for a pilot.

**Stop/revise signal:** The operator will not record the minimum data, cannot identify a useful decision, or sees no value beyond a one-off spreadsheet.

**Boundary:** This is records and reporting support, not veterinary, agronomic, engineering or compliance advice.

### Experiment 2 — Narrow environmental supplier/provider intelligence (B2, B13)

**Hypothesis:** A buyer or operator loses time because relevant providers are difficult to identify and compare.

**Potential user:** One defined buyer group, such as a small business seeking waste collection, a farm seeking a water service, or a facility seeking solar maintenance. Choose only one category and one geography for the test.

**First deliverable:** A shortlist of approximately five to ten candidate providers, with service area, service offered, contact route, evidence/source, date checked, confirmation status and unresolved questions. The count is a working sample size, not a market-coverage claim.

**Test sequence:**
1. Interview one prospective buyer about the exact service they need and their selection criteria.
2. Identify candidate providers from lawful public sources.
3. Contact providers to verify current service, location, capacity and terms where possible.
4. Label every record as confirmed, provider-stated but not independently verified, or unverified.
5. Ask the buyer to assess the shortlist and identify whether it changes their next step.
6. Test a paid research/shortlisting task before considering lead fees or a marketplace.

**Evidence of progress:** A buyer requests an updated shortlist, asks for introductions with consent, uses a provider, or pays for sourcing support.

**Stop/revise signal:** Buyers already have reliable sources, the providers are not responsive, or the information is too easy to obtain without assistance.

**Boundary:** Do not imply endorsement, guarantee provider quality or act as an agent without clear authority and disclosed terms.

### Experiment 3 — Project and tender intelligence for one supplier category (B3, B1)

**Hypothesis:** A defined group of environmental suppliers or contractors misses relevant opportunities because notices are fragmented or difficult to screen.

**Potential user:** One category only—for example, water-system contractors, waste-service providers, solar installers or environmental consultants.

**First deliverable:** A dated opportunity tracker with issuer, title, location, scope, eligibility, deadline, source link, last-checked date and status. Include only notices that can be traced to an authoritative source.

**Test sequence:**
1. Ask two or three businesses in the same supplier category how they currently find work.
2. Learn what makes a notice relevant or irrelevant to them.
3. Build a small sample tracker from official procurement/project sources.
4. Verify each live deadline and eligibility condition at the issuing source.
5. Show the sample and ask which entries they would actually pursue.
6. Offer a limited monitoring period or one-off research task; seek payment evidence.

**Evidence of progress:** A business uses an alert to take a concrete action, requests a recurring service or pays for monitoring.

**Stop/revise signal:** The opportunities are not relevant, the customer already has an effective alert system, or the notice quality is too poor to support decisions.

**Boundary:** A notice is not proof of available funds, award probability or eligibility. Do not submit bids or represent a business without authority.

### Experiment 4 — Waste/by-product buyer research without handling material (B7, B2, B13)

**Hypothesis:** A generator has a potentially useful by-product but lacks reliable information about specifications and possible buyers.

**Potential user:** One generator of a clearly described non-hazardous material, or one buyer looking for a particular input. Do not start with mixed or hazardous waste.

**First deliverable:** A material-and-market note recording the generator's description, approximate volume/frequency, contamination or quality constraints, location, possible buyer specifications, price basis if verified, transport responsibility and unknowns.

**Test sequence:**
1. Interview a generator about the material, how it is currently handled and whether there is a recurring cost or operational problem.
2. Interview potential buyers about exact specifications, minimum quantities, quality tests and delivery terms.
3. Compare the generator's information with buyer requirements; do not assume a match.
4. Identify transport, storage, legal classification and permission questions before any movement.
5. Ask whether the research itself is useful enough to pay for, even if no transaction occurs.

**Evidence of progress:** A buyer confirms a specification match, the generator authorises a next step, and the parties agree who bears transport/quality risk—or one party pays for the research independently of a transaction.

**Stop/revise signal:** Volumes are inconsistent, contamination is unknown, transport costs exceed value, or there is no lawful route.

**Boundary:** No collection, storage, transport, brokerage of controlled material or handling of hazardous waste in this first experiment.

### Experiment 5 — Public environmental intelligence brief (B1, B9, B12)

**Hypothesis:** A specific operator needs a recurring environmental or infrastructure question answered from scattered public information.

**Potential user:** One SME category, contractor type, farm operator, consultant or service provider.

**First deliverable:** A concise brief answering one decision-oriented question. Examples: which public projects are advancing in a defined service area; which official rule or programme changed; which facilities or service providers are publicly documented; or what public information is available about a local environmental service gap.

**Test sequence:**
1. Ask the user what decision they are trying to make and what information they currently lack.
2. Agree the scope, geography, date range and what the brief will not claim.
3. Use primary/official sources where available; retain source links and publication/check dates.
4. Separate confirmed facts, provider statements, estimates, interpretations and unknowns.
5. Deliver a short sample and ask the user to identify one action it enabled.
6. Offer a fixed-scope follow-up or periodic update only if the first output is useful.

**Evidence of progress:** The user changes a decision, commissions another brief, shares the work internally or pays for a follow-up.

**Stop/revise signal:** The information is not decision-relevant, the user can obtain it easily, or no budget owner can be identified.

**Boundary:** Research intelligence is not legal advice, technical certification, environmental measurement or a guarantee of project status.

## 4. A common validation record

For every interview or experiment, record:

- Date and geography.
- User type and role of the person interviewed (avoid unnecessary personal data).
- Problem described in the user's own words.
- How often it occurs and what happens when it does.
- Current workaround/provider and what it costs in money, time or disruption.
- Who decides and who pays.
- Evidence provided (document, actual workflow, transaction, repeated use, or statement).
- Sample shown and response.
- Requested next step.
- Payment evidence: none, stated willingness, quote requested, paid pilot, repeat purchase.
- Permissions, data constraints, safety and professional boundaries.
- What would disprove the hypothesis.

Keep **observations**, **user statements**, **documentary evidence**, **inferences** and **decisions** separate. A positive interview is not proof of demand. A paid pilot is stronger evidence, and repeat payment/use is stronger still.

## 5. Suggested working cadence

1. Select one experiment, not all five at once.
2. Prepare a sample deliverable before extensive research.
3. Speak with a small number of relevant users and document what they actually do today.
4. Revise the offer based on evidence.
5. Seek one bounded pilot with explicit scope, fee (if any), responsibilities and data permissions.
6. Review the evidence and decide: continue, change customer, change deliverable, partner, pause or stop.

A useful initial learning cycle is short and reversible. The goal is not to force a business idea to work; it is to discover which real problem is worth solving and what role can be entered responsibly.

## 6. Initial recommendation

Start by testing **Experiment 1 (operational records/reporting)** and **Experiment 2 (provider intelligence)** as the first two candidate paths. They can produce concrete samples without owning environmental assets, and they test two different value propositions: helping an operator manage its own activity, and helping a buyer navigate an external provider market.

Keep Experiments 3–5 ready as adjacent tests. Do not declare a winner until actual users, workflows and payment behaviour provide evidence.

The user's prior practical exposure to farm reporting can make Experiment 1 a useful starting hypothesis, but it does not establish that another operator will buy the service. Treat any existing familiarity as a way to ask better questions, not as proof of demand.

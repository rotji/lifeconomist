# Agriculture Economy and Opportunities
## A broad exploration domain within Workstream E

**Status:** Initial scope framework documented on 2026-11-07. The domain map is provisional and intentionally open; it is not an exhaustive agricultural taxonomy, a validated market map, or a selection of approved businesses.

## 1. Purpose and governing question

Explore the full range of economic activity and opportunities connected to agriculture and the wider systems that enable food, fibre, feed, biological products and other agricultural outputs to be produced, financed, processed, moved, traded, measured and consumed.

The governing question is:

> Across agricultural production and the systems on which it depends—from land, water, biological inputs and farm operations to processing, logistics, markets, finance, technology and final customers—what needs, resources, activities, products, services, markets and economic relationships could support useful and viable opportunities, and what evidence would justify acting on them?

This is deliberately broader than farming alone. Agriculture is not only the activity of growing crops or raising animals. It is an interconnected economy of inputs, production, services, infrastructure, knowledge, finance, aggregation, processing, distribution, trade, institutions and consumption. Value may be created before production, on the farm, between the farm and buyer, during processing, or through services and information that improve the performance of the system.

The map should remain open to activities and opportunities not anticipated in this first version.

## 2. Position within Lifeconomist

This is an application domain within **Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application**. It is not a new workstream and does not replace or redefine Workstreams A–D.

- **A — Deep Conceptual Foundations:** explain agricultural production systems, biological and physical processes, markets, incentives, value chains and economic mechanisms.
- **B — Measurement:** establish what can be measured, how it can be measured, and the limitations of production, yield, productivity, price, cost, loss, trade and other agricultural indicators.
- **C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** monitor agricultural production conditions, prices, supply, demand, weather, policy, trade, disease events and market developments.
- **D — Footprint Intelligence:** investigate observable farms, facilities, assets, inputs, logistics, corporate activity, land-use changes, supply chains and other agricultural footprints.
- **E — Opportunity Intelligence:** test whether findings can support a viable trade, investment, business, service, product, or intelligence opportunity.

The relationship is concurrent and bidirectional. An agricultural opportunity may begin with a farm problem, input shortage, market price, processing gap, logistics constraint, technology, buyer requirement, public programme, scientific development or observed change. Questions that cannot be answered within E should be routed to the appropriate A–D capability, then brought back into the opportunity assessment when useful.

## 3. Scope: a broad, provisional domain map

The following territories are an initial navigation structure. They are not mutually exclusive: one opportunity may span several territories, and future discoveries may justify adding, splitting, merging or renaming areas. Do not treat this list as a closed taxonomy.

### 3.1 Land, tenure, farm sites and agricultural resource access
- Farmland availability, ownership, leasing, customary tenure, use rights, access and land transactions.
- Farm location, size, fragmentation, boundaries, terrain, access roads, proximity to water, markets and services.
- Land suitability, land-use planning, farm consolidation, site selection, land preparation and development.
- Land records, due diligence, mapping, valuation support and farm-site intelligence.
- Access arrangements, community rights, conflicts, safeguards and the practical difference between nominal ownership and secure operational access.

### 3.2 Soil, fertility, nutrients and growing media
- Soil types, structure, organic matter, fertility, pH, salinity, compaction, erosion and degradation.
- Soil testing, sampling logistics, interpretation, mapping, monitoring and soil-health services.
- Fertilisers, lime, compost, manure, biochar, micronutrients, inoculants and other soil amendments.
- Nutrient management, precision application, blending, distribution and quality assurance.
- Growing media, substrates, soilless cultivation inputs and alternatives to conventional soil.
- Soil restoration and nutrient circularity, with performance and environmental claims independently assessed.

### 3.3 Water, irrigation, drainage and farm water systems
- Rain-fed production, irrigation sources, abstraction, storage, pumping, conveyance and application.
- Drip, sprinkler, centre-pivot, solar pumping and other irrigation systems.
- Drainage, waterlogging control, watershed dependencies, farm ponds and rainwater harvesting.
- Water quality, salinity, treatment, reuse, metering, leak detection and water productivity.
- Irrigation design, installation, maintenance, scheduling, monitoring and water-service businesses.
- Water rights, access, competing uses and seasonal reliability.

### 3.4 Seeds, planting materials, genetics and crop protection
- Seeds, seedlings, tubers, cuttings, planting materials, nurseries and propagation systems.
- Crop breeding, genetics, varietal development, seed multiplication, certification and distribution.
- Germination, seed quality, storage, treatment, packaging and traceability.
- Crop protection: pest, disease and weed management; biological and chemical controls; integrated pest management.
- Diagnostic services, scouting, advisory, application services and resistance management.
- Intellectual property, licensing, local adaptation, farmer access and input authenticity.

### 3.5 Fertilisers, crop nutrition and agricultural input markets
- Mineral fertilisers, organic fertilisers, blended products, micronutrients and speciality nutrients.
- Production, importation, formulation, blending, packaging, distribution and retail.
- Input procurement, bulk purchasing, dealer networks, stock availability and last-mile delivery.
- Product quality, adulteration, pricing, credit, seasonal demand and inventory management.
- Nutrient-use efficiency, application services, soil-test-linked recommendations and alternative nutrient sources.
- Regulation, standards, product registration and safe handling.

### 3.6 Crop production and field operations
- Cereals, legumes, roots and tubers, oilseeds, pulses, cash crops, fibre crops and industrial crops.
- Horticulture, vegetables, fruits, spices, flowers, seed production and protected cultivation.
- Planting, land preparation, crop establishment, weeding, spraying, fertilisation, irrigation, harvesting and field labour.
- Production systems: smallholder, commercial, contract, outgrower, cooperative, estate and mixed farming.
- Crop rotation, intercropping, conservation agriculture, mechanised and precision production.
- Farm planning, input scheduling, labour management, yield improvement and operational records.

### 3.7 Livestock, poultry and animal production
- Poultry, cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, rabbits and other farmed terrestrial animals.
- Breeding, genetics, hatcheries, day-old chicks, replacement stock and animal sourcing.
- Housing, feeding, nutrition, water, welfare, husbandry, biosecurity and production systems.
- Veterinary care, vaccination, diagnostics, disease surveillance and animal health products.
- Meat, eggs, milk, hides, skins, manure and other outputs and by-products.
- Farm management, production records, mortality control, feed conversion, performance monitoring and contract production.
- Animal movement, traceability, slaughter, processing and product safety.

### 3.8 Fisheries, aquaculture and aquatic food production
- Capture fisheries and their relationship to agricultural and food markets.
- Fish farming, ponds, cages, tanks, recirculating systems, hatcheries and fingerling supply.
- Feed, water quality, aeration, stocking, disease management, harvesting and farm operations.
- Aquatic species, genetics, breeding, processing, cold chain and distribution.
- Equipment, pond construction, water testing, advisory, finance and technical services.
- Resource sustainability, water rights, biosecurity and ecological safeguards.

### 3.9 Farm machinery, equipment, tools and mechanisation services
- Tractors, planters, harvesters, threshers, shellers, mills, sprayers, pumps and implements.

### 3.10 Labour, skills, advisory and farm-management services

- Recruitment, skills matching, outsourced farm management and specialist subcontracting.

### 3.11 Harvesting, aggregation, storage and post-harvest systems

### 3.14 Agricultural markets, pricing, procurement and commercial relationships

### 3.16 Agricultural technology, data, precision agriculture and digital services

### 3.17 Agricultural waste, residues, circularity and resource recovery

### 3.19 Food systems, consumption, nutrition and demand interfaces

### 3.20 Agricultural risk, resilience, climate and biosecurity

- Monitoring, risk intelligence, insurance support, emergency input/logistics and continuity planning.
- Separate hazard, exposure, vulnerability, impact, response and recovery; do not present forecasts as certainties.

## 4. Cross-cutting value-chain and opportunity positions

Agricultural value is not created only by owning and operating a farm. Investigate opportunities across the full system, including:

- **Input supply:** sourcing, manufacture, importation, distribution, retail, quality assurance and delivery of seeds, feed, fertiliser, chemicals, equipment and other inputs.
- **Production:** farm ownership, leasing, contract production, outgrower systems, farm management, specialist production and custom-hire operations.
- **Services:** agronomy, veterinary, testing, machinery hire, repair, irrigation, labour, training, recordkeeping and operational support.
- **Aggregation and quality:** collection, bulking, grading, sorting, weighing, storage, traceability and quality assurance.
- **Processing and manufacturing:** primary processing, food production, feed, ingredients, packaging, equipment and bio-based outputs.
- **Logistics and market access:** transport, cold chain, procurement, buyer matching, wholesale, retail, exports and fulfilment.
- **Finance and risk:** credit, insurance, guarantees, payments, investment, working capital and risk management.
- **Information and intelligence:** price, supplier, buyer, production, weather, policy, risk, location and supply-chain intelligence.
- **Technology and infrastructure:** software, sensors, data, connectivity, facilities, energy, water and shared assets.
- **Coordination and integration:** connect existing providers and stages to deliver a complete customer outcome where fragmentation creates a real problem.

Possible roles include producer, input distributor, dealer, service provider, machinery contractor, aggregator, processor, transporter, broker, trader, exporter, integrator, software provider, data/intelligence provider, project coordinator, financier, insurer, maintenance provider, implementation partner and specialist subcontractor. These are discovery categories, not prescribed business models or claims that each is accessible or viable.

## 5. Capital-light and access-aware discovery

The exploration must include opportunities that do not require the founder to own farmland, livestock, machinery, a warehouse, a processing plant, a fleet or expensive technology. Capital-light entry is an important discovery lens, not a permanent restriction on the domain.

Potential entry positions to investigate include:
- Farm, input, buyer, supplier, processor and service-provider research.
- Price, market, tender, policy, weather and supply intelligence.
- Customer discovery and demand aggregation before buying inputs or assets.
- Recordkeeping, inventory, production reporting and administrative support.
- Sourcing, procurement coordination, order aggregation and disclosed brokerage.
- Contract farming or production coordination where contracts, risks and responsibilities are clear.
- Machinery hire coordination and maintenance coordination using existing owners/operators.
- Contract processing, shared facilities, toll processing and third-party logistics.
- Sampling logistics, data collection, mapping, monitoring and reporting support.
- Digital implementation, training and managed services using existing tools.
- Small bounded pilots before committing to land, livestock, inventory, equipment or facilities.

For each idea, state the minimum viable role, what the operator must own or control, what can be outsourced or partnered, what permissions and competence are required, what must be paid upfront, and what can be tested before investment. A theoretical capital-light route is not practical until customer access, counterparties, trust, delivery responsibility, liability and unit economics are demonstrated.

## 6. End-to-end opportunity exploration

For each promising customer problem, examine the complete outcome the customer needs and the stages required to deliver it. Identify existing farms, input suppliers, technical specialists, machinery owners, laboratories, processors, warehouses, transporters, buyers, platforms, finance providers and other service operators that may already supply parts of the solution.

Ask:
1. Who is the customer and what outcome do they need?
2. Which stages, inputs, decisions and hand-offs are required?
3. Which providers already perform each stage, and are they available on workable terms?
4. Where is the actual gap, friction, cost, delay, quality problem or unmet need?
5. Could the opportunity be a specialist service, coordination role, integration, distribution, data product or partnership rather than asset ownership?
6. Who is responsible for quality, safety, loss, spoilage, payment, compliance and customer support?
7. Does the complete offer improve the customer's outcome and leave viable economics?

Do not assume that integrating every stage is better than serving one stage well. Existing providers are potential capabilities, not automatic partners; availability, permission, terms, quality and reliability must be verified.

## 7. Evaluation and evidence requirements

Use the existing Workstream E opportunity lifecycle and prioritisation principles. Add agricultural checks proportionate to the activity:

- **Production and biological feasibility:** species/variety, genetics, climate, season, soil, water, feed, husbandry, disease, yield, cycle length and biological variability.
- **Input and supply feasibility:** quality, authenticity, availability, seasonality, minimum order, lead times, storage, working capital and supplier concentration.
- **Operational feasibility:** labour, skills, machinery, utilities, maintenance, biosecurity, recordkeeping, capacity and downtime.
- **Post-harvest and quality:** grading, moisture, contamination, shelf life, storage losses, packaging, food safety and buyer specifications.
- **Market evidence:** identifiable buyers, actual purchasing volumes, grades, delivery terms, price basis, payment reliability, substitutes, competition and repeat demand.
- **Logistics:** farm-to-market distance, road access, transport cost, cold chain, handling, spoilage, aggregation density and delivery reliability.
- **Financial feasibility:** capital expenditure, operating expenditure, seasonal cash flow, working capital, utilisation, margins, financing cost, losses and downside scenarios.
- **Legal and institutional feasibility:** land and water rights, input/product registration, veterinary and food safety requirements, labour, environmental obligations, trade rules and contracts.
- **Risk and resilience:** weather, pests, diseases, theft, price volatility, counterparty default, infrastructure outages and correlated shocks.
- **Distributional and social effects:** farmer bargaining power, labour conditions, community rights, inclusion, benefit distribution and potential displacement.
- **Evidence quality:** provenance, date, observation period, sample, independence, method, uncertainty, conflicts of interest and whether claims have been independently tested.

Distinguish research findings, demonstration plots, controlled trials, farm trials, commercial operating performance, verified deliveries, purchase orders, completed transactions, repeat purchases and audited financial results. They are different levels of evidence. A product demonstration, supplier claim or announced programme is a lead for investigation, not proof of adoption, yield improvement or commercial viability.

## 8. Geography and market scope

The domain is global. Nigeria may be an initial practical test context when assessing crops, livestock, input systems, production economics, markets, infrastructure, regulation, finance, logistics and access. Do not assume that a production model or technology proven elsewhere transfers directly to Nigeria. Equally, do not limit discovery to Nigeria when relevant genetics, equipment, markets, buyers, technologies, suppliers or capital exist elsewhere.

For each opportunity, distinguish:
- Where production or the agricultural problem occurs.
- Where inputs, technology, equipment, skills and finance are available.
- Where processing capacity and buyers are located.
- Where the product or service can be sold and delivered.
- Which jurisdiction's land, water, food safety, veterinary, environmental, labour and trade rules apply.
- Who bears biological, price, logistics, credit and delivery risk.

## 9. Boundary and overlap rules

Agriculture is interconnected with, but not identical to, the environmental economy, food retail, manufacturing, finance and general logistics.

1. **Managed production versus wild ecosystems:** cultivated crops, farmed livestock and aquaculture belong primarily here. Wild biodiversity, habitats and ecosystem functions belong primarily in the environmental map, with cross-references where agriculture depends on them.
2. **Agricultural residues versus general waste systems:** residues arising from agricultural production and processing belong here when the main question is their generation, farm use or agricultural value chain. General collection, recycling and waste infrastructure may belong in the environmental domain.
3. **Farm water use versus general water services:** irrigation and farm water management belong here; wider water utilities and treatment systems may be cross-referenced to the environmental water territory.
4. **Agricultural production versus downstream food retail:** production and agricultural value chains belong here. Consumer-facing retail, hospitality and food service are included as demand interfaces, but a distinct retail business may warrant a separate food/consumer-market investigation.
5. **Farm assets versus finance:** agricultural assets and operating economics are examined here; financial products and institutions may also be investigated through the project's wider finance and capital-flow domains.
6. **Farm data versus general intelligence infrastructure:** agricultural use cases belong here; reusable sensing, GIS, analytics and data infrastructure may be cross-referenced to other intelligence capabilities.
7. **Agricultural processing versus general manufacturing:** processing of agricultural outputs belongs here as an agricultural value-chain activity; general-purpose machinery, chemicals, packaging or industrial manufacturing may have a separate primary home.

These are filing conventions, not claims that the systems operate independently. A single real-world opportunity may span several domains. Choose one primary home for the main question and use explicit cross-references for dependencies.

## 10. Working rules

1. **Keep the domain open:** do not narrow agricultural exploration to crop farming, livestock, poultry, mechanisation, or any single commodity.
2. **Explore the full value chain:** examine inputs, production, services, aggregation, storage, processing, logistics, markets, finance, data, institutions and final demand.
3. **Do not equate production with opportunity:** a crop, animal or technology needs a customer, reliable operating conditions, quality, market access and viable economics.
4. **Do not assume higher yield means higher profit:** include input costs, labour, losses, financing, prices, quality and risk.
5. **Do not assume demand from a market price alone:** verify buyer volumes, specifications, delivered prices, payment terms and actual transactions.
6. **Do not equate a supplier claim with performance:** seek independent and context-relevant evidence for yield, feed conversion, disease control, storage life, savings and other outcomes.
7. **Keep capital-light routes visible:** investigate services, coordination, distribution, partnerships, shared assets and contract processing before assuming ownership is required.
8. **Apply end-to-end thinking:** examine the complete customer outcome and existing capabilities, but do not force vertical integration.
9. **Use evidence proportionately:** match technical, biological, commercial, financial, legal, food-safety and environmental verification to the risk and scale.
10. **Preserve uncertainty:** mark ideas as observations, hypotheses, leads, tested findings or decisions; do not silently upgrade their status.
11. **Respect safety and professional boundaries:** do not provide veterinary, pesticide, engineering, food-safety or other regulated specialist services without appropriate competence and authorisation.
12. **Follow the project-wide Continuity, Pause, and Revision Principle:** inspect existing records before starting; do not repeat completed exploration; revisit earlier findings only when new evidence or a concrete dependency justifies it, and update only affected records.

## 11. Horizontal-first exploration sequence

**Operating decision (2026-11-07):** Agriculture Economy and Opportunities will initially progress horizontally across the major territories before descending deeply into individual branches.

- First, establish a useful broad map of each major territory at a comparable level of detail. Identify principal subcategories, systems, value-chain positions, technologies, users, markets and possible opportunity forms without attempting to exhaust every branch.
- Then move to the next major territory. Do not keep descending one territory to its smallest units while other major territories remain unexplored at the initial mapping level.
- Once the horizontal survey has covered the major territories, return for vertical exploration: descend into selected subcategories and smaller economic activity/opportunity units, guided by discoveries, evidence, dependencies and the user's choices.
- Surface an opportunity immediately when it appears. State what is known, what remains unverified and what a realistic entry route might require. The user may pause the survey to investigate it; otherwise record it as a lead and continue horizontally.
- Preserve each territory's stopping point so later vertical exploration resumes where it stopped rather than repeating its initial mapping.

This is a deliberate breadth-before-depth method, not a claim that every territory must receive identical effort or that the horizontal survey must be completed before responding to an urgent, unusually strong or user-selected opportunity. The user controls when to pause, deepen or change direction.

## 12. Initial status and next useful work

This document establishes a broad exploration frame only. It does not establish that the listed territories are exhaustive, that any commodity, technology, market or business is attractive, or that any activity should be started.

The next useful work is a horizontal survey across the mapped territories at comparable first-pass depth, preserving discoveries and stopping points. During that survey, surface practical opportunities and possible entry roles without prematurely narrowing the domain. After broad coverage, use the evidence and the user's choices to select vertical investigations.

# Environmental Economy — Land and Terrestrial Resources: First Decomposition

**Status:** Initial taxonomy and practical-entry framework, 2026-11-06. This is a working map, not a validated market assessment or recommendation to start a particular business.

**Parent map:** [Environmental Opportunity Intelligence](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-INTELLIGENCE.md)

## 1. Why this document exists

The environmental economy is being explored recursively: major territory → subcategory → finer subcategory → smallest useful economic activity or opportunity unit. We will move down one branch at a time, preserve the stopping point, and avoid repeating completed branches.

The first branch selected is **1.1 Land and terrestrial resources**.

A second question now applies throughout the exploration: how could someone enter or contribute to an environmental-economy field when they have little or no startup money, established network, specialist equipment, or institutional backing?

This is not a reason to narrow the environmental economy to only tiny businesses. It is a practical access lens: discover the least resource-intensive credible way to learn, contribute, test demand, and gain access to more capable partners or infrastructure. Keep larger opportunities visible for later.

## 2. Scope of land and terrestrial resources

For this branch, land is treated as a physical, spatial and ecological asset: its location, area, shape, terrain, surface condition, land cover, uses, constraints, access and relationships to surrounding systems.

This branch is not intended to absorb every subject that happens to occur on land. To reduce duplication:

- Detailed soil properties, fertility, contamination, erosion and soil treatment belong primarily under **Soil, Land and Geotechnical Systems**.
- Forest products and forestry operations belong primarily under **Forest and Forestry Resources**.
- Mineral deposits, mineral rights, extraction and mineral processing belong primarily under **Mineral and Geological Resources** and **Materials, Minerals, Chemicals and Advanced Materials**.
- Crop and livestock production belong primarily under **Agriculture, Food and Biological Production Systems**.
- Species, habitats and ecosystem functions belong primarily under **Biodiversity, Ecosystems and Biological Resources**.
- Mapping, remote sensing, surveying, land records and change detection may be cross-cutting capabilities under **Environmental Intelligence, Measurement, Monitoring and Verification**.

These are primary filing locations, not walls between connected subjects. A land-use question may require soil, water, biodiversity, geology, agriculture, property rights and intelligence evidence together.

## 3. Level 3 — Immediate subcategories of 1.1

The following is a provisional decomposition of **1.1 Land and Terrestrial Resources**. It identifies different dimensions of land rather than claiming that each is a separate market.

### 1.1.1 Land as a spatial and physical asset
Land parcels, plots, sites, area, boundaries, location, shape, adjacency, fragmentation and spatial configuration. Relevant questions include where land is, how much exists, how parcels connect, and what physical constraints affect its use.

### 1.1.2 Terrain, topography and landforms
Elevation, slope, aspect, valleys, plains, plateaus, hills, mountains, escarpments, depressions, ravines and other landforms. Terrain affects access, construction, drainage, erosion risk, agriculture, infrastructure and hazard exposure.

### 1.1.3 Surface cover and terrestrial land cover
Bare ground, vegetation cover, grassland, shrubland, woodland, forest cover, wetlands, cultivated surfaces, built-up areas, exposed rock and other surface-cover classes. This is about what physically covers the land; the detailed economic activity associated with each cover type may sit in another territory.

### 1.1.4 Land use and land-use systems
Residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, conservation, recreational, transport, mining, infrastructure and mixed-use land. Distinguish **land cover** (what is physically present) from **land use** (what people do with or designate the land for).

### 1.1.5 Terrestrial ecosystems and ecological landscapes
Connected terrestrial ecological areas, landscape mosaics, ecological corridors, habitat patches and land-based ecosystem functions. Detailed species and ecosystem analysis belongs primarily in the biodiversity territory, but landscape condition and connectivity are relevant here.

### 1.1.6 Land condition, disturbance and physical constraints
Physical instability, surface disturbance, compaction, erosion features, subsidence, landslide exposure, desertification, degradation, excavation, abandoned sites and other conditions affecting land capability. Detailed soil diagnosis and remediation are cross-referenced to their specialist territories.

### 1.1.7 Land availability, suitability and competing uses
Whether land is physically, legally, ecologically and practically suitable for a specified use; competing demands; exclusion zones; buffers; access constraints; and trade-offs between uses. Suitability is purpose-specific: land suitable for one use may be unsuitable for another.

### 1.1.8 Land access, tenure, rights and restrictions
Ownership and occupancy, customary and statutory tenure, leases, easements, licences, community rights, public land, protected status, access permissions, disputes and restrictions. Legal status must be checked in the relevant jurisdiction; a map or public record is not proof that land is available to use.

### 1.1.9 Land development, preparation and site servicing
Site selection, clearing where lawful, grading, earthworks, drainage, access roads, utilities, site preparation, rehabilitation obligations and ongoing site management. These activities can create environmental impacts and require permits, competent operators and safeguards.

### 1.1.10 Land observation, mapping and change intelligence
Parcel and boundary mapping, terrain models, land-cover classification, land-use mapping, site inventories, change detection, field verification and land-condition records. This is a capability layer that overlaps strongly with environmental intelligence and geospatial services.

### 1.1.11 Land stewardship, conservation and restoration
Land protection, sustainable management, rehabilitation, ecological restoration, erosion prevention, landscape connectivity, conservation agreements and long-term monitoring. Claims of restoration or improved land condition require a baseline, measurable outcomes, rights-holder engagement and follow-up evidence.

### 1.1.12 Land markets, valuation and allocation
Land transactions, rents, site valuation, land assembly, public allocation, concessions and the economic effects of location, access, infrastructure, restrictions and competing uses. Valuation is not simply a physical measurement; it depends on rights, market evidence, intended use and legal context.

## 4. How to interpret the hierarchy

The subcategories are analytical doors into the same physical landscape. They can overlap. For example, a proposed industrial site may require parcel and tenure checks (1.1.1 and 1.1.8), terrain analysis (1.1.2), land-cover and ecological review (1.1.3 and 1.1.5), suitability assessment (1.1.7), and site preparation analysis (1.1.9).

Do not force a single label where the real-world system is interconnected. Keep one primary location in the taxonomy and add cross-references to other relevant branches.

**Stopping point:** Level 3 for 1.1 is outlined. No Level 4 decomposition has yet been completed. The next taxonomy action is to select one of these subcategories and descend one level, not to jump to unrelated branches.

## 5. A practical access lens for someone starting with limited resources

### 5.1 The resource inventory is wider than money

Before deciding that an opportunity is inaccessible, inventory all resources that may be available, including:

- **Time:** hours available each week and ability to work consistently.
- **Knowledge:** what is already understood, what can be learned independently, and what requires formal training.
- **Digital tools:** phone, laptop, internet access, spreadsheet, maps, public datasets, open-source software and basic documentation tools.
- **Physical tools:** ordinary measuring tools, protective equipment, transport access, storage or workspace, if available.
- **Skills:** observation, writing, research, communication, sales, record-keeping, photography, mapping, data cleaning, coordination, repair or practical work.
- **Place-based access:** ability to observe a site, market, waste stream, water point, farm, business, road, facility or local problem without trespassing or violating privacy.
- **Credibility and trust:** reliability, clear records, honest communication, references earned through small completed tasks and transparent limits.
- **Relationships and proximity:** people or organisations that can answer questions, provide supervised access, refer work or supply specialist services. A large network is not assumed; one legitimate contact can be a starting point.
- **Existing infrastructure:** facilities, laboratories, equipment owners, collection routes, transporters, local associations, universities, public agencies and service providers that may be accessible through permission or paid service.
- **Learning capacity:** ability to test a small task, accept correction, document results and improve.

A resource only counts as available when access is real. For example, knowing that a laboratory exists does not mean its equipment or results are free to use.

### 5.2 A ladder of possible entry positions

These are routes to investigate, not guaranteed jobs or business models. A person may start at one rung and later move, combine roles or stop.

1. **Observe and document:** identify a specific local environmental condition, recurring problem, asset, material flow or service gap. Record what is seen, when, where, and what remains unknown.
2. **Learn and verify:** use public information, open tools, field observation with permission and conversations with relevant people to understand the issue. Do not present unverified observations as technical assessments.
3. **Create a useful small information output:** prepare a site or supplier directory, simple inventory, map, price/availability record, change log, buyer/supplier list, or concise research note where a real user needs it.
4. **Perform a bounded support task:** where competent and permitted, help with record-keeping, data collection, basic mapping, reporting, customer research, scheduling, documentation, sourcing or administrative coordination.
5. **Connect existing providers:** help a customer find and coordinate a qualified operator, laboratory, transporter, recycler, installer or other provider. Confirm consent, terms, competence, liability and who is responsible for delivery.
6. **Run a small service pilot:** agree on a clearly defined deliverable, scope, price or other lawful compensation, timeframe, acceptance criteria and limits before doing the work. Start manually if that is the lowest-risk way to test demand.
7. **Coordinate a multi-provider outcome:** only after understanding the customer need and provider dependencies, combine existing capabilities into a more complete service. Do not promise control over providers or outcomes that cannot be guaranteed.
8. **Invest in assets or specialist capability:** consider equipment, premises, staff, certification, software or facilities only when evidence shows that ownership is better than renting, contracting, partnering or remaining a specialist/coordinator.

The order is a useful learning ladder, not a mandatory progression. A person may enter through an existing skill or a supervised paid task rather than start at rung one.

### 5.3 Low-resource opportunity forms to examine across the environmental economy

- **Information and research:** directories, inventories, price and availability checks, supplier discovery, regulatory-change summaries and customer/problem research.
- **Field observation and data support:** permitted site observations, photo logs, basic measurements within competence, geotagged records, survey assistance and change documentation.
- **Administrative and operational support:** scheduling, job tracking, inventory records, collection records, maintenance logs, reporting and customer follow-up.
- **Sourcing and coordination:** locating qualified service providers, buyers, feedstock suppliers, equipment, transport or testing capacity.
- **Distribution and representation:** helping an existing provider reach a new customer segment, subject to a clear agreement and truthful claims.
- **Digital support:** data cleaning, spreadsheets, simple maps, public-data analysis, documentation and basic workflow setup.
- **Training and communication support:** explaining verified procedures, preparing records or supporting awareness work within actual competence.
- **Supervised technical assistance:** assisting a qualified professional or operator rather than independently performing regulated, hazardous or specialist work.

A small information product or coordination service is not automatically commercially valuable. Each must solve a real problem for an identifiable user who is willing and able to pay, or have another clearly evidenced route to support.

### 5.4 A minimum opportunity test

For each candidate entry route, answer:

1. **Who has the problem?** Name the actual user, customer, operator or institution.
2. **What is the problem or task?** Describe a concrete deliverable or outcome, not a broad aspiration.
3. **What is currently done?** Identify existing providers, alternatives, informal workarounds and why they may be insufficient.
4. **What can I credibly do now?** Separate current competence from skills that must be learned or supplied by a qualified partner.
5. **What resources are truly required?** Include time, data, transport, connectivity, tools, permissions, trust, working capital and specialist access—not just equipment price.
6. **What can be borrowed, rented, contracted or accessed?** Verify actual availability, terms and permission; do not assume free access.
7. **Who pays or otherwise supports the work?** Identify the payer, purchasing process, budget, price evidence and payment timing.
8. **What could go wrong?** Consider safety, environmental harm, legal restrictions, data accuracy, provider failure, non-payment, liability and reputational risk.
9. **What is the smallest reversible test?** Seek evidence before buying equipment, building software, hiring staff or making promises.
10. **What evidence would make us stop or change direction?** Define failure conditions as well as success conditions.

### 5.5 Boundaries and safeguards

- Do not enter land, facilities or restricted areas without permission.
- Do not collect personal, commercially confidential or sensitive location data without a lawful basis and appropriate consent.
- Do not conduct soil, water, air, engineering, ecological or health assessments beyond demonstrated competence and required authorization.
- Do not handle hazardous waste, chemicals, contaminated materials, wildlife or dangerous equipment as an informal experiment.
- Do not broker regulated services or make environmental, carbon, restoration, compliance or certification claims without the necessary authority and evidence.
- Do not promise a customer that a third-party provider will deliver unless responsibilities, terms and recourse are clear.
- Avoid paying upfront for speculative access, equipment, certification, memberships or training before verifying the provider, the requirement and the likely customer demand.
- Do not confuse a free tool with a free service: fieldwork, transport, data, connectivity, verification and time can still have costs.

## 6. How this access lens applies across all environmental territories

For every territory and every deeper taxonomy branch, record two parallel views:

**A. The full opportunity landscape:** what the field contains, how it works, its value chains, technologies, customers, institutions, infrastructure, capital requirements and possible large-scale opportunities.

**B. The accessible entry landscape:** the smallest credible ways a person with limited money, network and equipment might learn, contribute, earn, build trust, obtain access or test demand using resources actually available.

Do not let view B shrink view A. Do not let view A make view B unrealistic. The purpose is to discover practical entry points without losing sight of the full economic system.

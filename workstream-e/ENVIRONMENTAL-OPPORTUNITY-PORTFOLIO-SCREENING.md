# Environmental Opportunity Portfolio — Cross-Territory Screening

**Status:** First-pass portfolio screening framework and candidate longlist, 2026-11-06. This is a structured discovery aid, not a ranking of proven businesses, investment advice, or a decision to pursue any candidate. No candidate has yet passed customer, technical, environmental, legal, or economic validation.

**Parent:** [Environmental Opportunity Intelligence](ENVIRONMENTAL-OPPORTUNITY-INTELLIGENCE.md)

## 1. Why screen across the whole landscape?

The initial horizontal survey has mapped the major territories from 3.1 to 3.15. The next task is to compare potential vertical branches across the whole landscape before choosing where to descend. The first territory mapped must not receive automatic priority simply because it came first.

The screening process should identify:
- important environmental and operational problems;
- real users, operators, customers, rights-holders, and budget-holders;
- resource, process, technology, infrastructure, data, and market dependencies;
- points where value may be created or captured;
- evidence that can be accessed and checked;
- realistic participation routes using available capabilities and legitimate existing providers;
- unresolved constraints that could make an apparently attractive opportunity impractical.

The screen preserves two parallel views: **the complete opportunity landscape**, including capital-intensive projects, and **accessible participation routes**, including research, data, coordination, field support, distribution, and specialist-provider support. Neither view is allowed to erase the other.

## 2. Screening dimensions

Use the following dimensions when evidence becomes available. Do not fill a score merely to create a ranking.

| Dimension | Core question | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental significance | Does the activity address a material environmental pressure, resource constraint, or improvement opportunity? | Baselines, affected systems, scale, severity, measurable outcomes |
| Real problem and user | Who experiences the problem, and who has reason and authority to act? | Interviews, operating records, procurement, complaints, service gaps |
| Economic value | Is there a cost, loss, risk, revenue, or obligation that creates a reason to pay? | Cost of current practice, avoided loss, budgets, transaction evidence |
| Demand observability | Can potential users, buyers, contracts, or transactions be identified? | Named user groups, tenders, supplier activity, purchase patterns |
| Evidence accessibility | Can the key facts be investigated without privileged access or disproportionate cost? | Public records, open datasets, permitted site observation, accessible experts |
| Entry accessibility | Is there a credible first contribution without owning major assets? | Existing providers, paid tasks, partner access, digital/field skills, equipment needs |
| Capability leverage | Can existing infrastructure, laboratories, operators, platforms, or qualified providers be used legitimately? | Provider availability, permission, pricing, terms, capacity, reliability |
| Technical feasibility | Are the process, performance, and scale assumptions credible? | Independent tests, operating references, specifications, process limits |
| Capital and working capital | What must be financed upfront and during operation? | Equipment, inventory, deposits, logistics, receivables, maintenance |
| Legal and institutional burden | What rights, permits, standards, qualifications, and liabilities apply? | Applicable laws, licences, approvals, professional duties, enforcement |
| Environmental and social safeguards | Could the proposed activity shift harm or create unsafe outcomes? | Lifecycle impacts, exposure, community rights, waste/residuals, safeguards |
| Dependencies and timing | What must be in place first, and what seasonal or infrastructure constraints apply? | Input supply, grid/water/roads, policy, seasonality, customer workflows |

### Scoring discipline

When evidence is sufficient, each dimension may be rated 1–5 with a written evidence note and confidence level. A low score is not automatically a reason to discard a large or strategically important opportunity; it may indicate a dependency or barrier to understand. An unknown is **unknown**, not a neutral score. Do not combine dimensions into one weighted total until the user has reviewed the weights and the evidence supports comparison.

Suggested evidence confidence labels:
- **E0 — Unexamined:** no substantive evidence gathered.
- **E1 — Indicative:** plausible signal or secondary claim, not independently checked.
- **E2 — Corroborated:** multiple credible sources or one strong primary source, with limitations recorded.
- **E3 — Operationally evidenced:** direct observation, documented transactions, operating data, or a reproducible test supports the relevant claim.
- **E4 — Validated for a defined use:** customer, technical, legal, environmental, and economic evidence is adequate for the specific decision being considered.

Confidence applies to individual claims, not to a whole opportunity in the abstract.

## 4. Initial comparative interpretation — not a ranking

At this stage, the longlist suggests several recurring opportunity structures:

1. **Information and intelligence layer:** ENV-C05, C11, C12, and parts of C03/C06/C10/C14. The key test is whether information changes a decision and whether a user will pay for the result rather than merely appreciate a dashboard.
2. **Coordination and market-connection layer:** ENV-C01, C02, C07, C08, and C15. The key test is whether fragmentation, reliability, trust, quality, or transaction costs create a persistent problem that an intermediary can solve.
3. **Operations and performance layer:** ENV-C04 and C09. The key test is access to operational data, qualified technical partners, measurable savings, and authority to intervene.
4. **Physical transformation and treatment layer:** ENV-C02, C13, and C14. The key test is process performance, input consistency, residual management, capital, permits, and verified buyers or customers.
5. **Ecological and public-interest outcome layer:** ENV-C10 and parts of C12/C14. The key test is who funds the work, what outcomes can be measured, and how rights, safeguards, and long-term stewardship are secured.

These patterns are not mutually exclusive. A single service could combine intelligence, coordination, and operations. Do not assume that a digital product is automatically easier to sell or that an intermediary is automatically low-capital.

## 5. First-pass status of all candidates

All candidates begin at **E0 — Unexamined** unless a later evidence record explicitly changes the status of a particular claim. Their presence in this longlist means only that they merit structured investigation. No comparative score or priority rank is assigned yet.

The first screening pass should gather enough evidence to answer:
- Is the problem real and material in a defined setting?
- Who experiences it and who pays or has budget authority?
- What is currently done, by whom, and at what cost or failure rate?
- What evidence can be accessed and independently checked?
- What permissions, qualifications, infrastructure, and liabilities apply?
- What is the smallest legitimate test that can produce decision-useful evidence?

## 6. How this longlist should be used

- Do not explore all 15 candidates vertically at once.
- Do not automatically select the lowest-capital-looking candidate.
- Do not treat a large global market estimate as evidence of local demand.
- Do not infer customer willingness to pay from environmental importance.
- Do not equate an available dataset with a sellable intelligence product.
- Do not equate a technology demonstration with a reliable commercial process.
- Keep candidates open to merging, splitting, pausing, or rejecting as evidence develops.
- Surface any credible real-world opportunity immediately; the user decides whether to pause the portfolio screen and investigate it.

# Workstream E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application

**Status:** Proposed and approved as the working direction; detailed operating system not yet implemented or validated.

## Purpose

Translate knowledge, measurements, monitored developments, and investigated evidence into opportunities to create or capture economic value. Workstream E evaluates potential trading opportunities, investments, businesses, and commercial intelligence products or services.

Its central question is:

> Given what we understand about the world and what we can verify, where might there be an opportunity to create or capture economic value—and what evidence would justify acting on it?

Workstream E is an application and decision-support workstream, not a promise that research will produce profits. A plausible idea is not a validated opportunity, and a validated opportunity can still fail.

## Opportunity domains

### E1 — Trading opportunities

Investigate whether a market price, probability, spread, or other observable market condition may be inconsistent with a well-supported view of the underlying situation. Develop explicit hypotheses, time horizons, conditions for entry and exit, costs, downside scenarios, and invalidation criteria.

Potential areas may include foreign exchange, rates and bonds, commodities, other financial markets, and prediction markets where lawful and appropriate. These are possible areas of investigation, not a commitment to trade any particular asset.

### E2 — Investment opportunities

Evaluate assets, businesses, sectors, and structural themes that may benefit from economic or institutional changes. Consider valuation, expected cash flows, financing, competitive position, liquidity, downside risk, time horizon, and what evidence would weaken the thesis.

### E3 — Business opportunities

Investigate unmet needs, inefficiencies, dependencies, changing demand, and problems that customers may be willing to pay to solve. Assess customer access, alternatives, competition, delivery requirements, costs, pricing, and practical feasibility.

### E4 — Intelligence products and services

Explore whether research and monitoring capabilities can become useful paid outputs, such as sector intelligence, regulatory or supply-risk monitoring, market-change alerts, due-diligence research, or data products. These are candidate directions, not commitments to build each product.

## Relationship with Workstreams A–D

- **A — Deep Conceptual Foundations:** explains what a phenomenon means in reality and how its mechanisms may work.
- **B — Measurement:** establishes what can be measured, how, and with what limitations.
- **C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** tracks economic developments and assesses evidence and possible implications.
- **D — Footprint Intelligence:** investigates observable traces, entities, relationships, dependencies, and changes across relevant domains.
- **E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application:** uses relevant findings from A–D, together with market and customer evidence, to identify, test, compare, and evaluate possible economic actions.

The relationship is iterative, not a compulsory one-way pipeline. E may identify missing concepts for A, measurement needs for B, monitoring requirements for C, or trace-investigation questions for D. Not every finding from A–D needs to produce an opportunity, and not every E investigation requires all four other workstreams.

Workstream E does not replace or redefine the six core economic indicators, the model's four analytical layers, or the responsibilities of A–D. It is an additional application workstream across the project.

## Opportunity lifecycle

Use the following default process, adapting it to the type and scale of opportunity:

1. **Observation:** Record a development, unmet need, market condition, anomaly, or question worth investigating.
2. **Explanation:** Describe why it might matter, separating observations from interpretations and assumptions.
3. **Opportunity hypothesis:** State who might benefit, what action might be possible, and how value could be created or captured.
4. **Evidence and verification:** Gather relevant evidence, assess source quality, seek corroboration, examine alternatives, and identify what could disprove the hypothesis.
5. **Economic evaluation:** Estimate benefits, costs, required resources or capital, time horizon, competition, uncertainty, downside, and opportunity cost.
6. **Low-cost test:** Choose a suitable test before making a substantial commitment.
7. **Decision:** Proceed, revise, monitor, defer, or reject, recording the reasons and decision conditions.
8. **Outcome review:** Compare actual results with the original hypothesis and assumptions. Record failures as well as successes and feed lessons back into the appropriate workstreams.

A candidate may be rejected at any stage. Do not force every observation through to action.

## Validation methods

Choose validation methods that match the opportunity:

- **Trading:** historical testing with attention to data quality, look-ahead and survivorship bias, transaction costs, liquidity, risk, and out-of-sample performance; paper trading before considering real capital where appropriate.
- **Investing:** thesis review, valuation scenarios, downside analysis, sensitivity analysis, and monitoring of thesis-invalidating evidence.
- **Business:** customer conversations, competitor and substitute research, a small sample deliverable, pre-sales or a limited pilot where appropriate, and evidence of willingness to pay.
- **Intelligence services:** test whether the information is accurate, timely, decision-relevant, difficult enough to obtain, and valuable to a specific customer.

A backtest, interview, announcement, or small pilot is evidence of a particular kind and strength; none guarantees future profitability or broad market demand.

## Prioritization

Rank opportunities using a documented, context-appropriate assessment of:

- Strength and independence of supporting evidence
- Clarity of the mechanism and value proposition
- Potential value or return relative to costs
- Required capital, time, skills, and access
- Downside risk and reversibility
- Time to learn whether the hypothesis is valid
- Feasibility with current resources
- Competition, alternatives, and ability to capture value
- Confidence, uncertainty, and conditions that would invalidate the case

Do not create arbitrary numerical scores or thresholds before there is a reasoned, documented basis for them. Where useful, compare opportunities qualitatively first and make assumptions explicit.

## Initial operating posture

The long-term design is broad: discover and evaluate opportunities across trading, investing, business creation, and intelligence products or services.

The practical starting posture is to explore more than one domain while prioritizing affordable experiments that can produce useful evidence. Given limited starting capital, do not make personal trading profits the first success criterion. Trading research may begin with historical analysis and simulated or paper trading; business research may begin with a narrowly defined customer problem and a small sample service. These are prudent initial options, not permanent restrictions.

Do not build complicated software, automated trading systems, dashboards, or integrations merely because they are technically possible. First establish a real decision or customer need, test whether the proposed method helps, and identify where manual work becomes a meaningful bottleneck.

## Reality-first and evidence rules

- Distinguish an observation, interpretation, hypothesis, validated finding, decision, and realized outcome.
- An economic trend is not automatically a profitable trade or business.
- A market price move does not by itself identify its cause or prove mispricing.
- A company announcement or physical footprint does not by itself establish commercial success.
- A customer's interest does not by itself prove willingness to pay or sustainable demand.
- Record source provenance, dates, assumptions, limitations, contradictory evidence, and uncertainty.
- Consider legal, ethical, privacy, regulatory, and operational constraints.
- Evaluate potential loss and downside alongside potential benefit; never imply guaranteed returns.
- Record why an opportunity was pursued, deferred, or rejected, and compare results with expectations.

## Initial scope and next steps

Workstream E is established as a working direction, but its full process and any individual opportunity strategy remain unimplemented and unvalidated.

The initial sequence is:

1. Preserve this mandate and its boundaries in the project record.
2. Continue the existing Workstream A investigation of production; do not interrupt it to finish the GDP measurement draft.
3. When ready for practical exploration, identify a small set of candidate opportunities and state the evidence needed to evaluate them.
4. Select a low-cost test based on evidence, feasibility, and learning value—not excitement alone.
5. Record the result and refine the process before adding automation.

No particular trade, investment, business, or intelligence product is approved by this charter. Opportunity selection remains a separate evidence-based decision.

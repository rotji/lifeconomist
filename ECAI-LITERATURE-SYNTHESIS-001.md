# ECAI Foundational Literature Synthesis 001

**Status:** Initial source-backed synthesis; not a systematic review or exhaustive bibliography.  
**Prepared:** 2026-10-29  
**Parent document:** [ECAI-FOUNDATION.md](ECAI-FOUNDATION.md)  
**Research stage:** Stage 1 — Foundational literature synthesis

## 1. Purpose and limits

This document records an initial synthesis of established research relevant to Economic Consequence and Agency Intelligence (ECAI). It distinguishes source-supported findings from proposed implications for ECAI. The source set is selective and was gathered through targeted searches; it is not a preregistered systematic review, and it does not establish that ECAI's integrated framework is itself validated.

The initial research question is:

> What can established research tell us about tracing economic consequences, understanding collective economic agency, and evaluating whether action produces meaningful outcomes?

## 2. Summary of findings

1. **Consumer detriment can be measured, but measurement depends on scope and method.** The OECD's 2020 feasibility study reviews methodologies for estimating consumer detriment and the impact of consumer policy. It is a useful basis for examining harms and benefits to consumers, but it does not by itself provide a complete account of consequences across suppliers, workers, owners, public finance, communities, or the environment.
2. **Political consumerism is a documented form of participation, not proof of institutional influence.** Copeland and Boulianne's 2020 meta-analysis reviews 66 studies and more than 1,000 tests of factors associated with political consumerism. It finds support for associations with lifestyle-politics variables as well as education, political interest, and organizational membership. The authors note limitations in differentiating boycott from buycott, comparing national contexts, and standardizing measures. The study mainly explains who participates; it does not establish a universal causal effect of participation on corporate or policy outcomes.
3. **Boycotts can affect market valuation in some settings, but this is a specific outcome measure.** Tomlin's 2019 study analyzes 125 US boycotts from 1978 to 2017 and reports an average negative, statistically significant effect on target firms' shareholder wealth, with synthetic-control and placebo analyses used for robustness. The finding supports the possibility of market effects in the study's setting. Shareholder-wealth effects are not equivalent to proof that the targeted organization changed its conduct, that public policy changed, or that net social welfare improved.
4. **Boycotting and buycotting should not be treated as interchangeable.** Neilson's 2010 study of 21,535 adults in the 2002/2003 European Social Survey found differences in predictors of boycotting and buycotting. Kam and Deichert's three survey experiments reported that negative information was more powerful in inducing boycotting than positive information was in inducing buycotting. These findings justify measuring the two behaviours separately; they do not imply the same pattern in every population or context.
5. **Collective action is conditional, not impossible or automatic.** Ostrom's research challenges the claim that self-interested individuals inevitably fail to cooperate. Institutional rules, trust, reciprocity, monitoring, participation, conflict resolution, and context can matter. Design principles derived from common-pool resource institutions are useful hypotheses for examining collective economic action, but should not be copied as a universal blueprint for consumer campaigns or national-level policy influence.
6. **Supply-chain and input-output analysis help trace indirect effects but aggregate models have limits.** OECD material on inter-country input-output tables notes that sector-level aggregation can conceal firm- and product-level differences and that proportionality assumptions may not match specific firms' sourcing decisions. ECAI must therefore state the resolution of its evidence and avoid treating economy-wide averages as verified traces of a particular transaction.
7. **Evaluation must distinguish objectives, outputs, outcomes, and broader impacts.** OECD guidance emphasizes clear objectives, differential results across groups, causal contribution, and intended as well as unintended effects. Its criteria are evaluative questions, not a single causal-identification method. The method must fit the claim and available evidence.

## 3. Literature areas and ECAI implications

### 3.1 Consumer welfare and consumer detriment

**Source:** OECD (2020), *Measuring consumer detriment and the impact of consumer policy: Feasibility study*. https://doi.org/10.1787/0c2e643b-en

The OECD paper considers methods for estimating consumer detriment and assessing consumer-policy impacts, with the aim of strengthening evidence-based consumer policy.

**Supported conclusion:** Consumer harms and the effects of consumer policy are measurable research questions, but estimates require defined scopes and methods.

**Implication for ECAI:** For a purchase-level comparison, first establish the consumer's actual objective and constraints, then assess price, quality, time, risk, access, and other relevant consequences. Do not claim that a choice is socially better solely because it improves one consumer-welfare dimension. Broader effects need additional evidence.

**Limit:** The paper is a feasibility study focused on consumer detriment and consumer policy, not a complete method for tracing all societal consequences of spending.

### 3.2 Political consumerism: boycott and buycott

**Sources:**
- Copeland, L. and Boulianne, S. (2020), *Political consumerism: A meta-analysis*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0192512120905048
- Neilson, L. A. (2010), *Boycott or buycott? Understanding political consumerism*. https://doi.org/10.1002/cb.313
- Kam, C. D. and Deichert, M. (2020), *Boycotting, Buycotting, and the Psychology of Political Consumerism*. https://doi.org/10.1086/705922

Copeland and Boulianne synthesize 66 studies and over 1,000 tests on predictors of political consumerism. Their analysis reports associations with political distrust, liberal ideology, media use, education, political interest, and organizational membership, while also discussing limitations in cross-national comparisons and inconsistent operationalization. Neilson's analysis of 21,535 adults distinguishes boycotters and buycotters and reports different associations with individual characteristics. Kam and Deichert's three survey experiments find negative information more powerful than positive information for inducing boycott rather than buycott intentions/behaviour in their experimental settings.

**Supported conclusions:**
- Deliberate purchasing and avoidance are recognized forms of political participation.
- Boycotting and buycotting are conceptually distinct and should be measured separately.
- Participation predictors vary by how behaviour is defined, the population, and the research design.
- Participation research alone cannot demonstrate that campaigns change the target's behaviour or produce net benefits.

**Implication for ECAI:** Record the stated objective, target behaviour, participation, substitution to alternatives, and final outcome separately. A campaign that reduces purchases of a target but produces no durable institutional response is different from one that changes sourcing, governance, or policy.

**Limit:** The cited meta-analysis primarily studies predictors of participation; it does not supply a universal estimate of campaign effectiveness. Survey experiments about information and behaviour should not be treated as direct estimates of real-world campaign outcomes.

### 3.3 Market effects of boycotts

**Source:** Tomlin, K. M. (2019), *Assessing the Efficacy of Consumer Boycotts of U.S. Target Firms: A Shareholder Wealth Analysis*. https://doi.org/10.1002/soej.12389

Tomlin examines 125 US boycotts from 1978 to 2017. The paper reports that actual boycott calls had a negative and statistically significant average effect on target firms' shareholder wealth; synthetic-control and placebo tests are reported as robustness checks. It also reports variation by firm characteristics and boycott categories.

**Supported conclusion:** In the examined sample and outcome, boycott announcements were associated with a robust negative market-valuation effect on average.

**Implication for ECAI:** This is evidence for one link in a possible causal chain—campaign announcement to market valuation—not proof of the entire chain from consumer action to changed corporate conduct or social benefit. For each case, ECAI should separately assess:
- changes in consumer demand or sales;
- financial or reputational consequences;
- the target's response;
- the specific conduct or policy outcome;
- effects on workers, suppliers, consumers, and other parties;
- persistence after attention declines.

**Limit:** The sample covers US cases and a shareholder-wealth outcome. It should not be generalized without qualification to all countries, all targets, all campaign forms, or public policy outcomes.

### 3.4 Collective action and institutional design

**Sources:**
- Ostrom, E. (2000), *Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms*. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.14.3.137
- Ostrom, E. (1998), *A Behavioral Approach to the Rational Choice Theory of Collective Action*. https://doi.org/10.2307/2585925
- Ostrom Workshop, *Ostrom Design Principles*: https://www.ostrom.indiana.edu/courses-teaching/teaching-tools/ostrom-design/index.html

Ostrom's work uses theoretical development and empirical cases to explain why cooperation can emerge in social dilemmas. Factors include the type of resource and group, rules for contributions and benefits, trust, reciprocity, monitoring, conflict-resolution mechanisms, and participants' ability to shape rules. The well-known design principles were developed from common-pool resource governance cases.

**Supported conclusion:** Collective cooperation is context-dependent. It is not adequately explained by assuming that people always act as isolated, narrowly self-interested individuals, nor by assuming that shared interests automatically produce cooperation.

**Implication for ECAI:** When investigating a collective economic initiative, examine:
- clarity of the shared objective and participant boundaries;
- legitimacy and inclusiveness of decision-making;
- distribution of costs, benefits, and burdens;
- transparency and monitoring;
- mechanisms for disputes and accountability;
- free-riding and participation costs;
- adaptation when conditions change.

**Limit:** Principles developed for long-lived common-pool resource institutions are not a plug-in design for every consumer campaign, corporation, or government-policy initiative. Transfer requires explicit argument and case evidence.

### 3.5 Tracing economic transmission through production networks

**Source:** OECD, *Trends in Global Value Chains*, section on interpreting input-output-based GVC indicators and their caveats. https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-global-value-chains_8217c2ff-en/full-report/component-9.html

Input-output and inter-country input-output tables can describe production linkages between sectors and countries. OECD notes that these tables are aggregated and may hide firm-level, product-level, or technology-specific differences. They also rely on assumptions, including proportional allocation of inputs, that may not reflect a particular firm's sourcing behaviour.

**Supported conclusion:** Aggregate network methods are useful for system-level analysis but do not reveal every transaction-level or firm-specific relationship.

**Implication for ECAI:** Match the strength of the claim to the resolution of the evidence:
- Use transaction or firm-specific evidence for a claim about a particular business where available.
- Use sector-level input-output analysis for an explicitly aggregate estimate.
- Mark inferred relationships and proportional allocations as estimates.
- Avoid claiming that a particular purchase supports a specific supplier, job, owner, or policy unless the relevant link is evidenced.

**Limit:** An input-output model is not a direct observation of where every unit of money went. Its results depend on data quality, aggregation, assumptions, and the time period.

### 3.6 Evaluation and causal inference

**Sources:**
- OECD (2021), *Applying Evaluation Criteria Thoughtfully*. https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/applying-evaluation-criteria-thoughtfully_543e84ed-en/full-report/understanding-the-six-criteria-definitions-elements-of-analysis-and-key-challenges_2843ff7d.html
- OECD, *Evaluation Criteria*. https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/development-co-operation-evaluation-and-effectiveness/evaluation-criteria.html

OECD guidance distinguishes effectiveness from broader impact and encourages assessment of intended and unintended effects, differential outcomes, contextual factors, and the extent to which an intervention caused or contributed to change. The six DAC criteria—relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, and sustainability—are questions for evaluative judgement, not a single prescribed evaluation design.

**Supported conclusion:** An intervention's stated objective, implementation, immediate outputs, outcomes, broader impacts, and durability should not be collapsed into one claim of success.

**Implication for ECAI:** Before assessing an action, define:
1. the objective and target outcome;
2. the results chain and proposed mechanism;
3. the baseline and observation period;
4. the affected population and relevant comparison;
5. intended and unintended outcomes;
6. plausible rival explanations;
7. distribution of effects across groups;
8. durability and cost.

Use an evaluation design proportionate to the claim. Where causal identification is weak, report contribution, association, or plausibility rather than certainty.

**Limit:** Evaluation criteria help structure questions but do not by themselves solve attribution or establish a counterfactual.

## 4. Cross-literature synthesis

The sources support several methodological conclusions relevant to ECAI.

### 4.1 Separate participation, transmission, response, and impact

A defensible analysis distinguishes:
- **Input/action:** what participants did or resources they committed.
- **Reach/participation:** who participated and at what scale.
- **Immediate effect:** a change in purchases, sales, prices, finance, or public attention.
- **Institutional response:** what the target or intermediary changed.
- **Outcome/impact:** the resulting economic, social, environmental, or governance change.
- **Durability and distribution:** how long the effect lasted and who gained or lost.

These stages are related but not interchangeable. Evidence of one does not automatically establish the next.

### 4.2 Measure boycott and buycott separately

Avoid treating withdrawal of support and affirmative support for an alternative as the same action. Track both target-side changes and where redirected demand actually goes. A boycott without a feasible alternative can have different implications from a buycott that builds a substitute supplier or institution.

### 4.3 Distinguish valuation from operating and social outcomes

A stock-price reaction is a market outcome. It can be relevant, but it is not a substitute for observing company conduct, worker conditions, consumer welfare, public policy, or environmental effects. The same principle applies to reputational metrics and public statements.

### 4.4 Collective influence depends on conditions

The number of participants is only one variable. Relevant factors may include the target's dependence on the affected market, substitution possibilities, campaign duration, coordination, demands, target responsiveness, resource distribution, and whether participants can sustain action. These are candidate explanatory factors, not a universal predictive formula.

### 4.5 Trace indirect effects at the correct level

A transaction can be part of a wider network, but the available data may only support sector- or country-level estimates. ECAI should label the level of inference and avoid presenting aggregate modelling as a verified trace of a particular payment.

### 4.6 Evaluate unintended and distributional consequences

Actions can shift burdens to workers, suppliers, small businesses, households, or other stakeholders rather than eliminate them. A credible assessment should ask not only whether the target was pressured but also who bore the cost and whether the intended outcome improved.

## 5. Preliminary disagreements and unresolved questions

The initial source set does not justify a single universal theory of economic agency. Important open questions include:

- Which types of economic action most often produce durable changes in target behaviour, under which conditions?
- How do boycotts and buycotts compare when evaluated on the same final outcomes rather than different measures?
- How well do findings from US and European studies transfer to Nigeria and other contexts with different income constraints, market concentration, informality, and institutional conditions?
- What combination of transaction-level evidence, firm disclosures, sector statistics, and qualitative process tracing is sufficient for different kinds of claims?
- How should ECAI compare financial, social, environmental, and governance consequences without hiding value choices in a composite score?
- Which forms of collective governance maintain legitimacy, protect dissent, and distribute costs fairly?
- When is individual switching more feasible than collective action, and when are institutional or regulatory alternatives necessary?
- What evidence distinguishes a target's substantive response from symbolic statements or temporary changes?
- Which methods can evaluate outcomes when a credible control group or counterfactual is unavailable?

These should be answered through additional source review and case comparison rather than assumptions.

## 7. Research decisions from this synthesis

1. Preserve ECAI as a provisional cross-cutting framework; do not claim a new discipline has been established.
2. Treat consumer choice as one channel among several, not the whole framework.
3. Keep boycott and buycott analytically separate.
4. Keep market valuation, operational change, policy change, and social impact separate.
5. Require a documented transmission mechanism before claiming that an action can influence a target.
6. Use input-output models for aggregate analysis only at the level their assumptions support.
7. Evaluate unintended effects, distributional outcomes, and durability alongside intended results.
8. Use existing evidence and documented cases first; do not require the project owner to run personal experiments as a prerequisite.
9. Treat Nigerian applicability as an empirical question, not assume international findings transfer unchanged.

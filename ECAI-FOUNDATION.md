# Economic Consequence and Agency Intelligence (ECAI)

**Status:** Provisional research framework. Foundational synthesis is underway; the framework has not been validated as a unified method, product, or intervention system.  
**Established:** 2026-10-29  
**Relationship to Lifeconomist:** Cross-cutting research application using the existing Workstreams A–E and Reality-First methodology; not a new workstream.

## 1. Purpose

Economic Consequence and Agency Intelligence (ECAI) is a proposed framework for understanding the consequences of economic participation and identifying practical, evidence-based ways for people to influence those consequences.

It connects two questions:

1. **Economic consequence:** What happens because people spend, save, borrow, invest, work, produce, and trade in particular ways?
2. **Economic agency:** What can individuals and groups realistically do to influence those outcomes, and what evidence shows whether their actions work?

The framework should help investigate existing economic behaviour, trace relevant flows and relationships, compare feasible alternatives, identify plausible points of influence, and evaluate actual outcomes—including costs, trade-offs, uncertainty, and unintended consequences.

ECAI is a working research concept. Its name and boundaries may be revised if established terminology or evidence supports a better formulation.

## 2. Governing proposition

Understanding consequences without identifying possible actions may produce information with limited practical use. Encouraging action without understanding consequences may produce ineffective campaigns, misleading claims, or avoidable harm.

ECAI therefore proposes an evidence-to-action cycle:

**Economic behaviour → transmission through connected systems → consequences → comparison and leverage analysis → individual or collective action → measured outcomes → revised understanding.**

This is an analytical model, not a claim that every link is observable, linear, or causal. Feedback, delays, substitution, external shocks, institutional constraints, and interactions between mechanisms may alter outcomes. Each material link must be investigated on its own evidence.

## 3. Proposed operating model

### 3.1 Observe economic behaviour

Identify the behaviour relevant to the question: consumption, saving, borrowing, investment, work, production, trade, ownership, or participation in an institution. Distinguish observed behaviour from stated preferences, intentions, or assumed motives.

### 3.2 Trace economic transmission

Investigate how the behaviour may affect retailers, suppliers, workers, owners, lenders, investors, payment systems, governments, communities, and other relevant actors. Identify the actual roles, layers, relationships, flows, dependencies, timing, and constraints. Do not assume the first recipient captures the entire benefit or that money flows directly to a decision-maker.

### 3.3 Identify consequences

Consider relevant financial, economic, social, institutional, and environmental outcomes. Examples may include household costs, prices, profits, employment, wages, supplier income, public revenue, access, resilience, emissions, and decision-making power. The relevant outcome set depends on the question; no single metric captures every dimension.

### 3.4 Compare alternatives

Compare realistic choices and the trade-offs associated with them. Present costs, benefits, risks, affected groups, feasibility, and uncertainty. Avoid simplistic good/bad labels and do not hide incompatible dimensions inside an arbitrary universal score. Make value judgements explicit when a recommendation depends on a chosen objective.

### 3.5 Identify plausible leverage

Ask whether an individual or collective change could plausibly affect the desired outcome, through which mechanism, at what scale and time horizon, and under what conditions. Consider substitution, market structure, available alternatives, institutional responsiveness, coordination costs, free-riding, unequal resources, and the possibility that no meaningful leverage exists.

### 3.6 Evaluate action and outcomes

Where an action has occurred, compare outcomes with an appropriate baseline or counterfactual where possible. Separate participation and intermediate effects from final outcomes. Examine alternative explanations, unintended effects, distributional consequences, durability, and whether the observed change can reasonably be attributed to the action.

## 4. Two connected analytical layers

### Consequence intelligence

**Question:** What happens under current behaviour, and what evidence supports that explanation?

Typical work includes:
- Describe the behaviour and its context.
- Map relevant actors and distinguish owner, operator, funder, intermediary, decision-maker, beneficiary, and cost- or risk-bearing parties.
- Trace money, goods, services, information, authority, obligations, and other relevant flows.
- Identify direct effects, indirect effects, feedback, delays, and possible externalities.
- Record the evidence, definitions, source provenance, limitations, missing data, and uncertainty.
- Separate observations from estimates, interpretations, hypotheses, and causal claims.

### Agency intelligence

**Question:** What can people realistically change, by what mechanism, and with what likely consequences?

Possible mechanisms to investigate include:
- Redirecting purchases or supporting alternative suppliers.
- Coordinating purchasing or bargaining.
- Using shareholder rights and investment allocation.
- Supporting cooperatives or other alternative institutions.
- Changing personal saving, financing, or participation choices where feasible.
- Organizing lawful, transparent public advocacy tied to an economic or policy objective.
- Improving access to verified information and monitoring institutional responses.

These are candidate mechanisms, not endorsements or claims of proven effectiveness in every context. Market transactions must not be assumed to force a policy response. Research into financial-market actions must distinguish ordinary investment decisions from deceptive, coercive, or manipulative conduct and account for financial risk and potential harm to third parties.

## 5. Core research questions

1. What economic behaviour is actually occurring?
2. Where does the associated money, resource, obligation, or influence travel?
3. Which entities benefit, bear costs, or control relevant decisions?
4. What direct and indirect consequences can be established?
5. Which alternatives are feasible for people with different resources and constraints?
6. What mechanism could connect a proposed action to a desired outcome?
7. What scale, participation, time horizon, and conditions might be required?
8. What substitutes, counter-responses, or external factors could weaken the mechanism?
9. Who might be harmed, excluded, or made worse off?
10. What evidence would demonstrate that the action worked, failed, or had mixed effects?
11. How robust are the findings across places, institutions, income groups, and time periods?
12. What remains unknown, unmeasured, or dependent on value judgements?

## 6. Research foundations to reuse

ECAI should build on established fields rather than present their established ideas as new. Initial foundations include:

- **Consumer economics and consumer welfare:** purchasing behaviour, information asymmetry, consumer detriment, affordability, and policy effects.
- **Political consumerism:** deliberate purchasing and non-purchasing for political, ethical, social, or environmental objectives.
- **Collective action and institutional cooperation:** coordination, free-riding, trust, governance, and the conditions for durable cooperation.
- **Economic networks and supply-chain analysis:** direct and indirect transmission through production, suppliers, households, firms, finance, and governments.
- **Economic democracy and alternative institutions:** cooperatives, participation, ownership, and decision-making arrangements.
- **Impact evaluation and causal inference:** baselines, counterfactuals, causal mechanisms, unintended effects, distributional outcomes, and uncertainty.

This list is an initial research map, not a completed literature review. The next research stage should verify primary sources, clarify definitions, compare methods, and document where the fields agree, differ, or leave important gaps.

## 7. Evidence and inference rules

Apply the shared rules in [RESEARCH-METHODOLOGY.md](RESEARCH-METHODOLOGY.md) and [FOOTPRINT-INTELLIGENCE-ARCHITECTURE.md](FOOTPRINT-INTELLIGENCE-ARCHITECTURE.md). In particular:

- A trace, correlation, co-occurrence, incentive, or temporal sequence is not by itself proof of causation, intent, ownership, coordination, or illegality.
- Evidence that an action changed a market price or a firm's valuation does not alone prove a policy change or beneficial social outcome.
- A local purchase does not automatically prove local ownership, local sourcing, job creation, or fair treatment.
- Participation, awareness, commitments, and stated intentions are not substitutes for measured outcomes.
- Trace each material link in a causal chain; evidence at the endpoints does not establish the intermediate steps.
- Prefer independent corroboration and record conflicting evidence and alternative explanations.
- Distinguish measured values from estimates and assumptions. State the unit, time period, population, scope, and uncertainty when applicable.
- Do not aggregate incompatible financial, social, or environmental dimensions into one score without a defensible method and explicit value choices.
- Consider affordability and distribution: an option that improves one outcome may impose unacceptable costs on households, workers, suppliers, or other groups.
- Treat people as decision-makers with differing objectives and constraints, not as subjects to be manipulated. Keep proposed action voluntary, lawful, transparent, and accountable.

## 8. Comparison framework

When comparing approaches or actions, assess at least:

1. Evidence quality and relevance.
2. Clarity of the causal mechanism.
3. Measurability of the intended outcome.
4. Likely effectiveness and durability.
5. Affordability and accessibility.
6. Feasibility and coordination requirements.
7. Distribution of benefits, costs, and risks.
8. Potential for substitution or institutional counter-response.
9. Unintended consequences and safeguards.
10. Data availability, privacy, and verification requirements.
11. Transferability across jurisdictions and social contexts.
12. Cost of evaluation relative to the decision's importance.

Do not treat a preliminary qualitative judgement as a validated score. If quantitative ranking is later useful, define the scales, evidence rules, weighting choices, sensitivity checks, and missing-data treatment before interpreting results.

## 9. Relationship to Lifeconomist

ECAI is a focused application of the existing Lifeconomist architecture, not a new workstream and not a replacement for the Global Financial System Exploration Map.

- **A — Deep Conceptual Foundations:** clarify economic concepts and mechanisms relevant to behaviour, consequence, and agency.
- **B — Measurement:** define and validate measures for the outcomes under investigation. ECAI does not automatically expand the six foundational economic indicator candidates.
- **C — Evidence-Based Monitoring:** track relevant observations and assess developments without premature classification.
- **D — Footprint Intelligence:** investigate observable traces, entities, relationships, dependencies, flows, and cross-domain transmission.
- **E — Opportunity Intelligence and Economic Application:** consider practical implications where evidence justifies them, without forcing every finding into a commercial opportunity.

The cross-cutting Reality-First Principle, layered investigation, triangulation, uncertainty rules, and bidirectional feedback remain authoritative in the existing project methodology. ECAI should reuse those capabilities and contribute justified questions or findings back to the relevant workstreams.

## 10. Scope and non-goals

At this stage ECAI is:
- A research framework under development.
- Broad enough to examine individual choices, collective action, existing institutions, and alternative institutions.
- Applicable internationally, with Nigerian contexts included where relevant and supported by evidence.
- Open to positive, negative, mixed, null, and uncertain findings.

At this stage ECAI is **not**:
- A validated unified discipline or established standard.
- A consumer rating system, universal ethical score, or prescriptive spending guide.
- A boycott platform or campaign organization.
- A financial trading strategy or a claim that coordinated market transactions can reliably compel governments.
- A software implementation, dashboard, or business model.
- Evidence that every economic choice has a large, traceable, or actionable systemic effect.

## 11. Research programme and stage gates

### Stage 1 — Foundational literature synthesis
Map the established fields, core terminology, leading methods, disagreements, and evidence gaps. Distinguish established findings from ECAI's proposed synthesis.

**Exit condition:** a source-backed synthesis and terminology map that explains what is reused, what is combined, and what remains novel or unproven.

### Stage 2 — Consequence-tracing methodology
Specify how to describe behaviour, map actors and flows, classify consequences, evaluate sources, represent uncertainty, and compare alternatives.

**Exit condition:** a repeatable written method with clear evidence standards and safeguards against unsupported causal claims.

### Stage 3 — Documented case comparison
Examine successful, unsuccessful, mixed, and ambiguous real-world cases from relevant fields. Record objectives, context, mechanism, participation, intermediate effects, final outcomes, affected groups, costs, source quality, and alternative explanations.

**Exit condition:** a comparative evidence register that makes limits and transferability visible.

### Stage 4 — Feasibility and direction assessment
Compare candidate approaches against the framework in Section 8, including Nigerian practical constraints and international evidence where relevant.

**Exit condition:** a reasoned recommendation for the strongest combination or direction, with uncertainties, prerequisites, and explicit reasons for rejecting weaker alternatives.

### Stage 5 — Application decision
Only after the preceding stages should the project decide whether to develop a service, research tool, public resource, practical intervention, or no implementation.

**Exit condition:** a documented decision based on demonstrated need, accessible evidence, feasible delivery, likely value, safeguards, and a credible evaluation plan.

These stages organize research; they do not require the owner to personally experiment with every candidate action. Existing high-quality evidence and documented cases should be used first. New field tests should be proposed only when they resolve an important question that existing evidence cannot answer adequately.

## 12. Initial research status

**Established at project level:** relevant foundational disciplines and existing Lifeconomist methods are available to draw upon.

**Proposed:** ECAI's integrated operating model and its use as a common evidence-to-action framework.

**Not yet established:** that the integrated framework outperforms using the component methods separately; a complete literature review; a validated consequence-tracing protocol; a comparative case register; the strongest practical application; or any product or intervention.
